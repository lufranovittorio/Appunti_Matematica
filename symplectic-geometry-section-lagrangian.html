41.7 Lagrangian submanifolds02V4

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

Lagrangian submanifolds are the most important submanifolds of symplectic manifolds: graphs of closed \(1\)-forms (Corollary 41.4.3), and, as the next example shows, graphs of symplectomorphisms.

02V5 Example 41.7.1

  1. Every curve in a surface is Lagrangian: a line in a \(2\)-dimensional symplectic vector space is Lagrangian.

  2. Graphs of symplectomorphisms. For a diffeomorphism \(\varphi \) of \((M, \omega )\), the graph \(\Gamma _\varphi = \{ (x, \varphi (x))\} \subseteq M \times M\) is Lagrangian for the form \(\mathrm{pr}_2^*\omega - \mathrm{pr}_1^*\omega \) if and only if \(\varphi \) is a symplectomorphism: the parametrisation \(x \mapsto (x, \varphi (x))\) pulls the form back to \(\varphi ^*\omega - \omega \).

  3. The torus \(\{ |z_1| = \cdots = |z_n| = 1\} \subseteq (\mathbb {C}^n, \omega _0)\) is Lagrangian. Its tangent space at \(z\) is spanned by the vectors \(iz_je_j\), with \((e_j)\) the standard basis, and since \(\omega _0(u, v) = \operatorname {Re}\langle u, -iv\rangle \) (Example 41.2.3(3)), \(\omega _0(iz_je_j, iz_ke_k) = \operatorname {Re}\langle iz_je_j, z_ke_k\rangle = \delta _{jk}\operatorname {Re}(i|z_j|^2) = 0\).

02V6 Theorem 41.7.2 Weinstein’s Lagrangian neighbourhood theorem

Let \(L\) be a compact Lagrangian submanifold of a symplectic manifold \((M, \omega )\). Then a neighbourhood of \(L\) in \(M\) is symplectomorphic to a neighbourhood of the zero section in \((T^*L, -d\lambda )\), by a symplectomorphism that is the identity on \(L\) (identified with the zero section).

This is [CdS01] (Lagrangian neighbourhood theorem) or [MS17]. The proof chooses a compatible almost complex structure, uses \(J\) to identify the normal bundle of \(L\) with \(TL\), and the metric with \(T^*L\) (Proposition 41.2.2(4),(5), pointwise), so that a tubular neighbourhood gives a diffeomorphism which is symplectic along \(L\); Moser’s method, relative to \(L\), then corrects it to a symplectomorphism. Consequences:

  1. Nearby Lagrangians. A Lagrangian submanifold \(C^1\)-close to \(L\) corresponds, under the theorem, to a submanifold of \(T^*L\) close to the zero section and transverse to the fibres, hence to the graph of a \(1\)-form, which is closed by Corollary 41.4.3. So the Lagrangians near \(L\) are parametrised by the closed \(1\)-forms on \(L\) near \(0\). The graphs of exact forms \(df\) are the images of the zero section under the time-one maps of the Hamiltonians \(-f \circ \pi \) (Exercise 41.8.4, in a chart).

  2. Symplectomorphisms near the identity. Applied to the diagonal of \(M \times M\), the graph of the identity, which is Lagrangian for \(\mathrm{pr}_2^*\omega - \mathrm{pr}_1^*\omega \) by Example 41.7.1(2), the theorem shows that symplectomorphisms \(C^1\)-close to the identity correspond to closed \(1\)-forms on \(M\) near \(0\); in particular such a symplectomorphism, corresponding to \(\alpha \), is joined to the identity by the path of symplectomorphisms corresponding to \(t\alpha \), \(0 \leq t \leq 1\).

41.7.1 Contact forms and Legendrian submanifolds03EI

Contact geometry is the odd-dimensional sibling of symplectic geometry, and Legendrian submanifolds play the role of Lagrangian ones. It enters here because the space of unit covectors of a Riemannian manifold is a contact manifold, and the unit conormals of submanifolds are Legendrian; for hypersurfaces of \(\mathbb {R}^{n+1}\) these are the normal cycles, which carry the curvature integrals of Chapter 40.

03EJ Definition 41.7.3

A contact form on a manifold \(Y\) of dimension \(2n + 1\) is a \(1\)-form \(\alpha \) such that \(\alpha \wedge (d\alpha )^n\) vanishes nowhere. Its kernel \(\xi = \ker \alpha \), a field of hyperplanes, is the associated contact structure. A submanifold \(L \subseteq Y\) is isotropic if \(\iota ^*\alpha = 0\) for the inclusion \(\iota \colon L \to Y\), that is, if \(T_pL \subseteq \xi _p\) for all \(p \in L\), and Legendrian if moreover \(\dim L = n\).

03EK Lemma 41.7.4

Let \(\alpha \) be a \(1\)-form on a manifold of dimension \(2n + 1\) and \(p\) a point with \(\alpha _p \neq 0\), and let \(\xi _p = \ker \alpha _p\).

  1. \((\alpha \wedge (d\alpha )^n)_p \neq 0\) if and only if the restriction of \((d\alpha )_p\) to \(\xi _p\) is nondegenerate, that is, \((\xi _p, d\alpha _p)\) is a symplectic vector space.

  2. If \(\alpha \) is a contact form, every isotropic submanifold has dimension at most \(n\).

Proof â–¼

(1) Choose \(v\) with \(\alpha _p(v) = 1\) and a basis \(e_1, \ldots , e_{2n}\) of \(\xi _p\); together they form a basis of the tangent space. For a \(2n\)-form \(\beta \), the antiderivation rule (Proposition 12.5.6) gives \(\iota _v(\alpha \wedge \beta ) = \alpha (v)\beta - \alpha \wedge \iota _v\beta \). Evaluated on \(e_1, \ldots , e_{2n}\), the last term vanishes: by the formula for wedge products (Proposition 12.5.4) each of its terms contains a factor \(\alpha (e_i) = 0\). So \((\alpha \wedge \beta )(v, e_1, \ldots , e_{2n}) = \beta (e_1, \ldots , e_{2n})\), and with \(\beta = (d\alpha )^n\), the top form \(\alpha \wedge (d\alpha )^n\) is nonzero at \(p\) if and only if \((d\alpha |_{\xi _p})^n \neq 0\), which by Proposition 41.2.2(1) means that \(d\alpha \) is nondegenerate on \(\xi _p\).

(2) If \(\iota ^*\alpha = 0\), then also \(\iota ^*d\alpha = d\iota ^*\alpha = 0\). So \(T_pL\) is contained in \(\xi _p\) and is an isotropic subspace of the symplectic vector space \((\xi _p, d\alpha _p)\), of dimension \(2n\); by Proposition 41.2.2(3) it has dimension at most \(n\).

So Legendrian submanifolds are the isotropic submanifolds of the largest possible dimension, as Lagrangian submanifolds are in symplectic geometry. A contact structure is as far from integrable as possible. For vector fields \(X\), \(Y\) with values in \(\xi = \ker \alpha \), the invariant formula gives \(d\alpha (X, Y) = -\alpha ([X, Y])\) (Theorem 36.2.12), so \(\xi \) is involutive exactly when \(d\alpha \) vanishes on \(\xi \), and then, by Frobenius’s theorem (Theorem 33.9.8), it is tangent to a family of hypersurfaces. For a contact form \(d\alpha \) is instead nondegenerate on \(\xi \), and the integral submanifolds of \(\xi \) have dimension at most \(n\), half the rank of \(\xi \).

On the cotangent bundle \(T^*M\) of an \(m\)-manifold, the Euler vector field \(Z\) is the velocity of the flow \(\xi \mapsto e^t\xi \) by dilations in the fibres; in coordinates \(Z = \sum _ip_i\, \partial _{p_i}\). Since \(\iota _Z(\sum _idq^i \wedge dp_i) = -\sum _ip_i\, dq^i\), it satisfies

\[ \iota _Z\omega = -\lambda \]

for the canonical forms of Definition 41.4.1. A Riemannian metric \(g\) on \(M\) induces a norm on each \(T^*_qM\), given in coordinates by \(|\xi |^2 = \sum _{i,j}g^{ij}p_ip_j\), a smooth function on \(T^*M\), homogeneous of degree \(2\), so that \(Z(|\xi |^2) = 2|\xi |^2\). The unit cosphere bundle is \(S^*M = \{ \xi \in T^*M : |\xi | = 1\} \).

03EL Proposition 41.7.5

  1. The space of \(1\)-jets. On \(\mathbb {R}^{2n+1}\) with coordinates \((q, p, z) \in \mathbb {R}^n \times \mathbb {R}^n \times \mathbb {R}\), the form \(\alpha = dz - \sum _ip_i\, dq^i\) is a contact form. For a smooth \(f \colon \mathbb {R}^n \to \mathbb {R}\), the \(1\)-jet graph \(j^1f = \{ (q, df_q, f(q)) : q \in \mathbb {R}^n\} \) is a Legendrian submanifold.

  2. Unit cosphere bundles. For a Riemannian manifold \(M\) of dimension \(m\), \(S^*M\) is a hypersurface of \(T^*M\) of dimension \(2m - 1\), and the restriction of the tautological form \(\lambda \) to \(S^*M\) is a contact form.

  3. Unit conormals. For a submanifold \(S \subseteq M\) of dimension less than \(m\), the unit conormal bundle \(N^*_1S = N^*S \cap S^*M\) is a Legendrian submanifold of \(S^*M\).

Proof â–¼

(1) Here \(d\alpha = \sum _idq^i \wedge dp_i\). These \(2\)-forms commute and have square \(0\), so \((d\alpha )^n = n!\, dq^1 \wedge dp_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge dq^n \wedge dp_n\), and then \(dq^i \wedge (d\alpha )^n = 0\) because \(dq^i\) appears twice. Hence \(\alpha \wedge (d\alpha )^n = n!\, dz \wedge dq^1 \wedge dp_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge dq^n \wedge dp_n\), which vanishes nowhere. The jet graph is the image of the embedding \(q \mapsto (q, \partial _1f(q), \ldots , \partial _nf(q), f(q))\), of dimension \(n\), which pulls \(\alpha \) back to \(df - \sum _i\partial _if\, dq^i = 0\).

(2) On \(S^*M\) the differential of \(|\xi |^2\) is nonzero, since \(d(|\xi |^2)(Z) = 2\) there; so \(1\) is a regular value of \(|\xi |^2\) restricted to the complement of the zero section, and \(S^*M\) is a hypersurface (Theorem 33.6.11), to which \(Z\) is nowhere tangent. The form \(\omega ^m\) vanishes nowhere, by Proposition 41.4.2(1) and Proposition 41.2.2(1). Since \(\omega \) has even degree, the antiderivation rule gives

\[ \iota _Z(\omega ^m) = m\, (\iota _Z\omega ) \wedge \omega ^{m-1} = -m\, \lambda \wedge \omega ^{m-1} = (-1)^mm\, \lambda \wedge (d\lambda )^{m-1}. \]

At \(\xi \in S^*M\), let \(v_1, \ldots , v_{2m-1}\) be a basis of \(T_\xi S^*M\). As \(Z_\xi \) is not tangent to \(S^*M\), the vectors \(Z_\xi , v_1, \ldots , v_{2m-1}\) form a basis of \(T_\xi (T^*M)\), so \(\omega ^m(Z_\xi , v_1, \ldots , v_{2m-1}) \neq 0\), and therefore \((\lambda \wedge (d\lambda )^{m-1})(v_1, \ldots , v_{2m-1}) \neq 0\). Pull-backs commute with \(d\) and \(\wedge \), so with \(\alpha \) the restriction of \(\lambda \), \(\alpha \wedge (d\alpha )^{m-1}\) is the restriction of \(\lambda \wedge (d\lambda )^{m-1}\) and vanishes nowhere on \(S^*M\), a manifold of dimension \(2(m - 1) + 1\).

(3) In a slice chart with \(S = \{ q^{k+1} = \cdots = q^m = 0\} \), \(k {\lt} m\), the conormal bundle is \(N^*S = \{ q^{k+1} = \cdots = q^m = 0, \ p_1 = \cdots = p_k = 0\} \) (Corollary 41.4.3(3)), and \(\lambda = \sum _ip_i\, dq^i\) restricts to \(0\) on it: for \(i \leq k\) the coefficient \(p_i\) vanishes, and for \(i {\gt} k\) the form \(dq^i\) does. \(N^*S\) is invariant under the dilations \(\xi \mapsto e^t\xi \), so \(Z\) is tangent to it, and the restriction \(h\) of \(|\xi |^2\) to \(N^*S\) satisfies \(dh(Z) = 2h \neq 0\) where \(h = 1\). So \(N^*_1S = h^{-1}(1)\) is a submanifold of \(N^*S\) of dimension \(m - 1\), nonempty because \(k {\lt} m\); it lies in \(S^*M\), and \(\lambda \) vanishes on it. Since \(\dim S^*M = 2(m - 1) + 1\), it is Legendrian.

So the two geometries sit inside each other: Lagrangian submanifolds live in the symplectic manifold \(T^*M\), Legendrian ones in its hypersurface \(S^*M\), which carries the contact form. The next proposition makes the dictionary precise. A subset \(L \subseteq T^*M\) is conic if it is invariant under the dilations \(m_r(\xi ) = r\xi \), \(r {\gt} 0\); conormal bundles are conic.

03EY Proposition 41.7.6

Let \(M\) be a Riemannian manifold of dimension \(m\), \(\alpha \) the contact form on \(S^*M\) (Proposition 41.7.5(2)) and \(0_M\) the zero section.

  1. The map \(\Psi \colon S^*M \times (0, \infty ) \to T^*M \setminus 0_M\), \(\Psi (\xi , r) = r\xi \), is a diffeomorphism with \(\Psi ^*\lambda = r\alpha \), so that \(\Psi ^*\omega = -d(r\alpha )\): the symplectisation of \((S^*M, \alpha )\).

  2. If \(\Lambda \subseteq S^*M\) is a Legendrian submanifold, its cone \(\Psi (\Lambda \times (0, \infty ))\) is a conic Lagrangian submanifold of \(T^*M \setminus 0_M\) on which \(\lambda \) vanishes.

  3. Conversely, if \(L \subseteq T^*M \setminus 0_M\) is a conic Lagrangian submanifold, then \(\lambda \) vanishes on \(L\), and \(L \cap S^*M\) is a Legendrian submanifold whose cone is \(L\).

Proof â–¼

(1) \(\Psi \) is smooth, with smooth inverse \(\eta \mapsto (\eta /|\eta |, |\eta |)\). In coordinates \(m_r(q, p) = (q, rp)\), so \(m_r^*\lambda = r\lambda \). A tangent vector of \(S^*M \times (0, \infty )\) at \((\xi , r)\) is \((v, \rho \, \partial _r)\) with \(v \in T_\xi S^*M\), and \(d\Psi (v, \rho \, \partial _r) = dm_r(v) + \rho \, u\), where \(u = \frac{d}{ds}(r + s)\xi |_{s = 0}\) is tangent to the fibre \(T^*_qM\), so \(d\pi (u) = 0\) and \(\lambda (u) = 0\). Hence \((\Psi ^*\lambda )(v, \rho \, \partial _r) = (m_r^*\lambda )(v) = r\lambda (v) = r\alpha (v)\), that is, \(\Psi ^*\lambda = r\alpha \), and \(\Psi ^*\omega = -d\Psi ^*\lambda = -d(r\alpha )\).

(2) \(\Lambda \times (0, \infty )\) is a submanifold of dimension \((m - 1) + 1 = m\), and \(\Psi \) is a diffeomorphism, so its image \(L\) is a submanifold of dimension \(m\), clearly conic. By (1), \(\lambda \) restricted to \(L\) corresponds to \(r\alpha \) restricted to \(\Lambda \times (0, \infty )\), which is \(0\) because \(\alpha \) vanishes on \(\Lambda \). Then \(\omega = -d\lambda \) vanishes on \(L\) as well, and \(L\), of half the dimension of \(T^*M\), is Lagrangian (Proposition 41.2.2(3)).

(3) Since \(L\) is conic, the Euler vector field \(Z\) is tangent to \(L\). For \(v \in T_\xi L\), \(\lambda (v) = -\omega (Z_\xi , v) = 0\), because \(Z_\xi \) and \(v\) lie in the Lagrangian subspace \(T_\xi L\); so \(\lambda \) vanishes on \(L\). The restriction \(h\) of \(|\xi |^2\) to \(L\) satisfies \(dh(Z) = 2h \neq 0\), so \(\Lambda = L \cap S^*M = h^{-1}(1)\) is a submanifold of \(L\) of dimension \(m - 1\) (Theorem 33.6.11), on which \(\alpha \), the restriction of \(\lambda \), vanishes: it is Legendrian, as \(\dim S^*M = 2(m - 1) + 1\). Every \(\eta \in L\) is \(|\eta | \cdot (\eta /|\eta |)\) with \(\eta /|\eta | \in L \cap S^*M\), since \(L\) is conic; so \(L\) is the cone over \(\Lambda \).

By (3) applied to \(L = N^*S \setminus 0_M\), the proposition gives a second proof of Proposition 41.7.5(3). The dilations do not change the line through a covector, so one can also pass to the projectivised cotangent bundle \(\mathbb {P}(T^*M)\), the space of lines in the fibres, which needs no metric. There \(\lambda \) is defined only up to a nonzero factor, so \(\mathbb {P}(T^*M)\) carries a contact structure, the hyperplane field \(\ker \lambda \), but in general no global contact form; conic Lagrangian submanifolds correspond to its Legendrian submanifolds in the same way. This is the version used for the characteristic cycles of Remark 41.7.8.

03EM Example 41.7.7

  1. For \(S = \{ q\} \) a point, \(N^*_1S\) is the unit sphere \(S^*_qM\) of the fibre: a Legendrian sphere of dimension \(m - 1\). For a closed curve in \(\mathbb {R}^3\), the unit conormal bundle is a Legendrian torus in \(S^*\mathbb {R}^3 = \mathbb {R}^3 \times S^2\): the conormal vectors at each point of the curve form a circle.

  2. On \(\mathbb {R}^3\), the jet graph of \(f(q) = q^3\) is the Legendrian curve \(\{ (q, 3q^2, q^3)\} \). Its projection to the \((q, z)\)-plane is the graph of \(f\), smooth, while its projection to the \((q, p)\)-plane is the graph of \(f^{\prime }\), a Lagrangian curve in \(T^*\mathbb {R}\): projecting a Legendrian submanifold of \(J^1\mathbb {R}^n = T^*\mathbb {R}^n \times \mathbb {R}\) forgetting \(z\) gives an exact Lagrangian immersion, and \(z\) is a primitive of \(\lambda \) on it.

  3. The standard contact structure on \(S^3\). The horizontal planes of the Hopf connection (Example 38.7.6(3)) are the kernels of the restriction to \(S^3 \subseteq \mathbb {C}^2\) of \(\alpha = \sum _k(x_k\, dy_k - y_k\, dx_k)\), where \(z_k = x_k + iy_k\), since \(\alpha _z(u) = \operatorname {Im}\langle u, z\rangle \) and the tangent vectors with \(\langle u, z\rangle = 0\) are those orthogonal to \(iz\). This is a contact form, by the argument of (2) with the radial vector field \(R = \frac12\sum _k(x_k\, \partial _{x_k} + y_k\, \partial _{y_k})\), which satisfies \(\iota _R\omega _0 = \frac12\alpha \) and is transverse to \(S^3\). Its Legendrian curves are the horizontal curves of the Hopf connection.

03EN Remark 41.7.8

Normal cycles. Let \(X \subseteq \mathbb {R}^{n+1}\) be a compact region with smooth boundary \(\Sigma \) and outward unit normal \(\nu \). Identify \(T^*\mathbb {R}^{n+1}\) with \(\mathbb {R}^{n+1} \times \mathbb {R}^{n+1}\) by the Euclidean metric, so that \(S^*\mathbb {R}^{n+1} = \mathbb {R}^{n+1} \times S^n\) with contact form \(\alpha = \sum _i\xi _i\, dx^i\). The unit conormal bundle of \(\Sigma \) consists of the pairs \((x, \pm \nu (x))\); its component

\[ N(X) = \{ (x, \nu (x)) : x \in \Sigma \} \]

is the normal cycle of \(X\), a compact Legendrian submanifold of \(\mathbb {R}^{n+1} \times S^n\), oriented by \(\Sigma \), and so a closed current (Section 37.4).

  1. Curvatures as integrals over the normal cycle. The second projection \(\pi _2(x, \xi ) = \xi \) restricts on \(N(X)\) to the Gauss map, so by Remark 40.11.25

    \[ \int _{N(X)}\pi _2^*\mathrm{vol}_{S^n} = \int _\Sigma \nu ^*\mathrm{vol}_{S^n} = \deg (\nu )\operatorname {vol}(S^n) = \chi (X)\operatorname {vol}(S^n) . \]

    More generally, each coefficient \(\int _\Sigma \sigma _k(\kappa _1, \ldots , \kappa _n)\, dA\) of Steiner’s formula (Remark 40.11.23), with \(\sigma _k\) the elementary symmetric functions of the principal curvatures, is the integral over \(N(X)\) of an \(n\)-form \(\varphi _k\) on \(\mathbb {R}^{n+1} \times S^n\) that does not depend on \(X\), built from the \(dx^i\) and the \(d\xi _i\) [Fu94]. For \(k = 0\) this is the area of \(\Sigma \), and for \(k = n\) the computation above.

  2. Legendrian cycles of singular sets. The normal cycle survives without smoothness. For a compact convex set \(K\), the pairs \((x, \xi )\) with \(x \in \partial K\) and \(\xi \) the outward unit normal of a supporting hyperplane at \(x\) form a Lipschitz submanifold of \(\mathbb {R}^{n+1} \times S^n\), which defines a closed current on which \(\alpha \) vanishes: a Legendrian cycle. Integrating the forms \(\varphi _k\) over it defines the curvatures of \(K\), which are now measures and may be concentrated on edges and vertices; for a convex polygon the whole curvature sits at the vertices (Exercise 41.8.8). Federer introduced these curvature measures for sets of positive reach, which include convex sets and compact submanifolds [Fed59], and Fu constructed normal cycles of compact subanalytic sets, among them compact real algebraic sets [Fu94]. Normal cycles are additive, \(N(A \cup B) + N(A \cap B) = N(A) + N(B)\) when all four are defined, so the curvature integrals, the Euler characteristic among them, are additive functions of sets: this is the starting point of integral geometry.

  3. Characteristic cycles. In complex algebraic geometry, conic Lagrangian cycles in \(T^*M\), combinations of conormal bundles of subvarieties, appear as the characteristic cycles of constructible sheaves [KS90, Chapter IX], and MacPherson’s Chern classes of singular varieties [Mac74] can be computed from them: the same mechanism, curvature read off from conormal directions, extends characteristic classes to singular spaces.

03EZ Remark 41.7.9

Sphere bundle or cosphere bundle? The contact form lives naturally on covectors, because the tautological form \(\lambda \) lives on \(T^*M\): no metric is needed to define it, only to cut out the unit cosphere bundle \(S^*M\). Even the metric can be avoided: the space \(S(T^*M) = (T^*M \setminus 0_M)/(0, \infty )\) of rays of covectors carries the contact structure \(\ker \lambda \), well defined because \(m_r^*\lambda = r\lambda \) and independent of any choice, and every choice of metric identifies it with \(S^*M\). The tangent bundle has no such structure of its own. With a Riemannian metric, however, the isomorphism \(\flat \colon TM \to T^*M\), \(v \mapsto g(v, \cdot )\), maps the unit sphere bundle \(SM = \{ v \in TM : |v| = 1\} \) diffeomorphically onto \(S^*M\), and the pull-back of \(\lambda \) is

\[ (\flat ^*\lambda )_v(Y) = g\bigl(v, d\pi (Y)\bigr), \qquad Y \in T_v(TM), \]

since \(\pi \circ \flat = \pi \). So \(SM\) is a contact manifold too, with the contact form \(\alpha _v(Y) = g(v, d\pi (Y))\), and statements about unit covectors translate into statements about unit vectors. For the geodesic spray \(G\) of Proposition 40.6.1, \(d\pi (G_v) = v\), so \(\alpha (G) = |v|^2 = 1\) on \(SM\); in fact the geodesic flow is the Reeb flow of this contact form, the flow of the vector field \(R\) with \(\alpha (R) = 1\) and \(\iota _Rd\alpha = 0\) [MS17]. In \(\mathbb {R}^{n+1}\) with the Euclidean metric the identification is the obvious one, which is why the normal cycles of Remark 41.7.8 are usually said to live in the unit sphere bundle \(\mathbb {R}^{n+1} \times S^n\) rather than in the cosphere bundle: the normal vector \(\nu (x)\) and the conormal covector \(\langle \nu (x), \cdot \rangle \) carry the same information.
