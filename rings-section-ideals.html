8.3 Ideals and quotient rings00EJ

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

00EK Definition 8.3.1

A left ideal of a ring \(R\) is an additive subgroup \(I\) with \(ra \in I\) for \(r \in R\), \(a \in I\); right ideals are defined similarly, and a (two-sided) ideal is both. For a commutative ring these notions agree. The ideal generated by a subset \(S\) of a commutative ring is \((S) = \{ r_1 s_1 + \cdots + r_n s_n : r_i \in R, s_i \in S\} \); an ideal of the form \((a)\) is principal. For ideals \(I\), \(J\) of a commutative ring, \(I + J\), \(I \cap J\) and the product \(IJ\), generated by the products \(ab\) with \(a \in I\), \(b \in J\), are ideals, and \(IJ \subseteq I \cap J\).

00EL Theorem 8.3.2

Let \(I\) be an ideal of \(R\). The quotient group \(R/I\) is a ring with \((a + I)(b + I) = ab + I\), and \(\pi \colon R \to R/I\) is a surjective ring homomorphism with kernel \(I\). Every ring homomorphism \(f \colon R \to S\) with \(I \subseteq \ker f\) factors uniquely as \(\bar f \circ \pi \). Kernels of ring homomorphisms are ideals, and \(R/\ker f \cong \operatorname {im}f\). The ideals of \(R/I\) correspond to the ideals of \(R\) containing \(I\) via \(J \mapsto J/I\), and \((R/I)/(J/I) \cong R/J\).

Proof â–¼

If \(a - a', b - b' \in I\), then \(ab - a'b' = a(b - b') + (a - a')b' \in I\), so the product is well defined; the ring axioms follow from those of \(R\). The remaining statements follow from Theorems 7.4.3 and 7.4.4 applied to the additive groups, together with the observation that the maps constructed there preserve products and that preimages and images of ideals under surjective ring homomorphisms are ideals.

00EM Theorem 8.3.3 Chinese remainder theorem

Let \(R\) be a commutative ring and \(I_1, \ldots , I_n\) ideals with \(I_i + I_j = R\) for \(i \neq j\). Then \(I_1 \cap \cdots \cap I_n = I_1 \cdots I_n\), and the map \(R \to \prod _i R/I_i\) induces an isomorphism \(R/(I_1 \cap \cdots \cap I_n) \cong \prod _i R/I_i\).

Proof â–¼

For \(n = 2\): write \(1 = a + b\) with \(a \in I_1\), \(b \in I_2\). If \(x \in I_1 \cap I_2\) then \(x = xa + xb \in I_1I_2\). Given \(r_1, r_2 \in R\), the element \(r_1 b + r_2 a\) is congruent to \(r_1\) modulo \(I_1\) and to \(r_2\) modulo \(I_2\), so the map is surjective; its kernel is \(I_1 \cap I_2\). For \(n {\gt} 2\), note that \(I_1 + I_2 \cdots I_n = R\): from \(1 = a_j + b_j\) with \(a_j \in I_1\), \(b_j \in I_j\) we get \(1 = \prod _{j \geq 2}(a_j + b_j) \in I_1 + I_2 \cdots I_n\). Now use induction.

00EN Example 8.3.4

If \(n = p_1^{e_1} \cdots p_k^{e_k}\) with distinct primes \(p_i\), then \(\mathbb {Z}/n\mathbb {Z}\cong \prod _i \mathbb {Z}/p_i^{e_i}\mathbb {Z}\) as rings, since \((p_i^{e_i}) + (p_j^{e_j}) = \mathbb {Z}\) by Theorem 4.5.3.
