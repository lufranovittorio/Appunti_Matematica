40.13 Minimal surfaces02KG

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

A soap film spanning a wire takes the shape that minimises its area. Its surface is therefore a critical point of the area, and the first variation formula below identifies these critical points: they are the hypersurfaces with mean curvature \(H = 0\), the minimal ones. We give the classical examples, show that a minimal graph has the least area among all surfaces with the same boundary, compute the second variation, and prove Bernstein’s theorem: a minimal graph over the whole plane is a plane. Throughout, \(M \subseteq \mathbb {R}^{n+1}\) is a hypersurface with unit normal \(\nu \), shape operator \(S = -d\nu \) and mean curvature \(H = \frac1n\operatorname {tr}S\) (Definition 40.11.9 and Proposition 40.11.15), and we write \(|S|^2 = \operatorname {tr}(S^2) = \sum _i\kappa _i^2\).

02KH Proposition 40.13.1 First and second variation of area

Let \(x \colon \Omega \to \mathbb {R}^{n+1}\) be a parametrisation of \(M\), \(\phi \) a smooth function with compact support \(K \subseteq \Omega \), and \(x_s = x + s\phi \nu \) (with \(\nu = \nu \circ x\)). For small \(|s|\) the matrix \(g(s)_{ij} = \langle \partial _ix_s, \partial _jx_s\rangle \) is positive definite on \(K\); let \(A(s) = \int _K\sqrt{\det g(s)}\, du\), the area of the piece \(x_s(K)\). Then, with \(dA = \sqrt{\det g}\, du\) and \(|\nabla \phi |^2 = \sum _{i,j}g^{ij}\partial _i\phi \, \partial _j\phi \),

\[ A^{\prime }(0) = -n\int _KH\phi \, dA, \qquad \text{and, if } H = 0, \qquad A^{\prime \prime }(0) = \int _K\bigl(|\nabla \phi |^2 - |S|^2\phi ^2\bigr)\, dA . \]
Proof â–¼

Write \(x_i = \partial _ix\) and \(\nu _i = \partial _i\nu \). Then \(\partial _ix_s = x_i + s(\partial _i\phi \, \nu + \phi \nu _i)\). Since \(\langle x_i, \nu \rangle = 0\), \(\langle \nu _i, \nu \rangle = 0\) and \(|\nu | = 1\),

\[ g(s)_{ij} = g_{ij} + s\phi \bigl(\langle x_i, \nu _j\rangle + \langle \nu _i, x_j\rangle \bigr) + s^2\bigl(\partial _i\phi \, \partial _j\phi + \phi ^2\langle \nu _i, \nu _j\rangle \bigr). \]

By Proposition 40.11.15, \(\langle x_i, \nu _j\rangle = -h_{ij}\), and \(\nu _i = -\sum _kS^k_ix_k\) with \((S^k_i) = g^{-1}h\) the matrix of \(S\), so \(\langle \nu _i, \nu _j\rangle = (S^Tg\, S)_{ij} = (hg^{-1}h)_{ij}\). Hence \(g(s) = g(I + sY + s^2Z)\) with \(Y = -2\phi \, g^{-1}h\) and \(Z = g^{-1}(\nabla \phi \, \nabla \phi ^T) + \phi ^2(g^{-1}h)^2\), where \(\nabla \phi \) is the column \((\partial _i\phi )\); in particular \(g(s)\) is positive definite on \(K\) for small \(|s|\). For a matrix \(X\), \(\det (I + X) = 1 + \operatorname {tr}X + \frac12\bigl((\operatorname {tr}X)^2 - \operatorname {tr}(X^2)\bigr) + O(|X|^3)\) (expand the characteristic polynomial, or use the eigenvalues), and \(\sqrt{1 + \epsilon } = 1 + \frac\epsilon 2 - \frac{\epsilon ^2}8 + O(\epsilon ^3)\). With \(X = sY + s^2Z\) this gives

\[ \sqrt{\det g(s)} = \sqrt{\det g}\, \Bigl(1 + \frac s2\operatorname {tr}Y + s^2\Bigl(\frac12\operatorname {tr}Z + \frac18(\operatorname {tr}Y)^2 - \frac14\operatorname {tr}(Y^2)\Bigr) + O(s^3)\Bigr), \]

uniformly on \(K\). Now \(\operatorname {tr}Y = -2\phi \operatorname {tr}S = -2nH\phi \), \(\operatorname {tr}(Y^2) = 4\phi ^2|S|^2\) and \(\operatorname {tr}Z = |\nabla \phi |^2 + \phi ^2|S|^2\). So the coefficient of \(s\) is \(-nH\phi \), and if \(H = 0\) the coefficient of \(s^2\) is \(\frac12(|\nabla \phi |^2 + \phi ^2|S|^2) - \phi ^2|S|^2 = \frac12(|\nabla \phi |^2 - |S|^2\phi ^2)\). Integrating over \(K\) gives \(A^{\prime }(0)\) and \(A^{\prime \prime }(0)\).

02UE Remark 40.13.2

The second variation without \(H = 0\), and the Jacobi operator. The computation in the proof gives, without assuming \(H = 0\), the coefficient of \(s^2\) as \(\frac12\operatorname {tr}Z + \frac18(\operatorname {tr}Y)^2 - \frac14\operatorname {tr}(Y^2) = \frac12(|\nabla \phi |^2 - |S|^2\phi ^2 + n^2H^2\phi ^2)\), so

\[ A^{\prime \prime }(0) = \int _K\bigl(|\nabla \phi |^2 - |S|^2\phi ^2 + n^2H^2\phi ^2\bigr)\, dA . \]

For the sphere of radius \(r\) in \(\mathbb {R}^3\) and \(\phi = 1\) (the computation is local, so it applies to the whole sphere), \(|S|^2 = 2/r^2\) and \(n^2H^2 = 4/r^2\), so \(A^{\prime \prime }(0) = \frac{2}{r^2} \cdot 4\pi r^2 = 8\pi \), in agreement with \(A(s) = 4\pi (r + s)^2\). When \(H = 0\), Green’s formula (Proposition 40.5.4, applied on a compact domain with smooth boundary containing the support of \(\phi \)) gives \(\int |\nabla \phi |^2 = -\int \phi \, \Delta \phi \), hence

\[ A^{\prime \prime }(0) = -\int _K\phi \, L\phi \, dA, \qquad L = \Delta + |S|^2, \]

the Jacobi operator, or stability operator, of the minimal hypersurface: \(M\) is stable (the area does not decrease to second order under compactly supported normal variations) if and only if \(-L\) is nonnegative on such \(\phi \). For instance a minimal graph is stable, since it minimises area (Proposition 40.13.6 below).

02KI Definition 40.13.3

A hypersurface \(M \subseteq \mathbb {R}^{n+1}\) is minimal if \(H = 0\) everywhere. A minimal hypersurface is stable if \(A^{\prime \prime }(0) \geq 0\) for all variations as in Proposition 40.13.1.

By the proposition, \(M\) is minimal if and only if \(A^{\prime }(0) = 0\) for every such variation: if \(H(u_0) \neq 0\), a function \(\phi \geq 0\) supported where \(H\) has the sign of \(H(u_0)\), with \(\phi (u_0) {\gt} 0\), gives \(A^{\prime }(0) \neq 0\). The word “minimal” refers to this critical point property; a minimal surface need not minimise area. For a minimal surface in \(\mathbb {R}^3\) the principal curvatures are \(\pm \kappa \), so \(|S|^2 = 2\kappa ^2 = -2K\), and the second variation is \(\int _K(|\nabla \phi |^2 + 2K\phi ^2)\, dA\).

02KJ Example 40.13.4

We use Proposition 40.11.14.

  1. Planes are minimal, with \(S = 0\).

  2. The catenoid, the surface of revolution of the catenary, \(x(u, v) = (\cosh v\cos u, \cosh v\sin u, v)\). Here \(E = G = \cosh ^2v\), \(F = 0\), the unit normal is \(\nu = (\cos u, \sin u, -\sinh v)/\cosh v\), and \(h_{11} = -1\), \(h_{12} = 0\), \(h_{22} = 1\). So \(H = (Eh_{22} + Gh_{11})/(2EG) = 0\), and \(K = -1/\cosh ^4v\).

  3. The helicoid, swept out by a line rotating about an axis while moving along it, \(x(u, v) = (v\cos u, v\sin u, u)\). Here \(E = 1 + v^2\), \(F = 0\), \(G = 1\), \(\nu = (-\sin u, \cos u, -v)/\sqrt{1 + v^2}\), and \(h_{11} = h_{22} = 0\), \(h_{12} = 1/\sqrt{1 + v^2}\). So \(H = 0\) and \(K = -1/(1 + v^2)^2\).

02KK Proposition 40.13.5 Minimal surface equation

Let \(u \colon \Omega \to \mathbb {R}\) be smooth on an open \(\Omega \subseteq \mathbb {R}^2\), write \(p = u_x\), \(q = u_y\) and \(W = \sqrt{1 + p^2 + q^2}\), and give the graph of \(u\) the upward unit normal \(\nu = (-p, -q, 1)/W\). Then its mean curvature is

\[ 2H = \frac{(1 + q^2)u_{xx} - 2pq\, u_{xy} + (1 + p^2)u_{yy}}{W^3} = \frac{\partial }{\partial x}\Bigl(\frac pW\Bigr) + \frac{\partial }{\partial y}\Bigl(\frac qW\Bigr). \]

So the graph is minimal if and only if \(u\) solves the minimal surface equation \((1 + q^2)u_{xx} - 2pq\, u_{xy} + (1 + p^2)u_{yy} = 0\).

Proof â–¼

For \(x(x, y) = (x, y, u(x, y))\): \(E = 1 + p^2\), \(F = pq\), \(G = 1 + q^2\), \(EG - F^2 = W^2\), and \(h_{11} = u_{xx}/W\), \(h_{12} = u_{xy}/W\), \(h_{22} = u_{yy}/W\), since the second derivatives of \(x\) are \((0, 0, u_{xx})\), \((0, 0, u_{xy})\), \((0, 0, u_{yy})\). The formula for \(H\) in Proposition 40.11.14 gives the first expression. For the second, \(\partial _x(p/W) = (u_{xx}W^2 - p(pu_{xx} + qu_{xy}))/W^3\) and \(\partial _y(q/W) = (u_{yy}W^2 - q(pu_{xy} + qu_{yy}))/W^3\), whose sum is the first expression, as \(W^2 - p^2 = 1 + q^2\) and \(W^2 - q^2 = 1 + p^2\).

For example Scherk’s surface, the graph of \(u = \log \cos y - \log \cos x\) over the square \(|x|, |y| {\lt} \pi /2\), is minimal: \(p = \tan x\), \(q = -\tan y\), \(u_{xy} = 0\), and \((1 + q^2)u_{xx} + (1 + p^2)u_{yy} = \sec ^2y\sec ^2x - \sec ^2x\sec ^2y = 0\). It tends to \(\pm \infty \) on the sides of the square, and cannot be extended to a larger domain.

02KL Proposition 40.13.6 Minimal graphs minimise area

Let \(\Omega \subseteq \mathbb {R}^2\) be open and convex, \(u \colon \Omega \to \mathbb {R}\) a solution of the minimal surface equation, and \(G \subseteq \Omega \times \mathbb {R}\) a compact piece of its graph that is a surface with boundary, oriented by the upward normal. Let \(\Sigma \subseteq \Omega \times \mathbb {R}\) be a compact oriented surface with boundary (Definition 36.4.5) with \(\partial \Sigma = \partial G\) as oriented curves. Then \(\mathrm{Area}(\Sigma ) \geq \mathrm{Area}(G)\).

Proof â–¼

Extend the unit normal of the graph to the unit vector field \(N(x, y, z) = (-p, -q, 1)/W\) on \(\Omega \times \mathbb {R}\), independent of \(z\), and let \(\omega = \iota _N(dx \wedge dy \wedge dz)\). As in the proof of Corollary 40.3.13, \(d\omega = (\operatorname {div}N)\, dx \wedge dy \wedge dz\), and \(\operatorname {div}N = -\partial _x(p/W) - \partial _y(q/W) = -2H = 0\) by Proposition 40.13.5. The open set \(\Omega \times \mathbb {R}\) is convex, so \(\omega = d\eta \) for a \(1\)-form \(\eta \) by the Poincaré lemma (Theorem 37.2.4). By Stokes’ theorem (Theorem 36.4.8), \(\int _\Sigma \omega = \int _{\partial \Sigma }\eta = \int _{\partial G}\eta = \int _G\omega \). By Proposition 40.3.7, on a surface with unit normal \(n\) defining its orientation, \(\omega \) restricts to \(\langle N, n\rangle \, dA\). On \(G\), \(n = N\), so \(\int _G\omega = \mathrm{Area}(G)\); on \(\Sigma \), \(\langle N, n\rangle \leq 1\), so \(\int _\Sigma \omega \leq \mathrm{Area}(\Sigma )\).

The form \(\omega \) is a calibration: a closed form whose restriction to every surface is at most the area form, with equality on the graph. In particular minimal graphs are stable, since the surfaces \(x_s(K)\) of Proposition 40.13.1 stay in \(\Omega \times \mathbb {R}\) and have the same boundary as the graph for small \(|s|\). The comparison only involves surfaces inside the cylinder \(\Omega \times \mathbb {R}\) over a convex domain, where the calibration is defined; a minimal surface that is not a graph, such as the catenoid, need not have this property.

The minimal surface equation is a nonlinear elliptic equation, and its entire solutions are much more rigid than harmonic functions: \(x^2 - y^2\) is harmonic on all of \(\mathbb {R}^2\) without being affine, but the only minimal graphs over the whole plane are planes. The proof below is Nitsche’s [Nit57]; it reduces the statement to Liouville’s theorem through a theorem of Jörgens on the Monge–Ampère equation.

02KM Lemma 40.13.7 Jörgens

Let \(\varphi \colon \mathbb {R}^2 \to \mathbb {R}\) be smooth with \(\varphi _{xx} {\gt} 0\) and \(\varphi _{xx}\varphi _{yy} - \varphi _{xy}^2 = 1\) everywhere. Then the Hessian of \(\varphi \) is constant.

Proof â–¼

Write \(a = \varphi _{xx}\), \(b = \varphi _{xy}\), \(c = \varphi _{yy}\), so \(a {\gt} 0\) and \(ac - b^2 = 1\); then \(c {\gt} 0\), the Hessian \(\mathcal{H}\) is positive definite, and \(a + c \geq 2\sqrt{ac} \geq 2\).

Step 1: the Lewy map. Let \(L \colon \mathbb {R}^2 \to \mathbb {R}^2\), \(L(z) = z + \nabla \varphi (z)\). For \(z_1, z_2 \in \mathbb {R}^2\), \(\langle \nabla \varphi (z_2) - \nabla \varphi (z_1), z_2 - z_1\rangle = \int _0^1\langle \mathcal{H}(z_1 + t(z_2 - z_1))(z_2 - z_1), z_2 - z_1\rangle \, dt \geq 0\), so

\[ |L(z_2) - L(z_1)|^2 = |z_2 - z_1|^2 + 2\langle \nabla \varphi (z_2) - \nabla \varphi (z_1), z_2 - z_1\rangle + |\nabla \varphi (z_2) - \nabla \varphi (z_1)|^2 \geq |z_2 - z_1|^2 . \]

So \(L\) is injective, and its image is closed: if \(L(z_k) \to w\), then \((z_k)\) is a Cauchy sequence, \(z_k \to z\) and \(L(z) = w\). Its differential \(I + \mathcal{H}\) is positive definite, hence invertible, so \(L\) is a local diffeomorphism (Theorem 27.11.2) and its image is open. As \(\mathbb {R}^2\) is connected, \(L\) is a diffeomorphism of \(\mathbb {R}^2\) onto itself.

Step 2: a holomorphic function. Write \((\xi , \eta ) = L(x, y)\) and \(\zeta = \xi + i\eta \), and define \(F(\zeta ) = w + iv\) with \(w = x - \varphi _x\) and \(v = \varphi _y - y\), where \((x, y) = L^{-1}(\xi , \eta )\); \(F\) is smooth. From \(d(\xi , \eta ) = (I + \mathcal{H})\, d(x, y)\) and \(d(w, v) = N\, d(x, y)\) with \(N = \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 - a & -b \\ b & c - 1 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\), the Jacobian matrix of \((w, v)\) with respect to \((\xi , \eta )\) is

\[ N(I + \mathcal{H})^{-1} = \frac1{2 + a + c}\begin{pmatrix} 1 - a & -b \\ b & c - 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 + c & -b \\ -b & 1 + a \end{pmatrix} = \frac1{2 + a + c}\begin{pmatrix} c - a & -2b \\ 2b & c - a \end{pmatrix}, \]

using \(\det (I + \mathcal{H}) = 1 + a + c + ac - b^2 = 2 + a + c\) and \(ac - b^2 = 1\) to simplify the entries. This matrix satisfies the Cauchy–Riemann equations, so \(F\) is an entire function (Proposition 29.2.3), with

\[ F^{\prime } = \frac{(c - a) + 2ib}{2 + a + c}, \qquad |F^{\prime }|^2 = \frac{(a + c)^2 - 4(ac - b^2)}{(a + c + 2)^2} = \frac{a + c - 2}{a + c + 2} {\lt} 1 . \]

Step 3: Liouville. The derivative \(F^{\prime }\) is entire (Theorem 29.4.2) and bounded, hence constant (Corollary 29.4.7). So \(\rho = |F^{\prime }|^2 {\lt} 1\) is constant, hence so is \(a + c = 2(1 + \rho )/(1 - \rho )\), and then the real and imaginary parts of \(F^{\prime }\) show that \(c - a\) and \(b\) are constant. So \(a\), \(b\) and \(c\) are constant.

02KN Theorem 40.13.8 Bernstein

Let \(u \colon \mathbb {R}^2 \to \mathbb {R}\) be a smooth solution of the minimal surface equation on the whole plane. Then \(u\) is affine, \(u(x, y) = \alpha x + \beta y + \gamma \): an entire minimal graph is a plane.

Proof â–¼

With the notation of Proposition 40.13.5, write \(\mathcal{M} = (1 + q^2)u_{xx} - 2pq\, u_{xy} + (1 + p^2)u_{yy}\), and consider the smooth functions

\[ A = \frac{1 + p^2}{W}, \qquad B = \frac{pq}{W}, \qquad C = \frac{1 + q^2}{W}. \]

A direct computation, using \(p_x = u_{xx}\), \(p_y = q_x = u_{xy}\) and \(q_y = u_{yy}\), gives

\[ \frac{\partial A}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial B}{\partial x} = -\frac{q\mathcal{M}}{W^3}, \qquad \frac{\partial C}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial B}{\partial y} = -\frac{p\mathcal{M}}{W^3} \]

(for the first, both \(W^3\partial _yA\) and \(W^3\partial _xB\) are polynomials in \(p, q, u_{xx}, u_{xy}, u_{yy}\), and their difference is \(-q\mathcal{M}\); the second follows by exchanging \(x\) and \(y\)). As \(\mathcal{M} = 0\), the \(1\)-forms \(A\, dx + B\, dy\) and \(B\, dx + C\, dy\) are closed on \(\mathbb {R}^2\), hence exact by the Poincaré lemma (Theorem 37.2.4): there are smooth \(P\), \(Q\) with \(dP = A\, dx + B\, dy\) and \(dQ = B\, dx + C\, dy\). Then \(P\, dx + Q\, dy\) is closed, since \(P_y = B = Q_x\), so it is \(d\varphi \) for a smooth \(\varphi \). Its Hessian is

\[ \varphi _{xx} = A = \frac{1 + p^2}{W} {\gt} 0, \qquad \varphi _{xy} = B, \qquad \varphi _{yy} = C, \qquad \varphi _{xx}\varphi _{yy} - \varphi _{xy}^2 = \frac{(1 + p^2)(1 + q^2) - p^2q^2}{W^2} = 1 . \]

By Lemma 40.13.7, \(A\), \(B\) and \(C\) are constant. Then \(A + C = (2 + p^2 + q^2)/W = W + 1/W\) is constant, and so is \(W \geq 1\), since \(t \mapsto t + 1/t\) is injective on \([1, \infty )\). Hence \(p^2 = AW - 1\) and \(q^2 = CW - 1\) are constant, and so are \(p\) and \(q\), being continuous on the connected plane with constant square. So \(u\) has constant gradient and is affine.

02KO Remark 40.13.9

The Bernstein problem asks whether an entire minimal graph over \(\mathbb {R}^n\), a solution of \(\operatorname {div}(\nabla u/\sqrt{1 + |\nabla u|^2}) = 0\) on \(\mathbb {R}^n\), must be affine. The answer is yes for \(n \leq 7\) (De Giorgi for \(n = 3\), Almgren for \(n = 4\), Simons [Sim68] for \(n \leq 7\)) and no for \(n \geq 8\): Bombieri, De Giorgi and Giusti [BDGG69] constructed a non-affine entire minimal graph over \(\mathbb {R}^8\), related to the area-minimising cone over \(S^3 \times S^3 \subseteq \mathbb {R}^8\). The existence of minimal surfaces spanning a given curve, Plateau’s problem, is a separate and deep theory.

Minimal hypersurfaces in a Riemannian manifold. So far the ambient space was \(\mathbb {R}^{n+1}\). In a curved ambient manifold \(\overline{M}\) a new phenomenon appears: the normal geodesics leaving a hypersurface converge or diverge according to the curvature of \(\overline{M}\), exactly as nearby geodesics do in the Jacobi equation (Definition 40.8.7), and this changes the area of the pushed hypersurface to second order. The relevant curvature is the average over the tangent directions of the sectional curvatures of the planes containing the normal: for an orthonormal basis \(e_1, \ldots , e_n\) of \(T_pM\) and the unit normal \(\nu \),

\[ \overline{\mathrm{Ric}}(\nu , \nu ) = \sum _{a=1}^n\overline{R}(e_a, \nu , \nu , e_a) = \sum _{a=1}^n\overline{K}(e_a, \nu ), \]

by Definition 40.8.1 applied to the orthonormal basis \(e_1, \ldots , e_n, \nu \) of \(T_p\overline{M}\) (the term with \(\nu \) vanishes). The second variation formula below is the form in which the area variation is usually quoted.

02WM Proposition 40.13.10 Second variation of area in a Riemannian manifold

Let \(\overline{M}\) be a Riemannian manifold of dimension \(n + 1\), with Levi-Civita connection \(\overline{\nabla }\) and curvature \(\overline{R}\), and let \(M \subseteq \overline{M}\) be a hypersurface with unit normal field \(\nu \), shape operator \(S\) and mean curvature \(H\) (Definition 40.11.9). Let \(x \colon \Omega \to M\) be a parametrisation, \(\Omega \subseteq \mathbb {R}^n\) open, and let \(F \colon \Omega \times (-\varepsilon , \varepsilon ) \to \overline{M}\) be smooth with \(F(u, 0) = x(u)\), with \(F(u, s) = x(u)\) for \(u\) outside a compact set \(K \subseteq \Omega \), and with \(\partial _sF(u, 0) = \phi (u)\nu (x(u))\) for a smooth function \(\phi \). Let \(g(s)_{ij} = \langle \partial _iF, \partial _jF\rangle \), which is positive definite on \(K\) for small \(|s|\), and \(A(s) = \int _K\sqrt{\det g(s)}\, du\). Then \(A^{\prime }(0) = -n\int _KH\phi \, dA\), and if \(H = 0\),

\[ A^{\prime \prime }(0) = \int _K\Bigl(|\nabla \phi |^2 - \bigl(|S|^2 + \overline{\mathrm{Ric}}(\nu , \nu )\bigr)\phi ^2\Bigr)\, dA . \]
Proof â–¼

Step 1: the setting. Write \(F_i = \partial _iF\) and \(T = \partial _sF\), vector fields along \(F\), and \(D_s\), \(D_i\) for the covariant derivatives along the curves \(s \mapsto F(u, s)\) and \(u^i \mapsto F(u, s)\) (Proposition 40.4.1). Two facts will be used: \(D_sF_i = D_iT\) by the symmetry lemma (Lemma 40.4.3), and \(D_sD_iT - D_iD_sT = \overline{R}(T, F_i)T\) by Lemma 40.8.6. At \(s = 0\) we write \(x_i = \partial _ix\), \(h_{ij} = h(x_i, x_j)\), and \(Z = D_sT|_{s=0}\), the acceleration of the curves \(s \mapsto F(u, s)\); for \(u \notin K\) these curves are constant, so \(T\) and \(Z\) vanish there.

Step 2: derivatives of the metric. By the metric property of \(D_s\) and the symmetry lemma,

\[ g^{\prime }_{ij} = \langle D_iT, F_j\rangle + \langle F_i, D_jT\rangle , \qquad g^{\prime \prime }_{ij} = \langle D_sD_iT, F_j\rangle + \langle F_i, D_sD_jT\rangle + 2\langle D_iT, D_jT\rangle . \]

Step 3: derivatives of the area density. For a smooth family of invertible matrices, \((\det g)^{\prime } = \det g\, \operatorname {tr}(g^{-1}g^{\prime })\): indeed \(\det g(s + t) = \det g(s)\det \bigl(I + g(s)^{-1}(g(s + t) - g(s))\bigr)\) and \(\det (I + X) = 1 + \operatorname {tr}X + O(|X|^2)\). Also \((g^{-1})^{\prime } = -g^{-1}g^{\prime }g^{-1}\), obtained by differentiating \(g^{-1}g = I\). Hence

\[ \frac{(\sqrt{\det g})^{\prime }}{\sqrt{\det g}} = \frac12\operatorname {tr}(g^{-1}g^{\prime }), \qquad \frac{(\sqrt{\det g})^{\prime \prime }}{\sqrt{\det g}} = \frac14\bigl(\operatorname {tr}(g^{-1}g^{\prime })\bigr)^2 + \frac12\operatorname {tr}(g^{-1}g^{\prime \prime }) - \frac12\operatorname {tr}\bigl((g^{-1}g^{\prime })^2\bigr). \]

For a symmetric bilinear form \(B\) on \(T_pM\), \(\sum _{i,j}g^{ij}B(x_i, x_j) = \sum _aB(e_a, e_a)\) for any orthonormal basis \((e_a)\), the trace of \(B\) with respect to the metric; we use this repeatedly.

Step 4: the values at \(s = 0\). Since \(|\nu | = 1\), \(D_i\nu = \overline{\nabla }_{x_i}\nu \) is orthogonal to \(\nu \), so it is tangent, and \(D_i\nu = -Sx_i\) by Definition 40.11.6. So \(D_iT = (\partial _i\phi )\nu - \phi \, Sx_i\), and

\[ \langle D_iT, x_j\rangle = -\phi h_{ij}, \qquad \langle D_iT, D_jT\rangle = \partial _i\phi \, \partial _j\phi + \phi ^2\langle Sx_i, Sx_j\rangle . \]

Hence \(g^{-1}g^{\prime }\) is the matrix of \(-2\phi S\), with trace \(-2nH\phi \), and \(\operatorname {tr}((g^{-1}g^{\prime })^2) = 4\phi ^2|S|^2\). This already gives \(A^{\prime }(0) = \int _K\frac12(-2nH\phi )\, dA = -n\int _KH\phi \, dA\), differentiating under the integral sign (the integrand is smooth on \(K \times (-\varepsilon ^{\prime }, \varepsilon ^{\prime })\)). Further, \(\sum g^{ij}\langle D_iT, D_jT\rangle = |\nabla \phi |^2 + \phi ^2\sum _a|Se_a|^2 = |\nabla \phi |^2 + \phi ^2|S|^2\), as \(S\) is self-adjoint. Finally \(D_sD_iT = D_iZ + \phi ^2\overline{R}(\nu , x_i)\nu \) at \(s = 0\), and

\[ \sum _{i,j}g^{ij}\langle \overline{R}(\nu , x_i)\nu , x_j\rangle = \sum _a\overline{R}(\nu , e_a, \nu , e_a) = -\sum _a\overline{R}(e_a, \nu , \nu , e_a) = -\overline{\mathrm{Ric}}(\nu , \nu ). \]

So \(\frac12\operatorname {tr}(g^{-1}g^{\prime \prime }) = \sum g^{ij}\langle D_iZ, x_j\rangle - \phi ^2\, \overline{\mathrm{Ric}}(\nu , \nu ) + |\nabla \phi |^2 + \phi ^2|S|^2\).

Step 5: the term with \(Z\). Write \(Z = Z^\top + f\nu \) with \(Z^\top \) tangent to \(M\). Then \(\langle D_i(f\nu ), x_j\rangle = f\langle D_i\nu , x_j\rangle = -fh_{ij}\), whose trace is \(-nHf\). By the Gauss formula (Theorem 40.11.2), the tangential part of \(D_iZ^\top = \overline{\nabla }_{x_i}Z^\top \) is \(\nabla _{x_i}Z^\top \), so the trace of \(\langle D_iZ^\top , x_j\rangle \) is \(\operatorname {div}_MZ^\top \) (Definition 40.5.1(3)). So \(\sum g^{ij}\langle D_iZ, x_j\rangle = \operatorname {div}_MZ^\top - nHf\).

Step 6: conclusion. Now let \(H = 0\). Collecting Steps 3–5,

\[ \frac{(\sqrt{\det g})^{\prime \prime }(0)}{\sqrt{\det g}} = 0 + \operatorname {div}_MZ^\top - \phi ^2\, \overline{\mathrm{Ric}}(\nu , \nu ) + |\nabla \phi |^2 + \phi ^2|S|^2 - 2\phi ^2|S|^2 . \]

The vector field \(Z^\top \) vanishes outside \(K\), so \(\int _K\operatorname {div}_MZ^\top \, dA = 0\) by the divergence theorem (Proposition 40.5.4, applied on a compact domain with smooth boundary in \(x(\Omega )\) containing \(x(K)\) in its interior). Differentiating twice under the integral sign gives the formula. (For the variation \(F(u, s) = \exp _{x(u)}(s\phi (u)\nu (x(u)))\), which exists for small \(|s|\) as at the beginning of Section 40.10, the curves \(s \mapsto F(u, s)\) are geodesics, \(Z = 0\), and Step 5 is not needed.)

02WN Remark 40.13.11

Reading the formula: what each term means. Write \(Q(\phi ) = \int _K(|\nabla \phi |^2 - (|S|^2 + \overline{\mathrm{Ric}}(\nu , \nu ))\phi ^2)\, dA\) for the second variation. Its three terms describe three different effects of pushing \(M\) by the amount \(\phi \) in the normal direction.

  1. \(|\nabla \phi |^2\): tilting. Pushing different points by different amounts tilts the hypersurface, and a tilted piece has more area. For a flat piece of a hyperplane in \(\mathbb {R}^{n+1}\) this is exact: the pushed hypersurface is the graph of \(s\phi \), whose area element is \(\sqrt{1 + s^2|\nabla \phi |^2} = 1 + \frac{s^2}2|\nabla \phi |^2 + O(s^4)\), with second derivative \(|\nabla \phi |^2\) at \(s = 0\). This term is always \(\geq 0\), and it is the only one for a hyperplane in \(\mathbb {R}^{n+1}\), which is therefore stable.

  2. \(-|S|^2\phi ^2\): the shape of \(M\). Push a piece of a hypersurface in \(\mathbb {R}^{n+1}\) by the constant amount \(s\): then \(\partial _i(x + s\nu ) = x_i - sSx_i\), so the area element is multiplied by \(\det (I - sS) = \prod _i(1 - s\kappa _i) = 1 - s\sum _i\kappa _i + s^2\sum _{i{\lt}j}\kappa _i\kappa _j + O(s^3)\). If \(H = 0\), then \(\sum _{i{\lt}j}\kappa _i\kappa _j = \frac12((\sum _i\kappa _i)^2 - \sum _i\kappa _i^2) = -\frac12|S|^2\), and the second derivative is \(-|S|^2\). Geometrically, a minimal surface curves equally in opposite directions (\(\kappa _2 = -\kappa _1\) in \(\mathbb {R}^3\), like a saddle); pushing it expands it in one principal direction and contracts it in the other, the first-order effects cancel, and the second-order effect is a loss of area.

  3. \(-\overline{\mathrm{Ric}}(\nu , \nu )\phi ^2\): the curvature of the ambient space. The pushed hypersurface consists of points on the normal geodesics. If the ambient sectional curvatures \(\overline{K}(e_a, \nu )\) are positive, these geodesics converge, as the meridians do when one pushes the equator of a sphere towards a pole, and the area decreases; the amount is the sum \(\sum _a\overline{K}(e_a, \nu ) = \overline{\mathrm{Ric}}(\nu , \nu )\) over the \(n\) tangent directions (Example 40.13.12 below computes it exactly). In a space of constant curvature \(\kappa \), \(\overline{\mathrm{Ric}}(\nu , \nu ) = n\kappa \) (Proposition 40.8.4), so \(Q(\phi ) = \int _K(|\nabla \phi |^2 - (|S|^2 + n\kappa )\phi ^2)\, dA\).

The Jacobi operator and the notations of the literature. Green’s formula (Proposition 40.5.4, on a compact domain with smooth boundary containing \(x(K)\)) gives \(\int |\nabla \phi |^2\, dA = -\int \phi \, \Delta \phi \, dA\) for compactly supported \(\phi \), where \(\Delta = \operatorname {div}\operatorname {grad}\) is the Laplacian of \(M\) with its induced metric (the Laplace–Beltrami operator). Hence

\[ Q(\phi ) = -\int _K\bigl(\Delta \phi + |S|^2\phi + \overline{\mathrm{Ric}}(\nu , \nu )\phi \bigr)\phi \, dA = -\int _K\phi \, L\phi \, dA, \qquad L = \Delta + |S|^2 + \overline{\mathrm{Ric}}(\nu , \nu ), \]

the Jacobi operator, or stability operator, of \(M\). A minimal hypersurface is stable if \(Q(\phi ) \geq 0\) for all compactly supported \(\phi \). Both curvature terms make stability harder; positive Ricci curvature of \(\overline{M}\) pushes towards instability, negative towards stability. The literature uses several equivalent conventions, and it helps to have a dictionary:

  1. The second fundamental form is often written \(A\), and \(|A|^2 = \sum _{i,j}h_{ij}h^{ij} = \sum _i\kappa _i^2\) is our \(|S|^2\) (the norm of \(h\) and of \(S\) agree, as \(S\) is \(h\) with an index raised). The variation function is often written \(u\), the Laplacian \(\Delta _M\), and the area element \(d\mu \) or \(d\omega \).

  2. Many authors define the Jacobi operator with the opposite sign, \(L = -\Delta _M - |A|^2 - \overline{\mathrm{Ric}}(\nu , \nu )\), so that \(Q(u) = \int _Mu\, Lu\) and stability means that \(L\) is a nonnegative operator (its eigenvalues on compactly supported functions are \(\geq 0\)). This is the same quadratic form as ours.

  3. Some authors use the geometer’s Laplacian \(\Delta = -\operatorname {div}\operatorname {grad}\), which is a nonnegative operator; then \(L\) is written \(\Delta - |A|^2 - \overline{\mathrm{Ric}}(\nu , \nu )\).

To translate a formula, check it on the hyperplane in \(\mathbb {R}^{n+1}\): there \(Q(u) = \int |\nabla u|^2 \geq 0\).

Curves. For \(n = 1\), \(M\) is a curve in a surface \(\overline{M}\), \(A\) is its length, and \(S\) is its geodesic curvature, so \(H = 0\) means that \(M\) is a geodesic and then \(S = 0\). Since \(\overline{\mathrm{Ric}}(\nu , \nu ) = \overline{K}\) for a surface (Proposition 40.8.4), the formula becomes \(\int (\phi ^{\prime 2} - K\phi ^2)\, dt\) for a unit speed geodesic \(\gamma \). This is the index form \(I(V, V)\) of Proposition 40.10.4 for \(V = \phi \nu \): the unit normal \(\nu \) is parallel along \(\gamma \) (its derivative is orthogonal to \(\nu \) and, as \(\langle D_t\nu , \dot\gamma \rangle = -\langle \nu , D_t\dot\gamma \rangle = 0\), to \(\dot\gamma \)), so \(D_tV = \phi ^{\prime }\nu \) and \(R(V, \dot\gamma , \dot\gamma , V) = K\phi ^2\). So the second variation of area generalises the second variation of geodesics, and the three terms above correspond to those of Remark 40.10.5.

02WO Example 40.13.12

The equator of a sphere. Let \(M = S^n = \{ x_{n+2} = 0\} \) be the equator of the unit sphere \(\overline{M} = S^{n+1} \subseteq \mathbb {R}^{n+2}\), with the constant unit normal \(\nu = e_{n+2}\). The derivative of the constant field \(\nu \) in \(\mathbb {R}^{n+2}\) is zero, so its tangential projection \(\overline{\nabla }_X\nu \) vanishes (Corollary 40.11.3): \(S = 0\), the equator is totally geodesic and in particular minimal. As \(\overline{\mathrm{Ric}} = n\overline{g}\) (Proposition 40.8.4), the proposition with \(\phi = 1\) (the computation is pointwise, so it applies on all of \(S^n\)) predicts \(A^{\prime \prime }(0) = -n\operatorname {vol}(S^n)\) for the variation by normal geodesics. Directly: the normal geodesic from \(p\) is the great circle \(s \mapsto \cos s\, p + \sin s\, e_{n+2}\), so \(F_s(p) = \cos s\, p + \sin s\, e_{n+2}\) maps \(S^n\) onto the parallel sphere \(\{ x_{n+2} = \sin s\} \), with \(dF_s = \cos s \cdot \mathrm{id}\) on tangent vectors. So \(g(s) = \cos ^2s\, g\), \(A(s) = \cos ^ns\operatorname {vol}(S^n)\), and \(A^{\prime \prime }(0) = -n\operatorname {vol}(S^n)\), as predicted. The equator is a minimal hypersurface, but it is a maximum of the area among the parallel spheres, not a minimum.

02WP Corollary 40.13.13

Let \(\overline{M}\) be a Riemannian manifold with \(\overline{\mathrm{Ric}}(v, v) {\gt} 0\) for all \(v \neq 0\), and let \(M \subseteq \overline{M}\) be a compact minimal hypersurface without boundary with a unit normal field \(\nu \). Then pushing \(M\) along its normal geodesics, \(F_s(p) = \exp _p(s\nu (p))\), decreases its area to second order: \(M\) is not stable.

Proof â–¼

Cover \(M\) by finitely many parametrisations and take a subordinate partition of unity \((\rho _k)\) (Proposition 33.8.2). The area of \(F_s(M)\), computed with the metric \(F_s^*\overline{g}\), is \(A(s) = \sum _k\int \rho _k\sqrt{\det g(s)}\, du\), each term in the chart of \(\rho _k\). Steps 3 to 6 of the proof of Proposition 40.13.10 compute \((\sqrt{\det g})^{\prime \prime }(0)\) pointwise, with \(\phi = 1\) and \(Z = 0\) (the curves are geodesics), as \(-(|S|^2 + \overline{\mathrm{Ric}}(\nu , \nu ))\sqrt{\det g}\). Since \(\sum _k\rho _k = 1\),

\[ A^{\prime \prime }(0) = -\int _M\bigl(|S|^2 + \overline{\mathrm{Ric}}(\nu , \nu )\bigr)\, dA {\lt} 0 . \]

For example, in the round sphere \(S^{n+1}\) every compact minimal hypersurface with a unit normal is unstable, as the equator of Example 40.13.12 illustrates. The proof uses a global unit normal field (that is, \(M\) is two-sided), since it pushes every point of \(M\) to the same side. For a one-sided hypersurface, such as the image of the equator \(S^2 \subseteq S^3\) in \(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^3\), which is a totally geodesic copy of \(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^2\), the variation with \(\phi = 1\) is not defined and the argument does not apply.
