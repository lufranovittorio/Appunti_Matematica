28.11 Change of variables013S

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

013T Lemma 28.11.1

For \(T \in \mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb {R})\) and Borel \(E \subseteq \mathbb {R}^n\), \(\lambda (T(E)) = |\det T|\, \lambda (E)\).

Proof â–¼

Both sides are multiplicative in \(T\), and every invertible matrix is a product of elementary matrices (by Gaussian elimination): permutations of coordinates, scalings of one coordinate by \(c \neq 0\), and shears \(x_i \mapsto x_i + cx_j\) (\(i \neq j\)). Permutations and scalings map boxes to boxes, multiplying volumes by \(|\det T|\), so they multiply outer measure by \(|\det T|\). A shear preserves each line parallel to the \(i\)-th axis and acts on it by a translation; by Theorem 28.10.3 and Remark 28.10.5, integrating the one-dimensional measures of these sections shows \(\lambda (T(E)) = \lambda (E)\), and \(\det T = 1\).

013ULean Theorem 28.11.2 Change of variables

Let \(U, V \subseteq \mathbb {R}^n\) be open and \(\phi \colon U \to V\) a \(C^1\)-diffeomorphism. For every measurable \(f \colon V \to [0, \infty ]\), and every \(f \in L^1(V)\),

\[ \int _Vf\, d\lambda = \int _Uf(\phi (x))\, |\det D\phi (x)|\, d\lambda (x). \]
Proof â–¼

We use the norm \(\| x\| _\infty \) and the corresponding operator norm; the mean value inequality (Proposition 27.10.3(4)) holds for it with the same proof. Cubes are balls \(Q = \{ x : \| x - a\| _\infty \leq h\} \).

Step 1. Let \(Q \subseteq U\) be a cube with centre \(a\) and \(T \in \mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb {R})\), and let \(M = \sup _{y \in Q}\| T^{-1}D\phi (y)\| \). By the mean value inequality applied to \(T^{-1}\phi \), \(\phi (Q) \subseteq \phi (a) + T(\{ \| z\| _\infty \leq Mh\} )\), so \(\lambda (\phi (Q)) \leq |\det T|M^n\lambda (Q)\) by Lemma 28.11.1.

Step 2: \(\lambda (\phi (Q)) \leq \int _Q|\det D\phi |\) for cubes \(Q \subseteq U\). Let \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\). Since \(D\phi \) is uniformly continuous on \(Q\) and \(\| D\phi ^{-1}\| \) is bounded there, we can divide \(Q\) into subcubes \(Q_j\) with centres \(x_j\) such that \(\| D\phi (x_j)^{-1}D\phi (y)\| \leq 1 + \varepsilon \) for \(y \in Q_j\), and \(|\det D\phi |\) varies by less than \(\varepsilon \) on each \(Q_j\). Step 1 with \(T = D\phi (x_j)\) gives \(\lambda (\phi (Q)) \leq \sum _j\lambda (\phi (Q_j)) \leq (1 + \varepsilon )^n\sum _j|\det D\phi (x_j)|\lambda (Q_j) \leq (1 + \varepsilon )^n(\int _Q|\det D\phi | + \varepsilon \lambda (Q))\).

Step 3: \(\lambda (\phi (E)) \leq \int _E|\det D\phi |\) for measurable \(E \subseteq U\). An open \(W \subseteq U\) is a countable union of cubes with disjoint interiors (dyadic cubes), whose boundaries are null, so the inequality holds for \(W\). Let \(E \subseteq K\) with \(K \subseteq U\) compact, and \(C = \sup \) of \(|\det D\phi |\) on a compact neighbourhood \(N \subseteq U\) of \(K\). By regularity choose open \(W\) with \(E \subseteq W \subseteq N\) and \(\lambda (W \setminus E) {\lt} \delta \); then \(\lambda (\phi (E)) \leq \lambda (\phi (W)) \leq \int _E|\det D\phi | + C\delta \). This shows that \(\phi \) maps null sets to null sets, hence Lebesgue measurable sets to Lebesgue measurable sets (a measurable set is a Borel set up to a null set), and gives the inequality for \(E\) in a compact subset; exhaust \(U\) by compact sets for general \(E\).

Step 4. By Step 3, \(\int _V\mathbf{1}_{\phi (E)} \leq \int _U\mathbf{1}_{\phi (E)} \circ \phi \, |\det D\phi |\); by linearity and monotone convergence, \(\int _Vf \leq \int _U(f \circ \phi )|\det D\phi |\) for all measurable \(f \geq 0\). Applying this to \(\phi ^{-1}\) and the function \((f \circ \phi )|\det D\phi |\) on \(U\), and using \(D\phi (\phi ^{-1}(y))D(\phi ^{-1})(y) = 1\), gives the reverse inequality. The \(L^1\) case follows by linearity.

013V Example 28.11.3

With polar coordinates \((r, \theta ) \mapsto (r\cos \theta , r\sin \theta )\), a diffeomorphism \((0, \infty ) \times (0, 2\pi ) \to \mathbb {R}^2\) minus a closed half-line (a null set), with Jacobian determinant \(r\), Tonelli’s theorem gives \((\int _\mathbb {R}e^{-x^2}dx)^2 = \int _{\mathbb {R}^2}e^{-\| x\| ^2} = \int _0^{2\pi }\int _0^\infty e^{-r^2}r\, dr\, d\theta = \pi \). So \(\int _\mathbb {R}e^{-x^2}\, dx = \sqrt{\pi }\).

Fubini’s theorem reduces integration by parts in several variables to one variable. When one of the functions has compact support there is no boundary term.

02P4 Proposition 28.11.4 Integration by parts in \(\mathbb {R}^n\)

Let \(f, g \colon \mathbb {R}^n \to \mathbb {C}\) be continuously differentiable, and suppose that one of them has compact support. Then for each \(i\)

\[ \int _{\mathbb {R}^n}(\partial _if)\, g\, d\lambda = -\int _{\mathbb {R}^n}f\, \partial _ig\, d\lambda . \]

Consequently, for a continuously differentiable vector field \(F\) and a continuously differentiable function \(g\), one of them compactly supported, \(\int \operatorname {div}F\, g\, d\lambda = -\int \langle F, \nabla g\rangle \, d\lambda \), where \(\operatorname {div}F = \sum _i\partial _iF^i\) and \(\nabla g = (\partial _1g, \ldots , \partial _ng)\); and for \(f\) of class \(C^2\), \(\int (\sum _i\partial _i^2f)\, g\, d\lambda = -\int \langle \nabla f, \nabla g\rangle \, d\lambda \).

Proof â–¼

The product \(h = fg\) is continuously differentiable with compact support, contained in a cube \([-R, R]^n\), and \(\partial _ih = (\partial _if)g + f\, \partial _ig\) by the product rule; all these functions are continuous with compact support, hence integrable. By Fubini’s theorem (Theorem 28.10.3) we may integrate first in the variable \(x_i\), the others being fixed. For fixed values of the other variables, \(t \mapsto h(\ldots , t, \ldots )\) is continuously differentiable and vanishes for \(|t| \geq R\), so by the fundamental theorem of calculus (Theorem 27.8.3) \(\int _{-R}^R\partial _ih\, dx_i = h(\ldots , R, \ldots ) - h(\ldots , -R, \ldots ) = 0\). Hence \(\int \partial _ih\, d\lambda = 0\), which is the formula. For the vector field, apply it to \(F^i\) and \(g\) and sum over \(i\); for the last formula take \(F = \nabla f\).

The formula says that the operator \(\partial _i\) is anti-self-adjoint for the \(L^2\) inner product on compactly supported functions: \(\langle \partial _if, \bar g\rangle = -\langle f, \partial _i\bar g\rangle \). It is the reason why the derivative of a distribution is defined by \((\partial _iT)(\varphi ) = -T(\partial _i\varphi )\) (Chapter 32), and, applied twice, it shows that \(-\sum _i\partial _i^2\) is a non-negative operator: \(\int (-\sum _i\partial _i^2f)\bar f\, d\lambda = \int |\nabla f|^2\, d\lambda \geq 0\) for compactly supported \(f\) of class \(C^2\).

28.11.1 Volumes: Cavalieri’s principle and solids of revolution03DS

Fubini’s theorem and the change of variables formula contain the classical recipes for computing volumes. We write \(\lambda ^n\) for Lebesgue measure on \(\mathbb {R}^n\) and call \(\lambda ^3(E)\) the volume and \(\lambda ^2(E)\) the area of a set. The first recipe slices a solid by parallel planes.

03DT Proposition 28.11.5 Cavalieri’s principle

Let \(E \subseteq \mathbb {R}^n = \mathbb {R}^{n-1} \times \mathbb {R}\) be a Borel set, and for \(t \in \mathbb {R}\) let \(E_t = \{ y \in \mathbb {R}^{n-1} : (y, t) \in E\} \) be its slice at height \(t\). Then \(t \mapsto \lambda ^{n-1}(E_t)\) is measurable and

\[ \lambda ^n(E) = \int _\mathbb {R}\lambda ^{n-1}(E_t)\, dt . \]

In particular, two Borel sets whose slices at every height have the same \((n-1)\)-dimensional measure have the same volume.

Proof â–¼

Apply Tonelli’s theorem (Theorem 28.10.3(1)) to the indicator function \(\mathbf{1}_E\) on \(\mathbb {R}^{n-1} \times \mathbb {R}\), with \(\lambda ^n = \lambda ^{n-1} \times \lambda ^1\) on Borel sets (Remark 28.10.5), integrating first over \(y\): \(\int \mathbf{1}_E(y, t)\, d\lambda ^{n-1}(y) = \lambda ^{n-1}(E_t)\).

The principle is named after Bonaventura Cavalieri (1635), who stated it for plane figures and solids, but Archimedes had already used it, and Zu Chongzhi and his son Zu Geng used it in China in the fifth century to find the volume of the ball.

03DU Example 28.11.6

  1. Cones. Let \(B \subseteq \mathbb {R}^2\) be a Borel set of finite area \(A\) and \(h {\gt} 0\), and let \(C\) be the cone over \(B\) with apex \((0, 0, h)\), the union of the segments from the points of \(B \times \{ 0\} \) to the apex. Its slice at height \(t \in [0, h)\) is the scaled copy \((1 - t/h)B\), of area \((1 - t/h)^2A\) by Lemma 28.11.1. So \(\lambda ^3(C) = A\int _0^h(1 - t/h)^2\, dt = \frac13Ah\), whatever the shape of the base: a third of the prism with the same base and height.

  2. Archimedes’ ball. The slice of the half-ball \(\{ |x| \leq R, x_3 \geq 0\} \) at height \(t \in [0, R]\) is a disc of area \(\pi (R^2 - t^2)\). The slice of the cylinder \(\{ x_1^2 + x_2^2 \leq R^2, 0 \leq x_3 \leq R\} \) minus the cone \(\{ \sqrt{x_1^2 + x_2^2} {\lt} x_3\} \) is an annulus of area \(\pi R^2 - \pi t^2\). The slices have equal areas, so the half-ball has the volume of the cylinder minus the cone, \(\pi R^3 - \frac13\pi R^3 = \frac23\pi R^3\), and the ball has volume \(\frac43\pi R^3\). This is Archimedes’ argument, which he regarded as his finest: the ball is two thirds of the circumscribed cylinder.

A solid of revolution is obtained by rotating a plane region around an axis. We take the \(z\)-axis in \(\mathbb {R}^3\) as axis, and describe the region in the half-plane \(H = \{ (r, z) : r {\gt} 0\} \) of distances from the axis and heights. For a Borel set \(R \subseteq H\) let

\[ \operatorname {Rev}(R) = \bigl\{ (x, y, z) \in \mathbb {R}^3 : \bigl(\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}, z\bigr) \in R\bigr\} \]

be the solid swept out by \(R\), a Borel set since it is the preimage of \(R\) under a continuous map on \(\mathbb {R}^3\) minus the axis.

03DV Proposition 28.11.7 Volumes of solids of revolution

For every Borel set \(R \subseteq H\),

\[ \lambda ^3(\operatorname {Rev}(R)) = 2\pi \int _Rr\, d\lambda ^2(r, z) . \]

In particular:

  1. (Discs) if \(R = \{ (r, z) : a \leq z \leq b,\ 0 {\lt} r \leq f(z)\} \) for a continuous \(f \geq 0\) on \([a, b]\), then \(\lambda ^3(\operatorname {Rev}(R)) = \pi \int _a^bf(z)^2\, dz\);

  2. (Shells) if \(R = \{ (r, z) : a \leq r \leq b,\ 0 \leq z \leq g(r)\} \) for \(0 {\lt} a {\lt} b\) and a continuous \(g \geq 0\) on \([a, b]\), then \(\lambda ^3(\operatorname {Rev}(R)) = 2\pi \int _a^brg(r)\, dr\).

Proof â–¼

Use cylindrical coordinates \(\phi (r, \theta , z) = (r\cos \theta , r\sin \theta , z)\), a \(C^1\)-diffeomorphism from \(U = (0, \infty ) \times (0, 2\pi ) \times \mathbb {R}\) onto \(\mathbb {R}^3\) minus the closed half-plane \(\{ y = 0, x \geq 0\} \), which is a null set. Its Jacobian determinant is

\[ \det \begin{pmatrix} \cos \theta & -r\sin \theta & 0 \\ \sin \theta & r\cos \theta & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = r . \]

Since \(\mathbf{1}_{\operatorname {Rev}(R)}(\phi (r, \theta , z)) = \mathbf{1}_R(r, z)\), the change of variables formula (Theorem 28.11.2) and Tonelli’s theorem give \(\lambda ^3(\operatorname {Rev}(R)) = \int _U\mathbf{1}_R(r, z)\, r\, d\lambda ^3 = \int _0^{2\pi }d\theta \int _Rr\, d\lambda ^2 = 2\pi \int _Rr\, d\lambda ^2\). For (1), integrate first over \(r\): \(\int _0^{f(z)}r\, dr = \frac12f(z)^2\). For (2), integrate first over \(z\): \(\int _0^{g(r)}dz = g(r)\).

The two special cases are the “disc method” and the “shell method” of calculus courses: (1) cuts the solid by planes perpendicular to the axis, which meet it in discs of area \(\pi f(z)^2\), and is also a case of Cavalieri’s principle; (2) cuts it into thin cylindrical shells of radius \(r\), height \(g(r)\) and thickness \(dr\), of volume \(2\pi rg(r)\, dr\). For the ball, (1) with \(f(z) = \sqrt{R^2 - z^2}\) gives \(\pi \int _{-R}^R(R^2 - z^2)\, dz = \frac43\pi R^3\) once more.

The integral \(\int _Rr\, d\lambda ^2\) has a mechanical meaning. The centroid of a Borel set \(R \subseteq \mathbb {R}^2\) of finite positive area is the point \((\bar r, \bar z)\) with \(\bar r = \lambda ^2(R)^{-1}\int _Rr\, d\lambda ^2\) and \(\bar z = \lambda ^2(R)^{-1}\int _Rz\, d\lambda ^2\), its centre of mass for a uniform density. So the proposition can be read as follows.

03DW Corollary 28.11.8 Pappus–Guldin theorem for volumes

If \(R \subseteq H\) has finite positive area and centroid at distance \(\bar r\) from the axis, then

\[ \lambda ^3(\operatorname {Rev}(R)) = 2\pi \bar r \cdot \lambda ^2(R): \]

the volume of a solid of revolution is the area of the region times the length of the circle described by its centroid.

Proof â–¼

\(2\pi \int _Rr\, d\lambda ^2 = 2\pi \bar r\lambda ^2(R)\) by the definition of \(\bar r\).

The theorem was stated by Pappus of Alexandria around 320 and rediscovered by Paul Guldin in 1640; in Italian it is the teorema di Pappo–Guldino. It turns volume computations into centroid computations, and conversely.

03DX Example 28.11.9

  1. The torus. Rotating the disc of radius \(a\) centred at \((c, 0)\), with \(0 {\lt} a {\lt} c\), gives a solid torus. The centroid is the centre, by symmetry, so the volume is \(2\pi c \cdot \pi a^2 = 2\pi ^2ca^2\): the volume of a cylinder of radius \(a\) whose length is the central circle of the torus. Computing it with discs requires the integral \(\int _{-a}^a\pi \bigl((c + \sqrt{a^2 - z^2})^2 - (c - \sqrt{a^2 - z^2})^2\bigr)dz = 4\pi c\int _{-a}^a\sqrt{a^2 - z^2}\, dz\), which is the same thing, \(4\pi c \cdot \frac12\pi a^2\).

  2. The centroid of a half-disc. Conversely, rotating the half-disc \(\{ r^2 + z^2 \leq a^2, r {\gt} 0\} \) around the \(z\)-axis gives the ball of radius \(a\), so \(\frac43\pi a^3 = 2\pi \bar r \cdot \frac12\pi a^2\), and the centroid of a half-disc lies at distance \(\bar r = \frac{4a}{3\pi }\) from its diameter.
