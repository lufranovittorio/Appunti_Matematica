39.13 Isometries, Killing fields and space forms02JO

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

The symmetries of a Riemannian manifold are its isometries, and the infinitesimal symmetries are the vector fields whose flows consist of isometries, the Killing fields. This section shows that both are rigid: an isometry is determined by its value and its differential at one point, a Killing field by its value and its covariant derivative at one point, and so the Killing fields form a Lie algebra of dimension at most \(n(n + 1)/2\). The spaces with the most symmetry are those of constant curvature, and the theorem of Killing and Hopf classifies them: a complete simply connected manifold of constant curvature is Euclidean space, a round sphere or hyperbolic space, and every complete manifold of constant curvature is a quotient of one of these. At the end we state two rigidity theorems of a more difficult kind, Cheng’s maximal diameter theorem and the sphere theorem. Throughout, \(M\) has dimension \(n\).

A smooth map \(F \colon (N, h) \to (M, g)\) between Riemannian manifolds of the same dimension is a local isometry if \(F^*g = h\), that is, if every \(d_xF\) is a linear isometry. By the inverse function theorem (Theorem 32.4.14) it is then a local diffeomorphism, and it restricts to isometries between small open sets.

02JP Lemma 39.13.1

Let \(F \colon N \to M\) be a local isometry.

  1. \(F\) maps geodesics to geodesics: if \(v \in T_xN\) is in the domain of \(\exp _x\), then \(d_xF(v)\) is in the domain of \(\exp _{F(x)}\) and \(F(\exp _x(v)) = \exp _{F(x)}(d_xF(v))\).

  2. If \(N\) is connected and \(F^{\prime } \colon N \to M\) is another local isometry with \(F^{\prime }(x_0) = F(x_0)\) and \(d_{x_0}F^{\prime } = d_{x_0}F\) at one point \(x_0\), then \(F^{\prime } = F\).

Proof â–¼

(1) Let \(x \in N\), and choose an open \(U \ni x\) that \(F\) maps diffeomorphically onto an open \(V\), and a chart \(y\) on \(V\). Then \(y \circ F\) is a chart on \(U\), and since \(F^*g = h\), the coefficients of \(h\) in the chart \(y \circ F\) are the same functions as those of \(g\) in the chart \(y\). So the Christoffel symbols (Theorem 39.2.6) and the geodesic equations (Definition 39.2.7) are the same, and a curve \(c\) in \(U\) is a geodesic if and only if \(F \circ c\) is one. Being a geodesic is a local condition, so \(F \circ \gamma \) is a geodesic whenever \(\gamma \) is. For \(v\) in the domain of \(\exp _x\), the curve \(F \circ \gamma _v\) is a geodesic defined on \([0, 1]\) with initial velocity \(d_xF(v)\); by uniqueness it is the geodesic \(\gamma _{d_xF(v)}\), which is therefore defined on \([0, 1]\), and evaluating at \(t = 1\) gives the formula.

(2) Let \(A = \{ x \in N : F^{\prime }(x) = F(x),\ d_xF^{\prime } = d_xF\} \), which contains \(x_0\). It is closed, as \(F\), \(F^{\prime }\) and their differentials are continuous (in charts, the Jacobian matrices are continuous). It is open: if \(x \in A\) and \(\exp _x\) maps a ball \(B(0, \varepsilon )\) diffeomorphically onto a neighbourhood \(W\) of \(x\) (Proposition 39.5.1), then by (1), \(F(\exp _x v) = \exp _{F(x)}(d_xFv) = F^{\prime }(\exp _x v)\) for \(|v| {\lt} \varepsilon \), so \(F = F^{\prime }\) on \(W\), and hence also \(dF = dF^{\prime }\) on \(W\). As \(N\) is connected, \(A = N\).

For example, for a geodesic \(\gamma \) and an isometry \(F\), \(F \circ \gamma \) is the geodesic with initial velocity \(dF(\dot\gamma (0))\); this is the argument of Exercise 39.14.5, which finds the geodesics of the sphere as fixed sets of reflections.

02JQ Definition 39.13.2

A vector field \(X\) on \(M\) with local flow \(\Phi \) (Theorem 32.9.2) is a Killing field if every \(\Phi _t\) is an isometry from its domain onto its image: \(\langle d\Phi _t(v), d\Phi _t(w)\rangle = \langle v, w\rangle \) whenever \(\Phi _t\) is defined at the base point of \(v\) and \(w\). For vector fields \(X\), \(Y\), \(Z\) we put

\[ (L_Xg)(Y, Z) = X\langle Y, Z\rangle - \langle [X, Y], Z\rangle - \langle Y, [X, Z]\rangle . \]
02JR Proposition 39.13.3

The expression \(L_Xg\) is a symmetric tensor, and \((L_Xg)_p(v, w) = \frac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0}\langle d_p\Phi _t(v), d_p\Phi _t(w)\rangle \) for \(v, w \in T_pM\) (the Lie derivative of \(g\)). Moreover \((L_Xg)(Y, Z) = \langle \nabla _YX, Z\rangle + \langle Y, \nabla _ZX\rangle \). Consequently the following are equivalent:

  1. \(X\) is a Killing field;

  2. \(X\langle Y, Z\rangle = \langle [X, Y], Z\rangle + \langle Y, [X, Z]\rangle \) for all vector fields \(Y\), \(Z\);

  3. for every \(p\), the endomorphism \(v \mapsto \nabla _vX\) of \(T_pM\) is skew-adjoint: \(\langle \nabla _vX, w\rangle + \langle v, \nabla _wX\rangle = 0\).

In coordinates, (3) reads \(\nabla _iX_j + \nabla _jX_i = 0\), where \(\nabla _iX_j = \langle \nabla _{\partial _i}X, \partial _j\rangle \) (Killing’s equation).

Proof â–¼

Since \(\nabla \) is metric and torsion-free, \(X\langle Y, Z\rangle = \langle \nabla _XY, Z\rangle + \langle Y, \nabla _XZ\rangle \) and \([X, Y] = \nabla _XY - \nabla _YX\); substituting gives \((L_Xg)(Y, Z) = \langle \nabla _YX, Z\rangle + \langle Y, \nabla _ZX\rangle \). The right side depends only on the values of \(Y\) and \(Z\) at each point and is symmetric, so \(L_Xg\) is a symmetric tensor.

Fix \(p\) and vector fields \(Y\), \(Z\) near \(p\). Let \(V(t) = d\Phi _{-t}(Y_{\Phi _t(p)})\) and \(W(t) = d\Phi _{-t}(Z_{\Phi _t(p)})\), curves in \(T_pM\) with \(V(0) = Y_p\), \(W(0) = Z_p\), and by Proposition 32.9.7, \(V^{\prime }(0) = [X, Y]_p\) and \(W^{\prime }(0) = [X, Z]_p\). For \(a, b \in T_pM\) put \(G(t, a, b) = \langle d_p\Phi _t(a), d_p\Phi _t(b)\rangle \), smooth in \(t\) and bilinear in \((a, b)\). Since \(d\Phi _t(V(t)) = Y_{\Phi _t(p)}\),

\[ \langle Y, Z\rangle (\Phi _t(p)) = G(t, V(t), W(t)), \]

and differentiating at \(t = 0\) (the derivative of \(t \mapsto \Phi _t(p)\) is \(X_p\)), \(X_p\langle Y, Z\rangle = \partial _tG(0, Y_p, Z_p) + \langle [X, Y]_p, Z_p\rangle + \langle Y_p, [X, Z]_p\rangle \). So \((L_Xg)_p(Y_p, Z_p) = \partial _tG(0, Y_p, Z_p)\), and every pair of tangent vectors at \(p\) is of the form \((Y_p, Z_p)\).

(1) \(\Rightarrow \) (2): if \(X\) is Killing, \(G(t, a, b) = \langle a, b\rangle \) does not depend on \(t\), so \(L_Xg = 0\). (2) \(\Rightarrow \) (1): let \(v, w \in T_pM\) and \(f(t) = G(t, v, w)\), on the interval of \(t\) for which \(\Phi _t(p)\) is defined. For \(t_0\) in it, \(\Phi _{t_0 + s} = \Phi _s \circ \Phi _{t_0}\) near \(p\), so \(f(t_0 + s) = \langle d\Phi _s(v^{\prime }), d\Phi _s(w^{\prime })\rangle \) with \(v^{\prime } = d_p\Phi _{t_0}(v)\) and \(w^{\prime } = d_p\Phi _{t_0}(w)\), and \(f^{\prime }(t_0) = (L_Xg)(v^{\prime }, w^{\prime }) = 0\). So \(f\) is constant, equal to \(f(0) = \langle v, w\rangle \). (2) \(\Leftrightarrow \) (3) is the formula \((L_Xg)(Y, Z) = \langle \nabla _YX, Z\rangle + \langle Y, \nabla _ZX\rangle \).

02JS Example 39.13.4

  1. Euclidean space. On \(\mathbb {R}^n\), \(\nabla _vX = DX(x)v\), so \(X\) is a Killing field if and only if the matrix \(DX(x)\) is skew-symmetric for every \(x\). This holds for \(X(x) = Ax + b\) with \(A^T = -A\), whose flow \(\Phi _t(x) = e^{tA}x + \int _0^te^{sA}b\, ds\) consists of rigid motions (\(e^{tA}\) is orthogonal, since \((e^{tA})^Te^{tA} = e^{-tA}e^{tA} = I\)). Conversely, if \(\partial _iX^j + \partial _jX^i = 0\) everywhere, then \(T_{kij} = \partial _k\partial _iX^j\) is symmetric in \(k, i\) (Theorem 26.10.5) and antisymmetric in \(i, j\), and a tensor with these two properties vanishes: \(T_{kij} = -T_{kji} = -T_{jki} = T_{jik} = T_{ijk} = -T_{ikj} = -T_{kij}\). So \(DX\) is constant and \(X(x) = Ax + b\).

  2. Spheres. For \(A \in \mathfrak {so}(n + 1)\) the field \(X(x) = Ax\) is tangent to \(S^n\), as \(\langle Ax, x\rangle = 0\), and its flow \(e^{tA} \in \mathrm{SO}(n + 1)\) restricts to isometries of \(S^n\); so \(X\) is a Killing field on \(S^n\). For \(n = 2\) and \(A = L_a\) (Example 33.2.10) it is the field \(x \mapsto a \times x\) of the rotations about the axis \(a\).

  3. Lie groups. On a Lie group with a bi-invariant metric (Example 39.2.3) the left-invariant fields are Killing fields: the flow of \(X\) is \(\Phi _t(g) = g\exp (tX)\) (Theorem 33.3.3, translated by \(L_g\)), which is right multiplication by \(\exp (tX)\), an isometry.

02JT Proposition 39.13.5

Let \(X\) be a Killing field and \(\gamma \colon I \to M\) a geodesic. Then \(X \circ \gamma \) is a Jacobi field along \(\gamma \), with \(D_t(X \circ \gamma ) = \nabla _{\dot\gamma }X\).

Proof â–¼

Let \([a, b] \subseteq I\). The domain of the flow is open and contains \(\{ 0\} \times M\) (Theorem 32.9.2), so by compactness of \(\gamma ([a, b])\) there is \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\) such that \(\Phi _s\) is defined on a neighbourhood of \(\gamma ([a, b])\) for \(|s| {\lt} \varepsilon \). Each \(\Phi _s\) is an isometry onto its image, so by Lemma 39.13.1(1), \(f(s, t) = \Phi _s(\gamma (t))\) is a family of geodesics \(t \mapsto f(s, t)\). By Proposition 39.7.6(1), \(\partial _sf(0, t) = X(\gamma (t))\) is a Jacobi field on \([a, b]\); as \([a, b]\) is arbitrary, it is one on \(I\). The formula for \(D_t\) is Proposition 39.4.1.

02JU Theorem 39.13.6

Let \(M\) be connected, and \(p \in M\).

  1. A Killing field \(X\) with \(X_p = 0\) and \(\nabla _vX = 0\) for all \(v \in T_pM\) vanishes identically.

  2. The Killing fields form a Lie subalgebra \(\mathfrak {iso}(M, g)\) of the Lie algebra \(\mathfrak {X}(M)\) of vector fields (Proposition 32.9.7), and the linear map \(X \mapsto (X_p, \nabla X_p)\), where \(\nabla X_p\) is the endomorphism \(v \mapsto \nabla _vX\) of \(T_pM\), is injective with values in \(T_pM \oplus \mathfrak {so}(T_pM)\). Hence \(\dim \mathfrak {iso}(M, g) \leq n + \frac{n(n-1)}{2} = \frac{n(n + 1)}{2}\).

  3. The bound is attained by \(\mathbb {R}^n\) and by \(S^n\), whose Killing fields are exactly those of Example 39.13.4.

Proof â–¼

(1) Let \(Z\) be the set of points \(q\) with \(X_q = 0\) and \(\nabla X_q = 0\); it is closed and contains \(p\). If \(q \in Z\) and \(\exp _q\) maps \(B(0, \varepsilon )\) diffeomorphically onto \(W\), then for \(|v| {\lt} \varepsilon \), \(J(t) = X(\exp _q(tv))\) is a Jacobi field (Proposition 39.13.5) with \(J(0) = X_q = 0\) and \(D_tJ(0) = \nabla _vX = 0\), hence \(J = 0\) and \(X(\exp _qv) = J(1) = 0\). So \(X = 0\) on \(W\), hence also \(\nabla X = 0\) on \(W\), and \(Z\) is open. As \(M\) is connected, \(Z = M\).

(2) The map is linear, and it takes values in \(T_pM \oplus \mathfrak {so}(T_pM)\) by Proposition 39.13.3(3); by (1) it is injective, which gives the dimension bound. Killing fields form a vector space, since \(L_Xg\) is linear in \(X\). For the bracket, let \(X\) and \(W\) satisfy Proposition 39.13.3(2). Then for all \(Y\), \(Z\),

\[ \begin{aligned} XW\langle Y, Z\rangle & = X\langle [W, Y], Z\rangle + X\langle Y, [W, Z]\rangle \\ & = \langle [X, [W, Y]], Z\rangle + \langle [W, Y], [X, Z]\rangle + \langle [X, Y], [W, Z]\rangle + \langle Y, [X, [W, Z]]\rangle . \end{aligned} \]

Exchanging \(X\) and \(W\) and subtracting, the two middle terms cancel, and by the Jacobi identity \([X, [W, Y]] - [W, [X, Y]] = [[X, W], Y]\), so \([X, W]\langle Y, Z\rangle = \langle [[X, W], Y], Z\rangle + \langle Y, [[X, W], Z]\rangle \): \([X, W]\) is a Killing field.

(3) On \(\mathbb {R}^n\) the fields \(Ax + b\) with \(A\) skew form a space of dimension \(n(n - 1)/2 + n\), and by Example 39.13.4(1) they are all the Killing fields. On \(S^n\) the fields \(x \mapsto Ax\), \(A \in \mathfrak {so}(n + 1)\), form a space of dimension \(\dim \mathfrak {so}(n + 1) = n(n + 1)/2\): the map \(A \mapsto (x \mapsto Ax)\) is injective because \(S^n\) spans \(\mathbb {R}^{n+1}\). By (2) there are no other Killing fields on \(S^n\).

02JV Remark 39.13.7

The isometries of \(M\) form a group \(\mathrm{Isom}(M, g)\). By the theorem of Myers and Steenrod it is a Lie group, compact if \(M\) is compact, and its Lie algebra is identified with the Lie algebra of complete Killing fields (on a compact manifold every Killing field is complete); so \(\dim \mathrm{Isom}(M, g) \leq n(n + 1)/2\) [KN63, Chapter VI]. For instance \(\mathrm{Isom}(\mathbb {R}^n)\) is the group of rigid motions and \(\mathrm{Isom}(S^n) = \mathrm{O}(n + 1)\) (Corollary 39.13.11 below), both of the maximal dimension. A manifold whose isometry group has maximal dimension has constant curvature [KN63, Chapter VI].

Curvature restricts the Killing fields of a compact manifold, by an integration argument of Bochner.

02JW Theorem 39.13.8 Bochner

Let \(M\) be compact and oriented, and \(X\) a Killing field. Then, with \(|\nabla X|^2 = \sum _i|\nabla _{e_i}X|^2\) for an orthonormal basis \((e_i)\),

\[ \int _M\bigl(|\nabla X|^2 - \mathrm{Ric}(X, X)\bigr)\, \mathrm{vol}_g = 0 . \]

Hence if \(\mathrm{Ric} \leq 0\) everywhere, every Killing field is parallel (\(\nabla X = 0\)); and if \(\mathrm{Ric}\) is negative definite everywhere, every Killing field is zero, and \(\mathrm{Isom}(M, g)\) is finite.

Proof â–¼

Let \(f = \frac12|X|^2\) and \(p \in M\), and take normal coordinates at \(p\) (Proposition 39.5.1), with \(\partial _i = e_i\) orthonormal at \(p\). The \(i\)-th coordinate line \(\gamma _i(t) = \exp _p(te_i)\) is a unit speed geodesic, so \(\partial _i^2f(p) = (f \circ \gamma _i)^{\prime \prime }(0)\), and \(J = X \circ \gamma _i\) is a Jacobi field with \(D_tJ(0) = \nabla _{e_i}X\) (Proposition 39.13.5). Hence

\[ (f \circ \gamma _i)^{\prime \prime } = \frac{d}{dt}\langle D_tJ, J\rangle = |D_tJ|^2 + \langle D_t^2J, J\rangle = |D_tJ|^2 - \langle R(J, \dot\gamma _i)\dot\gamma _i, J\rangle , \]

which at \(t = 0\) is \(|\nabla _{e_i}X|^2 - R(X, e_i, e_i, X)\). Summing over \(i\), and using \(R(X, e_i, e_i, X) = R(e_i, X, X, e_i)\) (Proposition 39.7.2) and Definition 39.7.1, Proposition 39.12.3(5) gives

\[ \Delta f(p) = -\sum _i\partial _i^2f(p) = \mathrm{Ric}(X, X) - |\nabla X|^2 . \]

By Proposition 39.12.3(2), \(\int _M\Delta f\, \mathrm{vol}_g = (\Delta f, 1) = (f, \Delta 1) = 0\), which is the integral formula. If \(\mathrm{Ric} \leq 0\), the integrand \(|\nabla X|^2 - \mathrm{Ric}(X, X)\) is a non-negative continuous function with integral \(0\), so it vanishes: \(\nabla X = 0\) and \(\mathrm{Ric}(X, X) = 0\); if \(\mathrm{Ric}\) is negative definite, the latter forces \(X = 0\). Then the compact Lie group \(\mathrm{Isom}(M, g)\) has Lie algebra \(0\) (Remark 39.13.7), so it is discrete and compact, hence finite.

For the flat torus \(\mathbb {R}^n/\mathbb {Z}^n\) the Killing fields are therefore the parallel fields, the constant fields \(\sum _ic^i\partial _i\), whose flows are the translations; and a compact surface of constant curvature \(-1\) has only finitely many isometries.

We now turn to spaces of constant curvature. Recall that \(s_\kappa (t)\) is \(t\), \(\sin (\sqrt\kappa t)/\sqrt\kappa \) or \(\sinh (\sqrt{-\kappa }t)/\sqrt{-\kappa }\) for \(\kappa = 0\), \(\kappa {\gt} 0\) or \(\kappa {\lt} 0\), and that the proof of Theorem 39.10.8 shows: if \(M\) has constant sectional curvature \(\kappa \), \(v \neq 0\) lies in the domain of \(\exp _p\) and \(w = w_\parallel + w_\perp \) is the decomposition of \(w \in T_pM\) into a multiple of \(v\) and a vector orthogonal to \(v\), then

\[ |d_v\exp _p(w)|^2 = |w_\parallel |^2 + \Bigl(\frac{s_\kappa (|v|)}{|v|}\Bigr)^2|w_\perp |^2. \tag {$\dagger $} \]

Multiplying a metric by a constant \(c {\gt} 0\) does not change the Levi-Civita connection (the Koszul formula is homogeneous) or \(R(X, Y)Z\), and it multiplies the sectional curvatures by \(1/c\), since an orthonormal basis for \(cg\) is \((u, v)/\sqrt c\) for an orthonormal basis \((u, v)\) for \(g\).

02JX Definition 39.13.9

For \(n \geq 2\) and \(\kappa \in \mathbb {R}\) the model space \(M^n_\kappa \) is: Euclidean space \(\mathbb {R}^n\) if \(\kappa = 0\); the sphere \(S^n(r) = \{ x \in \mathbb {R}^{n+1} : |x| = r\} \) of radius \(r = 1/\sqrt\kappa \) with the induced metric if \(\kappa {\gt} 0\); and the hyperbolic space \(\{ x \in \mathbb {R}^n : x^n {\gt} 0\} \) with the metric \(|\kappa |^{-1}(dx_1^2 + \cdots + dx_n^2)/(x^n)^2\) if \(\kappa {\lt} 0\).

All three are complete, connected and simply connected with constant sectional curvature \(\kappa \). For \(\mathbb {R}^n\) this is clear. The sphere \(S^n(r)\) has curvature \(1/r^2 = \kappa \) (Example 39.9.12(1)), is complete, being compact (Theorem 39.5.7), and is simply connected for \(n \geq 2\) (Corollary 18.5.2). Hyperbolic space is simply connected, being a convex subset of \(\mathbb {R}^n\) (for \(n = 2\) its curvature is computed in Example 39.10.7(1)); that it is complete with curvature \(-1\) for \(\kappa = -1\) is computed in [dC92, Chapter 8], and the case of general \(\kappa {\lt} 0\) follows by scaling.

02JY Theorem 39.13.10 Killing–Hopf

Let \(M\) and \(N\) be complete, connected and simply connected Riemannian manifolds of the same dimension \(n \geq 2\), with the same constant sectional curvature \(\kappa \). For \(p \in M\), \(q \in N\) and a linear isometry \(I \colon T_pM \to T_qN\) there is a unique isometry \(F \colon M \to N\) with \(F(p) = q\) and \(d_pF = I\). In particular \(M\) is isometric to the model space \(M^n_\kappa \).

Proof â–¼

Uniqueness is Lemma 39.13.1(2). By Theorem 39.5.7, \(\exp _p\) and \(\exp _q\) are defined on the whole tangent spaces. The key observation is the following. Let \(U \subseteq T_pM\) be an open set on which \(\exp _p\) is a local diffeomorphism, and \(F = \exp _q \circ I \circ \psi \) for a smooth local inverse \(\psi \) of \(\exp _p|_U\). For \(x = \exp _p(v)\), \(v \in U\), and \(w \in T_pM\) the chain rule gives \(d_xF(d_v\exp _p(w)) = d_{Iv}\exp _q(Iw)\). The linear isometry \(I\) preserves lengths and maps \(w_\parallel \) and \(w_\perp \) to the components of \(Iw\) along and orthogonal to \(Iv\), so by \((\dagger )\) on \(M\) and on \(N\) the vectors \(d_v\exp _p(w)\) and \(d_{Iv}\exp _q(Iw)\) have the same length (for \(v = 0\) both differentials are the identity). As \(d_v\exp _p\) is onto \(T_xM\), \(d_xF\) preserves lengths, hence inner products: \(F\) is a local isometry.

The case \(\kappa \leq 0\). By Theorem 39.7.8, \(\exp _p\) and \(\exp _q\) are diffeomorphisms, so \(F = \exp _q \circ I \circ \exp _p^{-1} \colon M \to N\) is a diffeomorphism, a local isometry by the observation, hence an isometry; and \(F(p) = q\), \(d_pF = I\) as \(d_0\exp = \mathrm{id}\).

The case \(\kappa {\gt} 0\). Multiplying both metrics by \(\kappa \) we may assume \(\kappa = 1\). Let \(S = S^n\) be the unit sphere and \(o \in S\). We show: for every linear isometry \(J \colon T_oS \to T_pM\) there is an isometry \(G \colon S \to M\) with \(G(o) = p\) and \(d_oG = J\). Applying this to \(M\) with \(J\) and to \(N\) with \(I \circ J\) gives isometries \(G_M\) and \(G_N\), and \(F = G_N \circ G_M^{-1}\) has the required properties.

For a unit vector \(u \perp o\), the great circle \(c(t) = \cos t\, o + \sin t\, u\) has acceleration \(c^{\prime \prime } = -c\), which is normal to \(S\), so \(D_tc^{\prime } = (c^{\prime \prime })^\top = 0\) (Theorem 39.9.2): \(c\) is the geodesic with initial velocity \(u\). Hence \(\exp _o(v) = \cos |v|\, o + \sin |v|\, v/|v|\) for \(v \neq 0\). It maps \(B = B(0, \pi )\) bijectively onto \(S \setminus \{ -o\} \): every \(y \neq -o\) is uniquely \(\cos \theta \, o + \sin \theta \, u\) with \(\theta = \arccos \langle y, o\rangle \in [0, \pi )\) and, if \(\theta {\gt} 0\), a unit vector \(u \perp o\). By \((\dagger )\), with \(s_1(\ell ) = \sin \ell {\gt} 0\) for \(0 {\lt} \ell {\lt} \pi \), \(d_v\exp _o\) is injective for \(v \in B\); so \(\exp _o \colon B \to S \setminus \{ -o\} \) is a bijective local diffeomorphism, hence a diffeomorphism. By the observation (with the roles of \(M\) and \(N\) played by \(S\) and \(M\)), \(G_1 = \exp _p \circ J \circ (\exp _o|_B)^{-1} \colon S \setminus \{ -o\} \to M\) is a local isometry with \(G_1(o) = p\) and \(d_oG_1 = J\). Choose \(o^{\prime } \in S\) with \(o^{\prime } \neq \pm o\), and let \(G_2 = \exp _{p^{\prime }} \circ J^{\prime } \circ (\exp _{o^{\prime }}|_B)^{-1} \colon S \setminus \{ -o^{\prime }\} \to M\) with \(p^{\prime } = G_1(o^{\prime })\) and \(J^{\prime } = d_{o^{\prime }}G_1\), again a local isometry. The open set \(W = S \setminus \{ -o, -o^{\prime }\} \) contains \(o^{\prime }\), and it is connected: stereographic projection from \(-o\) (Example 17.9.4) maps it homeomorphically onto \(\mathbb {R}^n\) minus a point, which is connected for \(n \geq 2\) (Lemma 19.5.4). On \(W\) the local isometries \(G_1\) and \(G_2\) agree at \(o^{\prime }\) together with their differentials, so \(G_1 = G_2\) on \(W\) by Lemma 39.13.1(2). Since \(-o \neq -o^{\prime }\), they glue to a local isometry \(G \colon S \to M\). By Lemma 39.10.10, \(G\) is a covering map (\(S\) is compact and \(M\) connected and Hausdorff). As \(M\) is simply connected and \(S\) is path-connected, the monodromy action on a fibre is transitive and \(\pi _1(M)\) is trivial, so each fibre is a single point (Corollary 18.4.4(3)). So \(G\) is a bijective local isometry, that is, an isometry, with \(G(o) = p\) and \(d_oG = J\).

Finally, the model space \(M^n_\kappa \) satisfies the hypotheses, so \(M\) is isometric to it.

02JZ Corollary 39.13.11

  1. The model spaces are frame-homogeneous: for any two points \(p, q \in M^n_\kappa \) and any linear isometry \(I \colon T_pM^n_\kappa \to T_qM^n_\kappa \) there is a unique isometry \(F\) of \(M^n_\kappa \) with \(F(p) = q\) and \(d_pF = I\).

  2. The isometries of \(\mathbb {R}^n\) are the maps \(x \mapsto Ax + b\) with \(A \in \mathrm{O}(n)\) and \(b \in \mathbb {R}^n\).

  3. The isometries of \(S^n(r)\) are the restrictions of the maps \(A \in \mathrm{O}(n + 1)\).

Proof â–¼

(1) is Theorem 39.13.10 with \(M = N = M^n_\kappa \). (2) These maps are isometries, since \(d(Ax + b) = A\) preserves lengths. Given an isometry \(F\), \(A = d_0F\) is an orthogonal matrix and \(x \mapsto Ax + F(0)\) agrees with \(F\) at \(0\) together with its differential, so it equals \(F\) by Lemma 39.13.1(2). (3) Restrictions of orthogonal maps are isometries of \(S^n(r)\). Given an isometry \(F\) and \(o \in S^n(r)\), let \(A\) be the linear map with \(A(o) = F(o)\) and \(A = d_oF\) on \(T_oS^n(r) = o^\perp \); it maps the orthonormal basis formed by \(o/r\) and an orthonormal basis of \(o^\perp \) to an orthonormal basis, since \(d_oF\) is an isometry onto \(F(o)^\perp \), so \(A \in \mathrm{O}(n + 1)\). Then \(A|_{S^n(r)}\) and \(F\) agree at \(o\) together with their differentials, so they are equal.

02K0 Corollary 39.13.12 Space forms

Let \(M\) be a complete connected Riemannian manifold of dimension \(n \geq 2\) with constant sectional curvature \(\kappa \). Then \(M\) is isometric to \(M^n_\kappa /\Gamma \) for a group \(\Gamma \cong \pi _1(M)\) of isometries of \(M^n_\kappa \) acting freely, such that the projection \(M^n_\kappa \to M^n_\kappa /\Gamma \) is a covering map, with the metric for which this projection is a local isometry.

Proof â–¼

Let \(\pi \colon \widetilde{M} \to M\) be a universal covering (Theorem 18.6.5; manifolds are locally path-connected and locally simply connected). \(\widetilde{M}\) is a connected manifold (Proposition 19.6.1(4)) with a unique smooth structure making \(\pi \) a local diffeomorphism (Example 32.7.2), and \(\tilde g = \pi ^*g\) makes \(\pi \) a local isometry, so \(\widetilde{M}\) has constant curvature \(\kappa \). It is complete: let \(\tilde\gamma \) be a maximal geodesic of \(\widetilde{M}\) with \(\tilde\gamma (0) = \tilde x\). Then \(\pi \circ \tilde\gamma \) is a geodesic (Lemma 39.13.1(1)), which extends to a geodesic \(\gamma \) defined on \(\mathbb {R}\) by completeness of \(M\). Let \(\hat\gamma \) be the lift of \(\gamma \) starting at \(\tilde x\) (Proposition 18.4.3). Near each time, \(\hat\gamma \) is \(\gamma \) followed by a local inverse of \(\pi \), which is a local isometry, so \(\hat\gamma \) is a geodesic; it has the initial velocity of \(\tilde\gamma \), so it extends \(\tilde\gamma \) to \(\mathbb {R}\), and \(\tilde\gamma \) was defined on all of \(\mathbb {R}\). By Theorem 39.13.10 there is an isometry \(\Theta \colon M^n_\kappa \to \widetilde{M}\).

The deck transformations \(\tau \) of \(\pi \) satisfy \(\pi \circ \tau = \pi \), so \(\tau ^*\tilde g = \tau ^*\pi ^*g = \pi ^*g = \tilde g\): they are isometries. The group \(\mathrm{Deck}(\pi )\) is isomorphic to \(\pi _1(M)\) and acts transitively on each fibre of \(\pi \) (Proposition 18.6.3), and freely, since a deck transformation with a fixed point is a lift of \(\pi \) agreeing with the identity at one point, hence the identity by the uniqueness in Proposition 18.6.1. So the fibres of \(\pi \) are the orbits, and \(\pi \) induces a bijection \(\widetilde{M}/\mathrm{Deck}(\pi ) \to M\), a homeomorphism because \(\pi \) is open. The group \(\Gamma = \Theta ^{-1}\, \mathrm{Deck}(\pi )\, \Theta \) acts on \(M^n_\kappa \) by isometries, freely, and \(\pi \circ \Theta \) identifies \(M^n_\kappa /\Gamma \) with \(M\); the projection is the covering map \(\pi \circ \Theta \), a local isometry.

02K1 Example 39.13.13

  1. Flat manifolds. The flat tori \(\mathbb {R}^n/\mathbb {Z}^n\) and the flat Klein bottle (Example 39.2.5) are complete flat manifolds, quotients of \(\mathbb {R}^n\).

  2. Spherical space forms. \(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^n = S^n/\{ \pm 1\} \) and the lens spaces \(L(p, q) = S^3/(\mathbb {Z}/p)\) (Example 19.4.5) have constant curvature \(1\), because the maps \((z_1, z_2) \mapsto (\zeta ^kz_1, \zeta ^{qk}z_2)\) are unitary, hence orthogonal on \(\mathbb {R}^4 = \mathbb {C}^2\), and so isometries of \(S^3\). In particular, in dimension \(3\) there are infinitely many spherical space forms, with fundamental groups \(\mathbb {Z}/p\) for all \(p\).

  3. Hyperbolic surfaces. By the Gauss–Bonnet theorem (Corollary 39.7.10) a compact orientable surface with a metric of constant curvature \(-1\) has genus \(g \geq 2\), and its area is \(4\pi (g - 1)\); by Corollary 39.13.12 it is a quotient of the hyperbolic plane.

In even dimension the positive curvature case is very restricted.

02K2 Corollary 39.13.14

Let \(n\) be even and \(M\) complete and connected with constant curvature \(\kappa {\gt} 0\). Then \(M\) is isometric to \(S^n(r)\) or to \(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^n(r) = S^n(r)/\{ \pm 1\} \), with \(r = 1/\sqrt\kappa \).

Proof â–¼

By Corollaries 39.13.12 and 39.13.11(3), \(M = S^n(r)/\Gamma \) for a subgroup \(\Gamma \subseteq \mathrm{O}(n + 1)\) acting freely on the sphere. Every \(A \in \mathrm{SO}(n + 1)\) has the eigenvalue \(1\), because \(n + 1\) is odd: \(\det (A - I) = \det (A - AA^T) = \det A\, \det (I - A^T) = \det (I - A) = (-1)^{n+1}\det (A - I) = -\det (A - I)\). A unit eigenvector, scaled to length \(r\), is a fixed point of \(A\) on \(S^n(r)\), so \(\Gamma \cap \mathrm{SO}(n + 1) = \{ I\} \), and \(\det \colon \Gamma \to \{ \pm 1\} \) is injective. If \(\Gamma \neq \{ I\} \) it is \(\{ I, A\} \) with \(\det A = -1\) and \(A^2 = I\); then \(A\) is diagonalisable with eigenvalues \(\pm 1\) (its minimal polynomial divides \(x^2 - 1\)), and the eigenvalue \(1\) is excluded since the action is free, so \(A = -I\).

Finally we state two rigidity theorems for positive curvature, whose proofs use comparison geometry beyond this chapter. The first says that the diameter bound of the Bonnet–Myers theorem (Theorem 39.8.6) is attained only by the round sphere.

02K3 Theorem 39.13.15 Cheng’s maximal diameter theorem

Let \(M\) be complete and connected of dimension \(n\), with \(\mathrm{Ric} \geq (n - 1)\kappa g\) for some \(\kappa {\gt} 0\), and with diameter \(\pi /\sqrt\kappa \). Then \(M\) is isometric to the sphere \(S^n(1/\sqrt\kappa )\).

This is [Che75]. For example \(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^n\) with the metric of constant curvature \(1\) has \(\mathrm{Ric} = (n - 1)g\) but diameter \(\pi /2\), and a product \(S^2 \times S^2\) of unit spheres has \(\mathrm{Ric} = g\) and diameter \(\sqrt2\, \pi \), less than the bound \(\pi /\sqrt{1/3} = \sqrt3\, \pi \) given by Theorem 39.8.6 with \(n = 4\) and \(3\kappa = 1\).

02K4 Theorem 39.13.16 Sphere theorem

Let \(M\) be complete and simply connected with sectional curvatures in \((\frac14, 1]\). Then \(M\) is homeomorphic to \(S^n\) (Rauch, Berger, Klingenberg), and in fact diffeomorphic to \(S^n\) (Brendle and Schoen).

The topological statement is [dC92, Chapter 13]; the differentiable one is [BS09], which proves more generally that a compact manifold with pointwise \(\frac14\)-pinched curvature is diffeomorphic to a spherical space form. The constant \(\frac14\) is optimal: the complex projective space \(\mathbb {C}\mathbb {P}^m\) with the Fubini–Study metric (Chapter 45) has sectional curvatures filling \([\frac14, 1]\) after scaling, it is compact and simply connected, and for \(m \geq 2\) it is not homeomorphic to a sphere, since \(H^2(\mathbb {C}\mathbb {P}^m; \mathbb {Z}) \cong \mathbb {Z}\). By Theorem 39.8.6, the hypothesis of the sphere theorem already forces \(M\) to be compact.
