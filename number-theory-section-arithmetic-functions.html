10.6 Arithmetic functions and Möbius inversion02YR

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

Euler’s function is one of many functions on the positive integers that are determined by their values on prime powers. This section develops the algebra of such functions.

02YS Definition 10.6.1

An arithmetic function is a function \(f \colon \{ 1, 2, 3, \ldots \} \to \mathbb {C}\). It is multiplicative if \(f(1) = 1\) and \(f(mn) = f(m)f(n)\) whenever \(\gcd (m, n) = 1\), and completely multiplicative if moreover \(f(mn) = f(m)f(n)\) for all \(m, n\). The Dirichlet convolution of arithmetic functions \(f\) and \(g\) is

\[ (f * g)(n) = \sum _{d \mid n}f(d)\, g\Bigl(\frac{n}{d}\Bigr) = \sum _{de = n}f(d)\, g(e), \]

the sums running over the positive divisors \(d\) of \(n\), that is, over the pairs of positive integers \((d, e)\) with \(de = n\).

A multiplicative function is determined by its values at prime powers: \(f(p_1^{k_1} \cdots p_r^{k_r}) = f(p_1^{k_1}) \cdots f(p_r^{k_r})\). We use the following functions:

  • \(\varepsilon (1) = 1\) and \(\varepsilon (n) = 0\) for \(n {\gt} 1\); \(\mathbf{1}(n) = 1\) for all \(n\); \(\mathrm{N}(n) = n\). These are completely multiplicative.

  • Euler’s function \(\varphi \), multiplicative by Proposition 10.2.8.

  • \(\tau (n)\), the number of positive divisors of \(n\), and \(\sigma (n)\), their sum. By definition \(\tau = \mathbf{1} * \mathbf{1}\) and \(\sigma = \mathrm{N} * \mathbf{1}\).

  • The Möbius function: \(\mu (1) = 1\), \(\mu (n) = (-1)^r\) if \(n\) is a product of \(r\) distinct primes, and \(\mu (n) = 0\) if \(n\) is divisible by the square of a prime. It is multiplicative but not completely multiplicative: \(\mu (4) = 0 \neq \mu (2)^2\).

In this notation Proposition 10.2.8(4) reads \(\varphi * \mathbf{1} = \mathrm{N}\).

02YT Proposition 10.6.2

  1. Dirichlet convolution is commutative and associative, and \(\varepsilon \) is its identity element. With pointwise addition, the arithmetic functions form a commutative ring.

  2. An arithmetic function \(f\) has an inverse for \(*\) if and only if \(f(1) \neq 0\).

  3. If \(f\) and \(g\) are multiplicative, so is \(f * g\); and the inverse of a multiplicative function is multiplicative.

Proof â–¼

(1) Commutativity holds because \((f * g)(n) = \sum _{de = n}f(d)g(e)\) is symmetric in the roles of \(d\) and \(e\). Associativity: both \(((f * g) * h)(n)\) and \((f * (g * h))(n)\) equal \(\sum _{cde = n}f(c)g(d)h(e)\), the sum over the triples of positive integers with product \(n\). Further \((\varepsilon * f)(n) = \sum _{de = n}\varepsilon (d)f(e) = f(n)\). Distributivity over addition is clear.

(2) If \(f * g = \varepsilon \), then \(f(1)g(1) = 1\). Conversely, if \(f(1) \neq 0\), define \(g\) recursively by \(g(1) = 1/f(1)\) and, for \(n {\gt} 1\),

\[ g(n) = -\frac{1}{f(1)}\sum _{d \mid n, \ d {\gt} 1}f(d)\, g\Bigl(\frac{n}{d}\Bigr), \]

which only involves values of \(g\) at numbers less than \(n\). This says exactly that \((f * g)(1) = 1\) and \((f * g)(n) = 0\) for \(n {\gt} 1\).

(3) Let \(\gcd (m, n) = 1\). By unique factorisation, every divisor \(d\) of \(mn\) is uniquely a product \(d = d_1d_2\) with \(d_1 \mid m\) and \(d_2 \mid n\) (namely \(d_1 = \gcd (d, m)\), \(d_2 = \gcd (d, n)\)), and then \(\gcd (d_1, d_2) = \gcd (m/d_1, n/d_2) = 1\). Hence

\[ (f * g)(mn) = \sum _{d_1 \mid m, \ d_2 \mid n}f(d_1)f(d_2)\, g\Bigl(\frac{m}{d_1}\Bigr)g\Bigl(\frac{n}{d_2}\Bigr) = (f * g)(m)\, (f * g)(n), \]

and \((f * g)(1) = 1\). For the inverse, let \(f\) be multiplicative, \(g\) its inverse, and let \(h\) be the multiplicative function with \(h(p^k) = g(p^k)\) for all prime powers. Then \(f * h\) is multiplicative by what we just proved, and \((f * h)(p^k) = \sum _{j=0}^{k}f(p^j)g(p^{k-j}) = (f * g)(p^k) = \varepsilon (p^k)\), because the divisors of \(p^k\) are the \(p^j\). So \(f * h\) and \(\varepsilon \) are multiplicative functions that agree on prime powers, hence \(f * h = \varepsilon \) and \(h = g\) by uniqueness of inverses.

02YU Proposition 10.6.3

\(\mu * \mathbf{1} = \varepsilon \); that is, \(\sum _{d \mid n}\mu (d) = 0\) for every \(n {\gt} 1\).

Proof â–¼

Both sides are multiplicative (Proposition 10.6.2(3)), so it suffices to compare them at \(n = p^k\), \(k \geq 1\): the divisors are \(1, p, \ldots , p^k\), and \(\sum _{j=0}^{k}\mu (p^j) = 1 - 1 + 0 + \cdots + 0 = 0 = \varepsilon (p^k)\).

02YV Theorem 10.6.4 Möbius inversion

For arithmetic functions \(f\) and \(g\),

\[ g(n) = \sum _{d \mid n}f(d) \ \text{for all } n \qquad \text{if and only if}\qquad f(n) = \sum _{d \mid n}\mu (d)\, g\Bigl(\frac{n}{d}\Bigr) \ \text{for all } n . \]
Proof â–¼

The left side says \(g = f * \mathbf{1}\), the right side \(f = g * \mu \). Since \(\mu \) is the inverse of \(\mathbf{1}\) (Proposition 10.6.3), multiplying \(g = f * \mathbf{1}\) by \(\mu \) gives \(g * \mu = f\), and multiplying \(f = g * \mu \) by \(\mathbf{1}\) gives \(f * \mathbf{1} = g\).

02YW Example 10.6.5

  1. From \(\varphi * \mathbf{1} = \mathrm{N}\), inversion gives \(\varphi (n) = \sum _{d \mid n}\mu (d)\frac{n}{d} = n\sum _{d \mid n}\frac{\mu (d)}{d}\). Only squarefree \(d\) contribute, and for \(n = p_1^{k_1} \cdots p_r^{k_r}\) the sum is \(\prod _i(1 - 1/p_i)\), recovering Proposition 10.2.8(3).

  2. \(\tau \) and \(\sigma \) are multiplicative, being convolutions of multiplicative functions, and \(\tau (p^k) = k + 1\), \(\sigma (p^k) = 1 + p + \cdots + p^k = \frac{p^{k+1} - 1}{p - 1}\). So \(\tau (n) = \prod _i(k_i + 1)\) and \(\sigma (n) = \prod _i\frac{p_i^{k_i+1} - 1}{p_i - 1}\). For instance \(\tau (12) = 3 \cdot 2 = 6\) and \(\sigma (12) = 7 \cdot 4 = 28 = 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 6 + 12\).

  3. Counting irreducible polynomials. Let \(I_d\) be the number of monic irreducible polynomials of degree \(d\) over \(\mathbb {F}_p\). We claim that \(p^n = \sum _{d \mid n}dI_d\). By Theorem 9.6.2(2), \(\mathbb {F}_{p^n}\) consists of the \(p^n\) roots of \(x^{p^n} - x\), which are distinct. Each \(\alpha \in \mathbb {F}_{p^n}\) has a minimal polynomial over \(\mathbb {F}_p\) (Proposition 9.2.4), monic irreducible of some degree \(d = [\mathbb {F}_p(\alpha ) : \mathbb {F}_p]\), which divides \(n\) by the tower law (Theorem 9.2.2). Conversely, a monic irreducible \(f\) of degree \(d \mid n\) has a root \(\beta \) in a field \(\mathbb {F}_p(\beta ) = \mathbb {F}_p[x]/(f)\) with \(p^d\) elements, so \(\beta ^{p^d} = \beta \) by Theorem 9.6.2(2), hence \(\beta ^{p^n} = \beta \) (apply \(x \mapsto x^{p^d}\) \(n/d\) times), and \(f\), being the minimal polynomial of \(\beta \), divides \(x^{p^n} - x\). As \(x^{p^n} - x\) is the product of the \(x - \alpha \) over the \(p^n\) distinct \(\alpha \in \mathbb {F}_{p^n}\), \(f\) has exactly \(d\) distinct roots in \(\mathbb {F}_{p^n}\), and these are exactly the elements with minimal polynomial \(f\). Counting the elements of \(\mathbb {F}_{p^n}\) according to their minimal polynomials gives the claim. Möbius inversion, applied to \(g(n) = p^n\) and \(f(d) = dI_d\), gives \(nI_n = \sum _{d \mid n}\mu (d)p^{n/d}\). For \(n = 2\): \(I_2 = (p^2 - p)/2\); for \(p = 2\), \(n = 4\): \(I_4 = (16 - 4)/4 = 3\), namely \(x^4 + x + 1\), \(x^4 + x^3 + 1\) and \(x^4 + x^3 + x^2 + x + 1\). In particular \(I_n {\gt} 0\) for all \(n\): the term \(d = 1\) of the sum is \(p^n\), and the others have absolute value at most \(p^{n/d} \leq p^{n/2}\), so \(nI_n \geq p^n - \sum _{e=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor }p^e {\gt} p^n - p^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor + 1} \geq 0\) for \(n \geq 2\).

A number \(n\) is perfect if it is the sum of its divisors smaller than itself, that is, if \(\sigma (n) = 2n\): for instance \(6 = 1 + 2 + 3\), \(28 = 1 + 2 + 4 + 7 + 14\), \(496\) and \(8128\). Euclid observed that \(2^{k-1}(2^k - 1)\) is perfect when \(2^k - 1\) is prime; Euler proved the converse for even numbers.

02YX Theorem 10.6.6 Euclid, Euler

An even number \(n\) is perfect if and only if \(n = 2^{k-1}(2^k - 1)\) with \(k \geq 2\) and \(2^k - 1\) prime.

Proof â–¼

If \(q = 2^k - 1\) is prime, then by multiplicativity \(\sigma (2^{k-1}q) = \sigma (2^{k-1})\sigma (q) = (2^k - 1)(q + 1) = (2^k - 1)2^k = 2n\).

Conversely let \(n\) be even and perfect, and write \(n = 2^{k-1}m\) with \(k \geq 2\) and \(m\) odd. Then

\[ 2^km = 2n = \sigma (n) = \sigma (2^{k-1})\sigma (m) = (2^k - 1)\sigma (m). \]

Since \(2^k - 1\) is odd, it is coprime to \(2^k\), so it divides \(m\): \(m = (2^k - 1)M\), and then \(\sigma (m) = 2^kM = m + M\). Now \(m\) and \(M\) are distinct divisors of \(m\), as \(2^k - 1 \geq 3\), and \(\sigma (m) = m + M\) says that they are the only divisors of \(m\). As \(1\) divides \(m\), \(M = 1\), and \(m = 2^k - 1\) has only the divisors \(1\) and \(m\): it is prime.

02YY Remark 10.6.7

Primes of the form \(2^k - 1\) are called Mersenne primes; \(k\) must then be prime (Exercise 10.9.10), but this is not sufficient: \(2^{11} - 1 = 2047 = 23 \cdot 89\). It is unknown whether there are infinitely many Mersenne primes, and whether any odd perfect number exists.
