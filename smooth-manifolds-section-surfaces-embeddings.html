33.10 Surfaces in three- and four-dimensional space03ES

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

For surfaces, \(n = 2\), Whitney’s theorem (Theorem 33.9.10) gives an embedding into \(\mathbb {R}^4\): every smooth surface, compact or not, orientable or not, embeds in \(\mathbb {R}^4\). Every topological surface has a smooth structure, unique up to diffeomorphism [Moi77], so every surface embeds in \(\mathbb {R}^4\) at least topologically. In \(\mathbb {R}^3\) the answer depends on orientability: the compact orientable surfaces, the sphere and the connected sums of tori of the classification (Theorem 20.8.6), are the familiar surfaces with \(g\) handles in space, while the non-orientable ones do not embed in \(\mathbb {R}^3\) at all. We prove the second statement and write down an explicit embedding of \(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^2\) in \(\mathbb {R}^4\).

03ET Proposition 33.10.1

No compact non-orientable surface, that is, no connected sum \(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^2 \# \cdots \# \mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^2\) of \(k \geq 1\) projective planes, is homeomorphic to a subspace of \(\mathbb {R}^3\). In particular \(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^2\) and the Klein bottle \(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^2 \# \mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^2\) do not embed in \(\mathbb {R}^3\), even topologically.

Proof â–¼

Let \(N\) be such a surface. Its first homology group is the abelianisation of \(\pi _1(N)\) (Theorem 21.9.1), which is \(\mathbb {Z}^{k-1} \oplus \mathbb {Z}/2\) (see the discussion after Theorem 20.8.6), and \(H_2(N; \mathbb {Z}) = 0\) since \(N\) is closed, connected and not orientable (Theorem 23.4.3). By the universal coefficient theorem (Theorem 22.2.3),

\[ H^2(N; \mathbb {Z}) \cong \operatorname {Ext}^1_\mathbb {Z}(\mathbb {Z}^{k-1} \oplus \mathbb {Z}/2, \mathbb {Z}) \oplus \operatorname {Hom}(0, \mathbb {Z}) \cong \mathbb {Z}/2 . \]

Suppose \(K \subseteq \mathbb {R}^3\) were homeomorphic to \(N\). Regard \(\mathbb {R}^3\) as \(S^3\) minus a point; then \(K \subsetneq S^3\) is compact, nonempty and locally contractible, being a manifold, and Alexander duality (Theorem 23.7.2) gives \(\tilde{H}_0(S^3 \setminus K; \mathbb {Z}) \cong \tilde{H}^2(K; \mathbb {Z}) \cong \mathbb {Z}/2\). But \(\tilde{H}_0\) of any space is a free abelian group (Proposition 21.2.5), and \(\mathbb {Z}/2\) is not free. This contradiction proves the proposition.

The proof shows more: for a compact orientable surface \(\Sigma _g \subseteq \mathbb {R}^3\), \(\tilde{H}_0(S^3 \setminus \Sigma _g) \cong H^2(\Sigma _g; \mathbb {Z}) \cong \mathbb {Z}\), so the complement has exactly two components, an inside and an outside, generalising the Jordan–Brouwer theorem (Corollary 23.7.3). A non-orientable surface cannot separate space into an inside and an outside, since a unit normal pointing outwards would orient it; the proposition makes this intuition rigorous without assuming smoothness. Compactness is essential: the open Möbius band, which is \(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^2\) minus a point, does embed in \(\mathbb {R}^3\).

03EU Proposition 33.10.2

The map \(F \colon S^2 \to \mathbb {R}^4\), \(F(x, y, z) = (x^2 - y^2, \, 2xy, \, xz, \, yz)\), induces a smooth embedding \(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^2 \to \mathbb {R}^4\).

Proof â–¼

Identify \(\mathbb {R}^4\) with \(\mathbb {C}^2\) and write \(w = x + iy\), so that \(F(x, y, z) = (w^2, zw)\), with \(|w|^2 + z^2 = 1\). Since \(F(-p) = F(p)\), \(F\) factors as \(F = \bar F \circ \pi \) through the quotient map \(\pi \colon S^2 \to \mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^2\), and \(\bar F\) is smooth because \(\pi \) is a local diffeomorphism (Theorem 33.7.1 and Example 33.7.2); for the same reason \(\bar F\) is an immersion if and only if \(F\) is.

\(\bar F\) is injective. Let \(F(x, y, z) = F(x^{\prime }, y^{\prime }, z^{\prime })\) and \(w^{\prime } = x^{\prime } + iy^{\prime }\). If \(w = 0\), then \(w^{\prime 2} = w^2 = 0\), so \(w^{\prime } = 0\) and both points are \(\pm (0, 0, 1)\), which have the same image in \(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^2\). If \(w \neq 0\), then \(w^{\prime 2} = w^2\) gives \(w^{\prime } = \varepsilon w\) with \(\varepsilon = \pm 1\), and \(z^{\prime }w^{\prime } = zw\) gives \(z^{\prime } = \varepsilon z\); so \((x^{\prime }, y^{\prime }, z^{\prime }) = \varepsilon (x, y, z)\).

\(F\) is an immersion. A tangent vector at \((w, z)\) is a pair \((\delta w, \delta z) \in \mathbb {C}\times \mathbb {R}\) with \(\operatorname {Re}(\bar w\, \delta w) + z\, \delta z = 0\), and \(dF(\delta w, \delta z) = (2w\, \delta w, \, z\, \delta w + w\, \delta z)\). Suppose it vanishes. If \(w \neq 0\), the first component gives \(\delta w = 0\), and then the second gives \(\delta z = 0\). If \(w = 0\), then \(z = \pm 1\), the second component gives \(\delta w = 0\), and the tangency condition gives \(z\, \delta z = 0\), so \(\delta z = 0\).

So \(\bar F\) is an injective immersion of the compact manifold \(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^2\), hence an embedding (Proposition 33.6.7(3)).

The four functions are the quadratic forms \(x^2 - y^2\), \(2xy\), \(xz\), \(yz\); together with \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1\) and the omitted form \(x^2 + y^2 - 2z^2\) they span the quadratic forms in three variables, and the map \(p \mapsto pp^T\) of \(S^2\) into the \(6\)-dimensional space of symmetric matrices, which obviously identifies exactly \(p\) and \(-p\), is the Veronese embedding of \(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^2\); its image lies in an affine hyperplane (\(\operatorname {tr} = 1\)) and in a sphere, so \(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^2\) embeds in \(S^4 \subseteq \mathbb {R}^5\), and the proposition projects it further into \(\mathbb {R}^4\).

03EV Remark 33.10.3

\(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^2\) in \(\mathbb {R}^3\). Although \(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^2\) does not embed in \(\mathbb {R}^3\), it can be mapped into \(\mathbb {R}^3\) with controlled singularities.

  1. Steiner’s Roman surface is the image of \((x, y, z) \mapsto (yz, xz, xy)\), an even map, hence a smooth map \(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^2 \to \mathbb {R}^3\). It is not injective (the three segments of the coordinate axes inside the image are lines of double points) and it is not an immersion at six points (Exercise 33.11.12), where the surface looks like a Whitney umbrella.

  2. The cross-cap is a Möbius band whose boundary circle has been closed up by a disc, at the price of a segment of double points ending in two such umbrella points.

  3. Boy’s surface is an immersion of \(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^2\) in \(\mathbb {R}^3\), without umbrella points, whose only singularities are self-intersections along curves meeting in a single triple point. Hilbert had asked his student Werner Boy to prove that no immersion exists, and Boy found one instead [Boy03]. Taking connected sums, every compact non-orientable surface immerses in \(\mathbb {R}^3\); the familiar picture of the Klein bottle as a bottle passing through its own neck is such an immersion.
