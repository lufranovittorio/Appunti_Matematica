49.5 Structure and symmetries01RR

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

01RS Theorem 49.5.1 Beauville–Bogomolov decomposition

Let \(X\) be a compact Kähler manifold with \(c_1(X)_\mathbb {R}= 0\). Then there is a finite étale cover \(\tilde X \to X\) with

\[ \tilde X \cong T \times \prod _iY_i \times \prod _jZ_j, \]

where \(T\) is a complex torus, the \(Y_i\) are simply connected strict Calabi–Yau manifolds with holonomy \(\mathrm{SU}(\dim Y_i)\), and the \(Z_j\) are simply connected hyperkähler manifolds, with holonomy \(\mathrm{Sp}(\dim Z_j/2)\) and \(H^0(Z_j, \Omega ^2)\) spanned by a symplectic form.

This is [Bea83]. Its proof uses the Ricci-flat metric of Theorem 49.3.1 and the de Rham and Berger classifications of holonomy groups. In dimension \(2\) the only simply connected factors are K3 surfaces, which are both strict Calabi–Yau and hyperkähler.

01RT Remark 49.5.2

The first step is Bochner’s argument. For a Ricci-flat metric, harmonic \(1\)-forms are parallel (Theorem 41.3.4), and more generally every holomorphic \(p\)-form is parallel. So the holomorphic forms are determined by the holonomy representation, and \(h^{p,0}\) is the dimension of the space of holonomy-invariant forms of type \((p, 0)\) at a point [GHJ03]. For holonomy exactly \(\mathrm{SU}(n)\) the only invariants are \(1\) and the volume form, which gives \(h^{p,0} = 0\) for \(0 {\lt} p {\lt} n\).

01RU Remark 49.5.3

Mirror symmetry predicts that strict Calabi–Yau threefolds come in pairs \((X, X^\vee )\) with \(h^{1,1}(X) = h^{2,1}(X^\vee )\) and \(h^{2,1}(X) = h^{1,1}(X^\vee )\), the complex geometry of \(X\) being equivalent to the symplectic geometry of \(X^\vee \). The mirror of the quintic has \(h^{1,1} = 101\) and \(h^{2,1} = 1\), and the resulting prediction of the numbers of rational curves on the quintic [CdlOGP91] was a starting point of enumerative mirror symmetry.

01RV Remark 49.5.4

For projective Calabi–Yau manifolds the Hodge conjecture is known in some degrees. On any projective threefold it holds in all degrees, by the Lefschetz \((1,1)\)-theorem in degree \(2\) and hard Lefschetz in degree \(4\) (Chapter 66); the interesting group \(H^3\) carries no Hodge classes, being of odd degree. For K3 surfaces it holds in all degrees (Chapter 69). For higher-dimensional hyperkähler varieties it is largely open.
