27.13 Hausdorff measures02S4

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

Lebesgue measure \(\lambda ^n\) measures \(n\)-dimensional volume in \(\mathbb {R}^n\), but it does not see lower-dimensional sets: a curve in the plane, or a surface in space, has measure \(0\). How long is a curve, and what is the area of a surface, without choosing a parametrisation? And how big is the Cantor set, which has measure \(0\) and is uncountable? Hausdorff measures answer these questions at once: for every \(s \geq 0\) there is an \(s\)-dimensional measure \(\mathcal{H}^s\) on any metric space, defined by covering a set with small sets \(C\) and adding up \((\operatorname {diam}C)^s\). This and the next two sections are an introduction to geometric measure theory: Hausdorff measure and dimension, differentiability of Lipschitz maps, and the area formula, which computes \(\mathcal{H}^k\) of parametrised \(k\)-dimensional sets. We follow [EG92, Chapters 2–3]; the comprehensive reference is [Fed69].

Throughout, \(X\) is a metric space, \(\operatorname {diam}C = \sup \{ d(x, y) : x, y \in C\} \) for \(C \neq \emptyset \), and \(\operatorname {diam}\emptyset = 0\). For \(s \geq 0\) let \(\alpha (s) = \pi ^{s/2}/(2^s\Gamma (\frac s2 + 1))\), with \(\Gamma \) as in Exercise 27.16.7; for an integer \(n\), \(\alpha (n)\) is the volume of a ball of diameter \(1\) in \(\mathbb {R}^n\). The constant only serves to make \(\mathcal{H}^n\) equal to Lebesgue measure on \(\mathbb {R}^n\) (Theorem 27.13.4); in the sums below the empty set contributes \(0\) and every nonempty set \(C\) contributes \(\alpha (s)(\operatorname {diam}C)^s\), with the convention \((\operatorname {diam}C)^0 = 1\), also when \(C\) is a single point.

02S5 Definition 27.13.1

For \(A \subseteq X\), \(s \geq 0\) and \(\delta {\gt} 0\) let

\[ \mathcal{H}^s_\delta (A) = \inf \Bigl\{ \sum _k\alpha (s)(\operatorname {diam}C_k)^s : A \subseteq \bigcup _kC_k,\ \operatorname {diam}C_k \leq \delta \Bigr\} , \]

the infimum over countable covers by sets of diameter at most \(\delta \). As \(\delta \) decreases, fewer covers are allowed and \(\mathcal{H}^s_\delta (A)\) increases; the \(s\)-dimensional Hausdorff (outer) measure is \(\mathcal{H}^s(A) = \lim _{\delta \to 0}\mathcal{H}^s_\delta (A) = \sup _{\delta {\gt} 0}\mathcal{H}^s_\delta (A)\).

The restriction \(\operatorname {diam}C_k \leq \delta \) is essential: with arbitrary covers, a long segment could be covered by a single set and would get the same size as a short one, while small sets are forced to follow the shape of \(A\). Replacing each \(C_k\) by its closure does not change its diameter, so closed covers give the same infimum. And \(\mathcal{H}^s(A)\) only depends on the metric space \(A\): a cover of \(A\) by subsets of \(X\) gives the cover by the sets \(C_k \cap A\), of smaller diameters. In particular an isometry \(A \to B\) between subsets of two metric spaces preserves \(\mathcal{H}^s\).

02S6 Lemma 27.13.2

Let \(\mu ^*\) be an outer measure on \(X\) which is metric: \(\mu ^*(A \cup B) = \mu ^*(A) + \mu ^*(B)\) whenever \(d(A, B) = \inf \{ d(a, b) : a \in A, b \in B\} {\gt} 0\). Then every Borel set is \(\mu ^*\)-measurable.

Proof â–¼

The measurable sets form a \(\sigma \)-algebra (Theorem 27.3.2), so it suffices to show that a closed set \(F\) is measurable, that is, \(\mu ^*(E \cap F) + \mu ^*(E \setminus F) \leq \mu ^*(E)\) for every \(E\) with \(\mu ^*(E) {\lt} \infty \). Let \(E_j = \{ x \in E : d(x, F) \geq 1/j\} \). Since \(d(E \cap F, E_j) \geq 1/j\), \(\mu ^*(E \cap F) + \mu ^*(E_j) = \mu ^*((E \cap F) \cup E_j) \leq \mu ^*(E)\), and it remains to show \(\mu ^*(E_j) \to \mu ^*(E \setminus F)\). Let \(R_j = \{ x \in E : \frac1{j+1} \leq d(x, F) {\lt} \frac1j\} \). As \(F\) is closed, \(E \setminus F = E_j \cup \bigcup _{i \geq j}R_i\). If \(i \geq l + 2\), then \(d(R_i, R_l) {\gt} 0\), so by induction \(\sum _{i \leq N}\mu ^*(R_{2i}) = \mu ^*(\bigcup _{i \leq N}R_{2i}) \leq \mu ^*(E)\), and similarly for the odd indices. Hence \(\sum _i\mu ^*(R_i) {\lt} \infty \), and \(\mu ^*(E \setminus F) \leq \mu ^*(E_j) + \sum _{i \geq j}\mu ^*(R_i)\), where the tail tends to \(0\). With \(\mu ^*(E_j) \leq \mu ^*(E \setminus F)\) this gives the limit.

02S7 Proposition 27.13.3

  1. \(\mathcal{H}^s\) is a metric outer measure, so its restriction to the Borel sets is a measure.

  2. \(\mathcal{H}^0\) is the counting measure.

  3. If \(f \colon A \to Y\) is Lipschitz with constant \(L\), then \(\mathcal{H}^s(f(A)) \leq L^s\mathcal{H}^s(A)\). For \(X = \mathbb {R}^n\), \(\mathcal{H}^s\) is translation invariant and \(\mathcal{H}^s(rA) = r^s\mathcal{H}^s(A)\) for \(r {\gt} 0\).

  4. If \(\mathcal{H}^s(A) {\lt} \infty \) and \(t {\gt} s\), then \(\mathcal{H}^t(A) = 0\). If \(\mathcal{H}^t(A) {\gt} 0\) and \(s {\lt} t\), then \(\mathcal{H}^s(A) = \infty \).

Proof â–¼

(1) Each \(\mathcal{H}^s_\delta \) is an outer measure: it is monotone, and given covers of the \(A_k\) with sums at most \(\mathcal{H}^s_\delta (A_k) + \varepsilon 2^{-k}\), their union covers \(\bigcup _kA_k\). Then \(\mathcal{H}^s(\bigcup _kA_k) = \sup _\delta \mathcal{H}^s_\delta (\bigcup _kA_k) \leq \sup _\delta \sum _k\mathcal{H}^s_\delta (A_k) \leq \sum _k\mathcal{H}^s(A_k)\). If \(d(A, B) {\gt} 0\) and \(\delta {\lt} d(A, B)\), no set of diameter at most \(\delta \) meets both \(A\) and \(B\), so a \(\delta \)-cover of \(A \cup B\) splits into covers of \(A\) and of \(B\), and \(\mathcal{H}^s_\delta (A \cup B) \geq \mathcal{H}^s_\delta (A) + \mathcal{H}^s_\delta (B)\); let \(\delta \to 0\). Apply Lemma 27.13.2.

(2) \(\alpha (0) = 1\). A set of \(N\) points needs at least \(N\) nonempty sets of diameter less than the smallest distance between the points, and the \(N\) singletons suffice; so \(\mathcal{H}^0_\delta \) of it is \(N\) for small \(\delta \). An infinite set contains finite sets of every size.

(3) If \((C_k)\) is a \(\delta \)-cover of \(A\), then \((f(C_k \cap A))\) is an \(L\delta \)-cover of \(f(A)\) with \(\operatorname {diam}f(C_k \cap A) \leq L\operatorname {diam}C_k\); so \(\mathcal{H}^s_{L\delta }(f(A)) \leq L^s\mathcal{H}^s_\delta (A)\). Translations are isometries, and \(x \mapsto rx\) multiplies diameters by \(r\), with inverse \(x \mapsto x/r\).

(4) For a \(\delta \)-cover, \(\sum _k\alpha (t)(\operatorname {diam}C_k)^t \leq \frac{\alpha (t)}{\alpha (s)}\delta ^{t-s}\sum _k\alpha (s)(\operatorname {diam}C_k)^s\), so \(\mathcal{H}^t_\delta (A) \leq \frac{\alpha (t)}{\alpha (s)}\delta ^{t-s}\mathcal{H}^s_\delta (A) \leq \frac{\alpha (t)}{\alpha (s)}\delta ^{t-s}\mathcal{H}^s(A) \to 0\). The second statement is the contrapositive.

By (4), for each \(A\) there is a unique \(s_0 \in [0, \infty ]\) with \(\mathcal{H}^s(A) = \infty \) for \(s {\lt} s_0\) and \(\mathcal{H}^s(A) = 0\) for \(s {\gt} s_0\); it is the Hausdorff dimension \(\dim _{\mathcal{H}}A = \inf \{ s : \mathcal{H}^s(A) = 0\} \). At \(s = s_0\) the measure can be anything in \([0, \infty ]\). By (3), Lipschitz maps do not increase Hausdorff dimension, and bi-Lipschitz maps preserve it.

02S8 Theorem 27.13.4

On the Borel sets of \(\mathbb {R}^n\), \(\mathcal{H}^n = \lambda ^n\). Consequently the subsets of \(\mathbb {R}^n\) have Hausdorff dimension at most \(n\), and nonempty open subsets have dimension \(n\).

Proof â–¼

We show that \(\mathcal{H}^n = c\lambda ^n\) for a constant \(0 {\lt} c {\lt} \infty \); that \(c = 1\) is the content of the isodiametric inequality \(\lambda ^n(C) \leq \alpha (n)(\operatorname {diam}C)^n\), which says that among sets of given diameter the balls have the largest volume, together with Vitali’s covering theorem, and we refer to [EG92, Section 2.2] for these two steps.

The constant. Let \(Q = [0, 1)^n\). Covering \(Q\) by \(m^n\) cubes of side \(1/m\) and diameter \(\sqrt n/m\) gives \(\mathcal{H}^n_{\sqrt n/m}(Q) \leq \alpha (n)n^{n/2}\), so \(\mathcal{H}^n(Q) {\lt} \infty \). Conversely, a set \(C\) of diameter \(d\) lies in a closed box with sides \(d\) (each coordinate varies by at most \(d\) on \(C\)), so \(\lambda ^*(C) \leq d^n\); for any cover of \(Q\), \(\sum _k\alpha (n)(\operatorname {diam}C_k)^n \geq \alpha (n)\sum _k\lambda ^*(C_k) \geq \alpha (n)\), so \(c = \mathcal{H}^n(Q) \geq \alpha (n) {\gt} 0\).

Comparison. \(\mathcal{H}^n\) is a translation invariant measure on the Borel sets (Proposition 27.13.3). For \(j \geq 0\), \(Q\) is the disjoint union of \(2^{jn}\) translates of \([0, 2^{-j})^n\), so every half-open dyadic cube \(\prod _i[a_i2^{-j}, (a_i + 1)2^{-j})\) (\(a_i \in \mathbb {Z}\), \(j \in \mathbb {Z}\)) has \(\mathcal{H}^n\)-measure \(c2^{-jn} = c\lambda ^n\) of it; for \(j {\lt} 0\) use that such a cube is a disjoint union of \(2^{-jn}\) unit cubes. Fix \(m \geq 0\) and \(P = [-2^m, 2^m)^n\). The dyadic cubes contained in \(P\), together with \(P\), form a family closed under finite intersections, on which \(\mathcal{H}^n\) and \(c\lambda ^n\) agree and are finite. By Lemma 27.3.6 the two measures agree on the \(\sigma \)-algebra it generates, which contains every open subset of the interior of \(P\) (a countable union of dyadic cubes), hence every Borel subset of the interior. The boundary of \(P\) is a finite union of pieces of hyperplanes, which are null for both measures: a piece \([0, 1]^{n-1} \times \{ 0\} \) is covered by \(j^{n-1}\) cubes of diameter \(\sqrt n/j\), with \(\sum \alpha (n)(\operatorname {diam})^n \to 0\) as \(j \to \infty \). So the two measures agree on the Borel subsets of \(P\), and letting \(m \to \infty \), on all Borel sets.

The last statement follows from Proposition 27.13.3(4): \(\mathcal{H}^n\) is finite on bounded sets and \(\mathbb {R}^n\) is a countable union of them, so \(\mathcal{H}^t = 0\) for \(t {\gt} n\), while a nonempty open set has positive \(\mathcal{H}^n\)-measure.

02S9 Example 27.13.5

The Cantor set has Hausdorff dimension \(s = \log 2/\log 3\), and \(\alpha (s)/2 \leq \mathcal{H}^s(C) \leq \alpha (s)\); note \(3^s = 2\). Let \(C \subseteq [0, 1]\) be as in Counterexample 27.8.7. For \(k \geq 0\) and digits \(a_1, \ldots , a_k \in \{ 0, 2\} \) let \(I_{a_1\cdots a_k}\) be the closed interval with left end \(\sum _{j \leq k}a_j3^{-j}\) and length \(3^{-k}\); there are \(2^k\) of them, and \(C\) is contained in their union \(C_k\), since the digits beyond the \(k\)-th contribute at most \(3^{-k}\).

  1. Upper bound. The \(2^k\) intervals form a \(3^{-k}\)-cover of \(C\) with \(\sum \alpha (s)3^{-ks} = \alpha (s)2^k3^{-ks} = \alpha (s)\). So \(\mathcal{H}^s(C) \leq \alpha (s)\).

  2. Separation. Two of these intervals of the same generation \(k\) are at distance at least \(3^{-k}\): if their digit strings first differ at position \(j \leq k\), their left ends differ by at least \(2 \cdot 3^{-j} - \sum _{j {\lt} i \leq k}2 \cdot 3^{-i} = 3^{-j} + 3^{-k} \geq 2 \cdot 3^{-k}\). So a set of diameter less than \(3^{-k}\) meets at most one of them.

  3. The Cantor measure. Let \(\mu = \mu _F\) be the measure of Lemma 27.12.3 for the Cantor function \(F\). Since \(F\) is constant on each interval of \([0, 1] \setminus C\), \(\mu ([0, 1] \setminus C) = 0\) and \(\mu (C) = F(1) - F(0) = 1\). On the generation-\(k\) interval \(I\) with left end \(a = \sum _{j \leq k}a_j3^{-j}\) and right end \(a + \sum _{j {\gt} k}2 \cdot 3^{-j}\), \(\mu (I) = F(a + 3^{-k}) - F(a) = \sum _{j {\gt} k}2^{-j} = 2^{-k}\), by the formula for \(c\) on \(C\).

  4. Lower bound. Let \(U\) be a closed set with \(0 {\lt} \operatorname {diam}U = d {\lt} 1\), and \(k\) with \(3^{-k-1} \leq d {\lt} 3^{-k}\). By (2), \(U\) meets at most one generation-\(k\) interval \(I\), and \(\mu (U) \leq \mu (I) + \mu ([0, 1] \setminus C) = 2^{-k} = 3^s3^{-(k+1)s} \leq 2d^s\); for a single point, \(\mu (U) = 0\). For a cover of \(C\) by closed sets \(U_i\) of diameter less than \(1\), \(1 = \mu (C) \leq \sum _i\mu (U_i \cap [0, 1]) \leq 2\sum _i(\operatorname {diam}U_i)^s\). Hence \(\mathcal{H}^s_\delta (C) \geq \alpha (s)/2\) for \(\delta {\lt} 1\).

The argument in (4), bounding the mass that a measure on \(A\) gives to small sets, is the mass distribution principle, the standard way to bound Hausdorff measures from below. So the Cantor set is too small to have positive length but too large to have zero \(s\)-dimensional measure for any \(s {\lt} \log 2/\log 3 \approx 0.63\).
