41.4 The cotangent bundle02UP

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

Let \(M\) be a manifold of dimension \(n\) and \(\pi \colon T^*M \to M\) its cotangent bundle. A chart with coordinates \(q^1, \ldots , q^n\) on \(U \subseteq M\) gives coordinates \((q, p)\) on \(T^*U\), where a covector \(\xi = \sum _ip_i\, dq^i|_q\) has coordinates \((q^1, \ldots , q^n, p_1, \ldots , p_n)\).

02UQ Definition 41.4.1

The tautological (or Liouville) \(1\)-form \(\lambda \) on \(T^*M\) is defined at a point \(\xi \in T^*_qM\) by

\[ \lambda _\xi (Y) = \xi \bigl(d\pi (Y)\bigr), \qquad Y \in T_\xi (T^*M); \]

a point of \(T^*M\) is a covector on \(M\), and \(\lambda \) applies it to the projection of \(Y\) to \(M\). The canonical symplectic form is \(\omega = -d\lambda \).

02UR Proposition 41.4.2

  1. In coordinates \(\lambda = \sum _ip_i\, dq^i\) and \(\omega = \sum _idq^i \wedge dp_i\); so \(\omega \) is a symplectic form.

  2. \(\lambda \) is characterised by the property \(\alpha ^*\lambda = \alpha \) for every \(1\)-form \(\alpha \) on \(M\), regarded as a section \(\alpha \colon M \to T^*M\). Consequently \(\alpha ^*\omega = -d\alpha \).

  3. For a diffeomorphism \(f \colon M \to N\), the cotangent lift \(F \colon T^*M \to T^*N\), \(F(\xi ) = \xi \circ (d_qf)^{-1}\) for \(\xi \in T^*_qM\), satisfies \(F^*\lambda _N = \lambda _M\); so it is a symplectomorphism.

Proof â–¼

(1) A tangent vector at \(\xi = \sum p_i\, dq^i\) is \(Y = \sum _i(a^i\partial _{q^i} + b_i\partial _{p_i})\), and \(d\pi (Y) = \sum _ia^i\partial _{q^i}\), so \(\lambda (Y) = \sum _ip_ia^i\): \(\lambda = \sum _ip_i\, dq^i\). Then \(\omega = -\sum _idp_i \wedge dq^i = \sum _idq^i \wedge dp_i\), which is closed and, at each point, the standard form in the basis \(\partial _{q^i}\), \(\partial _{p_i}\).

(2) Since \(\pi \circ \alpha = \mathrm{id}\), \((\alpha ^*\lambda )_q(v) = \lambda _{\alpha _q}(d\alpha (v)) = \alpha _q(d\pi \, d\alpha (v)) = \alpha _q(v)\). Conversely, let \(\mu = \sum _i(A_i\, dq^i + B^i\, dp_i)\) in coordinates satisfy \(\alpha ^*\mu = \alpha \) for all \(\alpha = \sum _ia_i\, dq^i\); then \(\sum _iA_i(q, a(q))\, dq^i + \sum _kB^k(q, a(q))\, da_k = \sum _ia_i\, dq^i\). Constant \(a_i = c_i\) give \(A_i(q, c) = c_i\); then at a point \(q_0\), the functions \(a_k = c_k + \sum _jd_{kj}(q^j - q_0^j)\) give \(\sum _kB^k(q_0, c)d_{ki} = 0\) for every matrix \((d_{kj})\), so \(B^k = 0\). Hence \(\mu = \lambda \). Finally \(\alpha ^*\omega = -d\alpha ^*\lambda = -d\alpha \).

(3) \(\pi _N \circ F = f \circ \pi _M\), so \((F^*\lambda _N)_\xi (Y) = F(\xi )(d\pi _N\, dF(Y)) = F(\xi )(df\, d\pi _M(Y)) = \xi (d\pi _M(Y)) = \lambda _M(Y)\).

02US Corollary 41.4.3

  1. The graph \(\alpha (M)\) of a \(1\)-form \(\alpha \) is a Lagrangian submanifold of \(T^*M\) if and only if \(d\alpha = 0\). In particular the zero section and the graphs \(df(M)\) of differentials of functions are Lagrangian.

  2. The fibres \(T^*_qM\) are Lagrangian.

  3. For a submanifold \(S \subseteq M\), the conormal bundle \(N^*S = \{ \xi \in T^*_qM : q \in S, \ \xi |_{T_qS} = 0\} \) is a Lagrangian submanifold of \(T^*M\).

Proof â–¼

(1) \(\alpha \colon M \to T^*M\) is an embedding onto its graph, of dimension \(n\), and the restriction of \(\omega \) to it corresponds to \(\alpha ^*\omega = -d\alpha \). (2) In coordinates the fibre is \(\{ q = \text{const}\} \), on which every \(dq^i\) vanishes. (3) In a slice chart with \(S = \{ q^{k+1} = \cdots = q^n = 0\} \), \(N^*S = \{ q^{k+1} = \cdots = q^n = 0, \ p_1 = \cdots = p_k = 0\} \), a submanifold of dimension \(n\) with coordinates \(q^1, \ldots , q^k, p_{k+1}, \ldots , p_n\), on which each term \(dq^i \wedge dp_i\) vanishes.

Along the zero section \(0_M\) the tangent spaces split canonically: \(T_{0_q}(T^*M) = T_q(0_M) \oplus T_{0_q}(T^*_qM) \cong T_qM \oplus T^*_qM\), the second summand being the vertical subspace (Definition 38.7.1). Both summands are Lagrangian, and in coordinates \(\omega (\sum a^i\partial _{q^i}, \sum b_i\partial _{p_i}) = \sum a^ib_i\): the symplectic form pairs them by the duality \(T_qM \times T_q^*M \to \mathbb {R}\), as in Proposition 41.2.2(4). Away from the zero section the vertical subspace is still canonical, but a horizontal complement requires a choice, a connection (Section 38.7).

02UT Proposition 41.4.4

Let \(\nabla \) be a connection on \(TM\), with the induced connection on \(T^*M\) and its horizontal distribution \(H \subseteq T(T^*M)\) (Proposition 38.7.3). The vertical distribution \(V\) is Lagrangian, and \(H\) is Lagrangian if and only if \(\nabla \) is torsion-free. In that case \(T(T^*M) = H \oplus V\) with \(H_\xi \cong T_qM\), \(V_\xi \cong T^*_qM\) and \(\omega (h, v) = \langle v, d\pi (h)\rangle \) for \(h \in H_\xi \) and \(v \in V_\xi \) (identified with a covector by the vertical lift).

Proof â–¼

\(V\) is the tangent bundle of the fibres, Lagrangian by Corollary 41.4.3(2). With \(\nabla _{\partial _i}\partial _k = \sum _j\Gamma ^j_{ik}\partial _j\), the dual connection satisfies \(\nabla _{\partial _i}dq^j = -\sum _k\Gamma ^j_{ik}\, dq^k\), so for \(\alpha = \sum _jp_j\, dq^j\), \(\nabla _{\partial _i}\alpha = \sum _k(\partial _ip_k - \sum _j\Gamma ^j_{ik}p_j)\, dq^k\). By Proposition 38.7.3, \(H\) is spanned by the horizontal lifts of the coordinate fields,

\[ X_i = \partial _{q^i} + \sum _{j,k}\Gamma ^j_{ik}p_j\, \partial _{p_k}. \]

Then \(\omega (X_i, X_l) = dp_i(X_l) - dp_l(X_i) = \sum _jp_j(\Gamma ^j_{li} - \Gamma ^j_{il}) = -\sum _jp_jT^j_{il}\), where \(T(\partial _i, \partial _l) = \sum _jT^j_{il}\partial _j\) is the torsion (Exercise 38.9.7). This vanishes for all \(p\) if and only if \(T = 0\); and \(H\) has dimension \(n\). Finally \(\omega (X_i, \partial _{p_k}) = \delta _{ik}\), which is the stated pairing.

So a torsion-free connection, for instance the Levi-Civita connection of a metric, splits \(T(T^*M)\) into two transverse Lagrangian subbundles paired by duality, extending the canonical splitting along the zero section, where every horizontal distribution is tangent to the zero section.

The derivative of a \(1\)-form regarded as a map. A \(1\)-form \(\alpha \) on \(M\) can be differentiated in two quite different ways, and it is worth seeing how the two are related. Its exterior derivative \(d\alpha \) is a \(2\)-form on \(M\). But \(\alpha \) is also a smooth map \(\alpha \colon M \to T^*M\), a section of \(\pi \), and as such it has at each point \(q\) a differential, a linear map

\[ \alpha _* \colon T_qM \to T_{\alpha _q}(T^*M), \]

which we write \(\alpha _*\) rather than \(d_q\alpha \) so as not to confuse it with the exterior derivative. Its image is the tangent space of the graph \(\alpha (M)\) at the point \(\alpha _q\). For \(\alpha = df\) one may ask whether the identity \(d(df) = 0\) (Remark 36.2.9) means that the map \(df \colon M \to T^*M\) has zero differential. It does not, and it could not: \(\pi \circ df = \mathrm{id}_M\), so \(d\pi \circ (df)_* = \mathrm{id}\) and \((df)_*\) is injective. We shall see that \((df)_*\) is, apart from a part that is the same for every \(1\)-form, the Hessian of \(f\). What \(d(df) = 0\) says is subtler: the exterior derivative only sees the antisymmetric part of the derivative of a \(1\)-form, and the Hessian is symmetric. Geometrically, it says that the tangent spaces of the graph of \(df\) are Lagrangian, which is Corollary 41.4.3(1) seen pointwise.

02XB Proposition 41.4.5

Let \(\alpha \) be a \(1\)-form on \(M\), \(q \in M\), and \(\alpha _* \colon T_qM \to T_{\alpha _q}(T^*M)\) the differential of \(\alpha \) regarded as a map \(M \to T^*M\).

  1. In coordinates, if \(\alpha = \sum _ia_i\, dq^i\) and \(v = \sum _jv^j\partial _{q^j}\), then

    \[ \alpha _*(v) = \sum _jv^j\, \partial _{q^j} + \sum _{i,j}\frac{\partial a_i}{\partial q^j}(q)\, v^j\, \partial _{p_i}. \]

    So in the bases \(\partial _{q^j}\) of \(T_qM\) and \(\partial _{q^j}\), \(\partial _{p_i}\) of \(T_{\alpha _q}(T^*M)\), the matrix of \(\alpha _*\) is \(\begin{pmatrix} I \\ A \end{pmatrix}\), where \(A = (\partial a_i/\partial q^j)\) is the Jacobian matrix of the components of \(\alpha \), with rows indexed by \(i\) and columns by \(j\).

  2. For \(u, v \in T_qM\),

    \[ \omega (\alpha _*u, \alpha _*v) = -d\alpha (u, v) = u^{T}(A - A^{T})v, \]

    where in the last expression \(u\) and \(v\) are the columns of their components. So \(d\alpha \) is, up to sign, the antisymmetric part of the Jacobian matrix of \(\alpha \).

  3. Let \(\nabla \) be a connection on \(TM\), with the induced connection on \(T^*M\) and its horizontal distribution (Proposition 38.7.3). Then

    \[ \alpha _*(v) = v^h_{\alpha _q} + \mathrm{vl}_{\alpha _q}(\nabla _v\alpha ): \]

    the horizontal part of \(\alpha _*(v)\) is the horizontal lift of \(v\), the same for all \(1\)-forms, and its vertical part is the covariant derivative \(\nabla _v\alpha \in T^*_qM\). If \(\nabla \) is torsion-free, then

    \[ d\alpha (u, v) = (\nabla _u\alpha )(v) - (\nabla _v\alpha )(u), \qquad u, v \in T_qM . \]
Proof â–¼

(1) In the coordinates \((q, p)\) of \(T^*U\), the map \(\alpha \) is \(q \mapsto (q, a_1(q), \ldots , a_n(q))\). Its Jacobian matrix has the identity matrix in the rows of the coordinates \(q^j\) and the matrix \((\partial a_i/\partial q^j)\) in the rows of the coordinates \(p_i\); this is the formula.

(2) By the definition of the pullback, \((\alpha ^*\omega )_q(u, v) = \omega (\alpha _*u, \alpha _*v)\), and \(\alpha ^*\omega = -d\alpha \) by Proposition 41.4.2(2). For the matrix expression we compute both sides in coordinates. By (1), \(\alpha _*u\) has components \((u, Au)\) and \(\alpha _*v\) has components \((v, Av)\), and \(\omega = \sum _idq^i \wedge dp_i\) gives

\[ \omega (\alpha _*u, \alpha _*v) = \sum _i\bigl(u^i(Av)_i - v^i(Au)_i\bigr) = u^{T}Av - v^{T}Au = u^{T}(A - A^{T})v . \]

On the other side \(d\alpha = \sum _{i,j}\partial _ja_i\, dq^j \wedge dq^i\), so \(d\alpha (u, v) = \sum _{i,j}\partial _ja_i\, (u^jv^i - v^ju^i) = v^{T}Au - u^{T}Av\), which is the same number with the opposite sign.

(3) The first formula is Proposition 38.7.3(2), applied to the bundle \(T^*M\) with the induced connection and to its section \(\alpha \). For the second, extend \(u\) and \(v\) to vector fields \(U\) and \(V\) near \(q\). By the Leibniz rule \((\nabla _U\alpha )(V) = U(\alpha (V)) - \alpha (\nabla _UV)\) (Proposition 38.3.6(1)),

\[ (\nabla _U\alpha )(V) - (\nabla _V\alpha )(U) = U(\alpha (V)) - V(\alpha (U)) - \alpha (\nabla _UV - \nabla _VU) = U(\alpha (V)) - V(\alpha (U)) - \alpha ([U, V]), \]

since \(\nabla _UV - \nabla _VU = [U, V]\) for a torsion-free connection. The right-hand side is \(d\alpha (U, V)\) by the invariant formula for the exterior derivative (Theorem 36.2.8); evaluate at \(q\).

So the derivative of \(\alpha \) as a map has two parts. The horizontal part carries no information about \(\alpha \). The vertical part is a bilinear form \((u, v) \mapsto (\nabla _u\alpha )(v)\) on \(T_qM\), which depends on the choice of the connection; but its antisymmetric part is \(d\alpha \), which does not. For the flat connection of a chart, whose Christoffel symbols vanish, the horizontal lift of \(\partial _{q^j}\) is \(\partial _{q^j}\) and \((\nabla _{\partial _{q^j}}\alpha )(\partial _{q^i}) = \partial a_i/\partial q^j\), and (3) reduces to (1). The symmetric part of \(\nabla \alpha \) has no meaning independent of the connection, except where \(\alpha \) vanishes, as we now see for \(\alpha = df\).

02XC Corollary 41.4.6

Let \(f\) be a smooth function on \(M\), and give \(M\) a Riemannian metric with its Levi-Civita connection.

  1. The differential of the map \(df \colon M \to T^*M\) at \(q\) is

    \[ (df)_*(v) = v^h + \mathrm{vl}\bigl(\operatorname {Hess}f(v, \cdot )\bigr), \qquad v \in T_qM, \]

    so the tangent space of the graph \(df(M)\) at \(d_qf\) is the graph of the linear map \(T_qM \to T^*_qM\), \(v \mapsto \operatorname {Hess}f(v, \cdot )\), in the splitting \(H \oplus V\).

  2. For \(u, v \in T_qM\),

    \[ \omega \bigl((df)_*u, (df)_*v\bigr) = -d(df)(u, v) = \operatorname {Hess}f(v, u) - \operatorname {Hess}f(u, v) = 0 . \]

    Thus the identity \(d(df) = 0\), the symmetry of the Hessian, and the fact that the graph of \(df\) is Lagrangian are one and the same statement.

  3. Let \(q\) be a critical point of \(f\), so that \(d_qf = 0_q\) lies on the zero section. In the canonical splitting \(T_{0_q}(T^*M) = T_qM \oplus T^*_qM\),

    \[ (df)_*(v) = \bigl(v, \operatorname {Hess}f_q(v, \cdot )\bigr), \]

    where \(\operatorname {Hess}f_q(v, w) = \sum _{i,j}v^iw^j\, \partial _i\partial _jf(q)\) in any chart does not depend on the metric. The tangent spaces at \(0_q\) of the graph \(df(M)\) and of the zero section \(0_M\) intersect in \(\{ (v, 0) : \operatorname {Hess}f_q(v, \cdot ) = 0\} \), and they span \(T_{0_q}(T^*M)\) if and only if \(\operatorname {Hess}f_q\) is nondegenerate.

Proof â–¼

(1) By Proposition 41.4.5(3) with \(\alpha = df\), the vertical part of \((df)_*(v)\) is \(\nabla _vdf = \operatorname {Hess}f(v, \cdot )\) (Definition 40.5.1(2)).

(2) The first equality is Proposition 41.4.5(2). By Proposition 41.4.5(3), since the Levi-Civita connection is torsion-free, \(d(df)(u, v) = (\nabla _udf)(v) - (\nabla _vdf)(u) = \operatorname {Hess}f(u, v) - \operatorname {Hess}f(v, u)\), and this vanishes, either because \(d(df) = 0\) or because the Hessian is symmetric (Proposition 40.5.2(3)).

(3) In the coordinates of the proof of Proposition 41.4.4, the horizontal lifts of the coordinate fields are \(X_i = \partial _{q^i} + \sum _{j,k}\Gamma ^j_{ik}p_j\, \partial _{p_k}\), which at a point with \(p = 0\) equal \(\partial _{q^i}\); so at \(0_q\) the horizontal lift of \(v\) is the vector \((v, 0)\), tangent to the zero section \(\{ p = 0\} \). By (1), \((df)_*(v) = (v, \operatorname {Hess}f_q(v, \cdot ))\), and the Hessian at a critical point has the stated form and does not depend on the metric by Proposition 40.5.2(4). Write \(H_q \colon T_qM \to T^*_qM\), \(v \mapsto \operatorname {Hess}f_q(v, \cdot )\). The two tangent spaces are \(\{ (v, H_qv)\} \) and \(\{ (v, 0)\} \). A vector lies in both when \((v, H_qv) = (w, 0)\), that is, \(w = v\) and \(H_qv = 0\). Their sum contains every \((v, 0)\) and every \((0, H_qv) = (v, H_qv) - (v, 0)\), so it is \(T_qM \oplus H_q(T_qM)\); it is everything if and only if \(H_q\) is surjective, that is (as \(\dim T_qM = \dim T^*_qM\)), an isomorphism, which means that \(\operatorname {Hess}f_q\) is nondegenerate.

02XD Remark 41.4.7

A critical point \(q\) with \(\operatorname {Hess}f_q\) nondegenerate is called nondegenerate, and a function all of whose critical points are nondegenerate is a Morse function. Two submanifolds whose tangent spaces at a common point span the whole tangent space meet transversally there. So (3) says: the critical points of \(f\) are the points where the graph of \(df\) meets the zero section, and the nondegenerate ones are those where it meets the zero section transversally. Both are Lagrangian submanifolds of dimension \(n\) in the \(2n\)-dimensional manifold \(T^*M\), so a transverse intersection point is isolated (Exercise 41.8.6). This picture, critical points of functions as intersections of Lagrangian submanifolds, is the starting point of Morse theory and of its symplectic generalisations, which we do not develop.

02XE Example 41.4.8

  1. On \(\mathbb {R}^2\) with coordinates \((x, y) = (q^1, q^2)\), let \(f = \frac12(x^2 - y^2)\). Then \(df = x\, dx - y\, dy\), and the map \(df \colon \mathbb {R}^2 \to T^*\mathbb {R}^2 = \mathbb {R}^4\) is \((x, y) \mapsto (x, y, x, -y)\). Its differential has matrix \(\begin{pmatrix} I \\ A \end{pmatrix}\) with \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\), the Hessian matrix of \(f\), which is symmetric. The graph is the plane \(\{ p_1 = q^1, \ p_2 = -q^2\} \), on which \(\omega = dq^1 \wedge dp_1 + dq^2 \wedge dp_2\) restricts to \(dq^1 \wedge dq^1 - dq^2 \wedge dq^2 = 0\): it is Lagrangian. It meets the zero section \(\{ p = 0\} \) only at the origin, the critical point of \(f\), and transversally, as \(A\) is invertible: the origin is a nondegenerate critical point (a saddle).

  2. On \(\mathbb {R}\), let \(f = x^3\). Then \(df = 3x^2\, dx\) and the graph of \(df\) is the parabola \(\{ p = 3q^2\} \) in the plane \(T^*\mathbb {R}= \mathbb {R}^2\). It touches the zero section \(\{ p = 0\} \) at the origin, tangentially: the critical point \(0\) is degenerate, as \(f^{\prime \prime }(0) = 0\). (On a \(1\)-dimensional manifold every curve in \(T^*M\) is Lagrangian, so here the Lagrangian condition says nothing.)

  3. On \(\mathbb {R}^2\), let \(\alpha = x\, dy\), so \(a_1 = 0\) and \(a_2 = x\). The Jacobian matrix is \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\), which is not symmetric: \(\alpha _*(\partial _x) = \partial _{q^1} + \partial _{p_2}\) and \(\alpha _*(\partial _y) = \partial _{q^2}\), and

    \[ \omega \bigl(\alpha _*\partial _x, \alpha _*\partial _y\bigr) = (dq^2 \wedge dp_2)(\partial _{p_2}, \partial _{q^2}) = -1 = -d\alpha (\partial _x, \partial _y), \]

    as \(d\alpha = dx \wedge dy\). So the graph of \(\alpha \) is not Lagrangian, and \(\alpha \) is not the differential of a function. Splitting \(A\) into its symmetric and antisymmetric parts corresponds to writing \(\alpha = \frac12\, d(xy) + \frac12(x\, dy - y\, dx)\): the exact part \(\frac12\, d(xy)\) has the symmetric Jacobian \(\frac12\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\), the Hessian of \(\frac12xy\), and the part \(\frac12(x\, dy - y\, dx)\) has the antisymmetric Jacobian \(\frac12\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\), which carries all of \(d\alpha \).
