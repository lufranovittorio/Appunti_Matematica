00JX Exercise 11.12.1

Show that \(\operatorname {tr}(AB) = \operatorname {tr}(BA)\) for \(A \in M_{m \times n}(k)\), \(B \in M_{n \times m}(k)\), and deduce that similar matrices have the same trace. Show that if \(\chi _f = \prod _i (x - \lambda _i)\) then \(\operatorname {tr} f = \sum \lambda _i\) and \(\det f = \prod \lambda _i\).

00JY Exercise 11.12.2

Suppose \(\chi _f\) splits. Show that the number of Jordan blocks \(J_e(\lambda )\) with \(e \geq j\) in the Jordan form of \(f\) is \(\dim \ker (f - \lambda )^j - \dim \ker (f - \lambda )^{j-1}\).

00JZ Exercise 11.12.3

Let \(V\) be a finite-dimensional complex vector space and \(\mathcal{F}\) a set of pairwise commuting endomorphisms. Show that there is a basis in which all elements of \(\mathcal{F}\) are upper triangular.

00K0 Exercise 11.12.4

Show that every invertible complex matrix is uniquely \(A = UP\) with \(U\) unitary and \(P\) Hermitian with positive eigenvalues.

00K1 Exercise 11.12.5

Let \(\omega \) be a nondegenerate alternating form on \(V\) of dimension \(2m\) and \(\mathrm{Sp}(V, \omega )\) the group of \(f \in \mathrm{GL}(V)\) with \(\omega (fv, fw) = \omega (v, w)\). Show that \(\det f = \pm 1\) for \(f \in \mathrm{Sp}(V, \omega )\). (It is in fact always \(1\); this can be shown using the Pfaffian, Chapter 12.)

00K2 Exercise 11.12.6

02Q5 Exercise 11.12.7

Let \(q(x_1, x_2) = x_1^2 + 4x_1x_2 + x_2^2\) on \(\mathbb {R}^2\), with Gram matrix \(G = \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\).

  1. Complete the square to find a change of variables \(x = Py\) with \(q = y_1^2 - 3y_2^2\), and check that \(P^TGP = \operatorname {diag}(1, -3)\) but that \(P^{-1}GP\) is not diagonal.

  2. Find an orthogonal \(Q\) with \(Q^TGQ = Q^{-1}GQ\) diagonal.

  3. Compare the two diagonal forms with Sylvester’s law of inertia, and explain why they are congruent but not similar.

Solution â–¼

(1) \(q = (x_1 + 2x_2)^2 - 3x_2^2\), so \(y_1 = x_1 + 2x_2\), \(y_2 = x_2\), that is, \(x = Py\) with \(P = \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\). Then \(GP = \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 2 & -3 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\), \(P^TGP = \operatorname {diag}(1, -3)\), while \(P^{-1} = \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\) gives \(P^{-1}GP = \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} 5 & -6 \\ 2 & -3 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\). (2) The eigenvalues of \(G\) are \(3\) and \(-1\), with unit eigenvectors \((1, 1)/\sqrt2\) and \((1, -1)/\sqrt2\); with these as columns, \(Q\) is orthogonal and \(Q^TGQ = Q^{-1}GQ = \operatorname {diag}(3, -1)\). (3) Both diagonal matrices have one positive and one negative entry, signature \((1, 1)\), so they are congruent, as Sylvester’s law requires; they are not similar, since their eigenvalues \(\{ 1, -3\} \) and \(\{ 3, -1\} \) differ. The matrix \(P^{-1}GP\) of (1), similar to \(G\), has eigenvalues \(3\) and \(-1\), as it must.

0301 Exercise 11.12.8

Show that the reduced row echelon form \(R\) of a matrix \(A\) in Theorem 11.6.4 is unique: it depends only on \(A\), not on the row operations used.

Solution â–¼

By Lemma 11.6.2 the columns of \(R\) satisfy the same linear relations as the columns of \(A\). Column \(j\) of \(R\) is a pivot column exactly when it is not a combination of the columns before it (a pivot column \(e_s\) is not a combination of earlier columns, which vanish in row \(s\); a non-pivot column has zeros below the row of the last pivot to its left, so it is a combination of the earlier pivot columns \(e_1, \ldots , e_s\)). So the pivot positions are determined by \(A\). A non-pivot column \(j\) of \(R\) equals \(\sum _sc_se_s\), the sum over the pivot columns \(e_s\) before it, and the coefficients \(c_s\) are the unique coefficients expressing column \(j\) of \(A\) as a combination of the pivot columns of \(A\) before it. So every column of \(R\) is determined by \(A\).

0302 Exercise 11.12.9

For which \(a \in \mathbb {R}\) does the system \(x + y + z = 1\), \(x + ay + z = 1\), \(x + y + az = 2\) have solutions? Find them.

Solution â–¼

The matrix of the system has determinant \((a - 1)^2\): subtracting the first row from the others gives rows \((0, a - 1, 0)\) and \((0, 0, a - 1)\). For \(a \neq 1\) the solution is unique: the differences of the equations give \((a - 1)y = 0\) and \((a - 1)z = 1\), so \(y = 0\), \(z = 1/(a - 1)\) and \(x = 1 - 1/(a - 1) = (a - 2)/(a - 1)\), as Cramer’s rule also gives. For \(a = 1\) the matrix has rank \(1\) and the augmented matrix has rank \(2\) (the minor \(\det \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\) from the first and third rows, first and last columns, is \(1\)), so by Theorem 11.6.5 there is no solution: the equations \(x + y + z = 1\) and \(x + y + z = 2\) contradict each other.

0303 Exercise 11.12.10

Compute the rank of

\[ A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \]

with the bordered minors theorem, and find a basis of its column space.

Solution â–¼

The minor \(M = \det \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr) = -1\) in rows \(1, 2\) and columns \(1, 2\) is nonzero. Its four bordering minors use row \(3\) or \(4\) and column \(3\) or \(4\); they all vanish, because row \(3\) is row \(1\) minus row \(2\) and row \(4\) is row \(1\) plus row \(2\), so each bordering \(3 \times 3\) matrix has dependent rows. So \(\operatorname {rank}A = 2\), and the first two columns form a basis of the column space (indeed column \(3\) is column \(1\) minus column \(2\), and column \(4\) equals column \(1\)).

0304 Exercise 11.12.11

Show that \(\operatorname {rank}(AB) \leq \min (\operatorname {rank}A, \operatorname {rank}B)\) and \(\operatorname {rank}(A + B) \leq \operatorname {rank}A + \operatorname {rank}B\) for matrices of compatible sizes, and that \(\operatorname {rank}(PAQ) = \operatorname {rank}A\) for invertible \(P\) and \(Q\). Deduce that every matrix of rank \(r\) can be written as \(P\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} I_r & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)Q\) with \(P\), \(Q\) invertible.

Solution â–¼

The image of \(AB\) is contained in the image of \(A\), and it is the image under \(A\) of the image of \(B\), whose dimension is at most \(\operatorname {rank}B\). The image of \(A + B\) is contained in \(\operatorname {im}A + \operatorname {im}B\). If \(P\) and \(Q\) are invertible, \(\operatorname {rank}(PAQ) \leq \operatorname {rank}A = \operatorname {rank}(P^{-1}(PAQ)Q^{-1}) \leq \operatorname {rank}(PAQ)\). For the normal form, bring \(A\) to reduced row echelon form by row operations, then use column operations (multiplication on the right) to move the pivot columns to the front and to clear the remaining entries of the first \(r\) rows, which are multiples of the pivot columns \(e_1, \ldots , e_r\).

03FH Exercise 11.12.12

Find the polynomial \(p \in \mathbb {Q}[x]\) of degree at most \(3\) with \(p(-1) = 2\), \(p(0) = 1\), \(p(1) = 2\) and \(p(2) = 11\), first with the Lagrange polynomials of Example 11.2.2(2), then by solving the linear system for the coefficients. Why is there exactly one?

Solution â–¼

With \(a_0, a_1, a_2, a_3 = -1, 0, 1, 2\):

\[ L_0 = -\tfrac 16x(x - 1)(x - 2), \quad L_1 = \tfrac 12(x + 1)(x - 1)(x - 2), \quad L_2 = -\tfrac 12(x + 1)x(x - 2), \quad L_3 = \tfrac 16(x + 1)x(x - 1), \]

and \(p = 2L_0 + L_1 + 2L_2 + 11L_3\). Expanding, the coefficients of \(x^3\) are \(-\frac13 + \frac12 - 1 + \frac{11}6 = 1\), of \(x^2\) are \(1 - 1 + 1 + 0 = 1\), of \(x\) are \(-\frac23 - \frac12 + 2 - \frac{11}6 = -1\), and the constant term is \(L_1(0) = 1\); so \(p = x^3 + x^2 - x + 1\). Directly, \(p = c_0 + c_1x + c_2x^2 + c_3x^3\) must satisfy \(c_0 = 1\), \(c_0 + c_1 + c_2 + c_3 = 2\), \(c_0 - c_1 + c_2 - c_3 = 2\) and \(c_0 + 2c_1 + 4c_2 + 8c_3 = 11\); the second and third equations give \(c_2 = 1\) and \(c_1 + c_3 = 0\), and the last then gives \(2c_1 + 8c_3 = 6\), so \(c_3 = 1\) and \(c_1 = -1\). There is exactly one solution because the Lagrange polynomials form a basis of \(\mathbb {Q}[x]_{\leq 3}\), in which the coordinates of \(p\) are its four prescribed values.

03FI Exercise 11.12.13

Let \(a_0, \ldots , a_d \in k\) be distinct and \(\mathrm{ev} \colon k[x]_{\leq d} \to k^{d+1}\), \(p \mapsto (p(a_0), \ldots , p(a_d))\).

  1. Show that \(\mathrm{ev}\) is linear and that its matrix with respect to the bases \(1, x, \ldots , x^d\) and \(e_0, \ldots , e_d\) is the Vandermonde matrix \(V = (a_i^j)_{0 \leq i, j \leq d}\).

  2. Deduce from the Vandermonde determinant (Example 11.5.4) that \(\mathrm{ev}\) is an isomorphism, which gives a second proof that interpolation is always possible and unique.

  3. Show that \(\mathrm{ev}^{-1}(e_i) = L_i\), so that the columns of \(V^{-1}\) are the coefficient vectors of the Lagrange polynomials.

  4. What happens if two of the \(a_i\) coincide?

Solution â–¼

(1) \((p + q)(a) = p(a) + q(a)\) and \((cp)(a) = cp(a)\). The \(j\)-th basis vector \(x^j\) is sent to \((a_0^j, \ldots , a_d^j)\), which is the \(j\)-th column of \(V\). (2) \(\det V = \prod _{i {\lt} j}(a_j - a_i) \neq 0\) because the \(a_i\) are distinct, so \(V\) is invertible, and \(\mathrm{ev}\) is a linear map between spaces of dimension \(d + 1\) with invertible matrix. So for each \((b_0, \ldots , b_d)\) there is exactly one \(p\) of degree at most \(d\) with \(p(a_i) = b_i\). (3) \(L_i(a_l) = 1\) for \(l = i\) and \(0\) otherwise, that is, \(\mathrm{ev}(L_i) = e_i\). The \(i\)-th column of \(V^{-1}\) is the coordinate vector of \(\mathrm{ev}^{-1}(e_i) = L_i\) in the basis of monomials. (4) If \(a_i = a_l\) with \(i \neq l\), two rows of \(V\) coincide and \(\det V = 0\): the values at \(a_i\) and \(a_l\) must agree, so \(\mathrm{ev}\) is not surjective, and since the dimensions agree it is not injective either: the nonzero polynomial \(\prod _{j \neq l}(x - a_j)\), of degree \(d\), vanishes at all the given points.

03FJ Exercise 11.12.14

With the notation of Example 11.2.2(2), show that \(\sum _{i=0}^dL_i = 1\), and more generally \(\sum _{i=0}^da_i^mL_i = x^m\) for \(0 \leq m \leq d\). By comparing coefficients of \(x^d\), deduce that

\[ \sum _{i=0}^d\frac{a_i^m}{\prod _{j \neq i}(a_i - a_j)} = \begin{cases} 0 & 0 \leq m {\lt} d,\\ 1 & m = d.\end{cases} \]

Check the case \(d = 1\) by hand.

Solution â–¼

Apply \(p = \sum _ip(a_i)L_i\) to \(p = x^m\), which has degree \(m \leq d\): this gives \(x^m = \sum _ia_i^mL_i\), and \(m = 0\) gives \(\sum _iL_i = 1\). The coefficient of \(x^d\) in \(L_i\) is \(1/\prod _{j \neq i}(a_i - a_j)\), since the numerator \(\prod _{j \neq i}(x - a_j)\) is monic of degree \(d\). The coefficient of \(x^d\) in \(x^m\) is \(0\) for \(m {\lt} d\) and \(1\) for \(m = d\). For \(d = 1\): \(\frac1{a_0 - a_1} + \frac1{a_1 - a_0} = 0\) and \(\frac{a_0}{a_0 - a_1} + \frac{a_1}{a_1 - a_0} = \frac{a_0 - a_1}{a_0 - a_1} = 1\).

03FK Exercise 11.12.15

Let \(a_0, \ldots , a_d \in k\) be distinct and \(f = \prod _i(x - a_i)\). Show that the map \(k[x]/(f) \to k^{d+1}\), \(p + (f) \mapsto (p(a_0), \ldots , p(a_d))\), is a well-defined isomorphism of rings (with the product taken componentwise on \(k^{d+1}\)), and that it is the Chinese remainder theorem (Theorem 8.3.3) for the ideals \((x - a_i)\). Show that the classes of the \(L_i\) are the idempotents corresponding to the \(e_i\), that is, \(L_i^2 \equiv L_i\), \(L_iL_l \equiv 0\) for \(i \neq l\), and \(\sum _iL_i \equiv 1\) modulo \(f\).

Solution â–¼

Evaluation at the \(a_i\) is a ring homomorphism \(k[x] \to k^{d+1}\) that kills \(f\), so it factors through \(k[x]/(f)\) (Theorem 8.3.2). The ideals \((x - a_i)\) are pairwise comaximal, since \((x - a_i) - (x - a_l) = a_l - a_i\) is a unit; their product, which equals their intersection, is \((f)\); and \(k[x]/(x - a_i) \cong k\) by \(p \mapsto p(a_i)\) (Corollary 8.6.7). So the Chinese remainder theorem gives exactly this isomorphism. Alternatively: by division with remainder every class has a unique representative of degree at most \(d\), and on \(k[x]_{\leq d}\) the map is the linear isomorphism of Exercise 11.12.13. The \(L_i\) map to \(e_i\), and \(e_i^2 = e_i\), \(e_ie_l = 0\) for \(i \neq l\), \(\sum _ie_i = (1, \ldots , 1)\); since the map is injective, the same relations hold for the classes of the \(L_i\).

03FL Exercise 11.12.16

(Secret sharing.) Let \(p\) be a prime, \(\mathbb {F}_p = \mathbb {Z}/p\mathbb {Z}\), and \(1 \leq t \leq n {\lt} p\). To share a secret \(s \in \mathbb {F}_p\) among \(n\) people so that any \(t\) of them together can recover it, choose \(c_1, \ldots , c_{t-1} \in \mathbb {F}_p\) at random, let \(q = s + c_1x + \cdots + c_{t-1}x^{t-1}\), and give person \(i\) the value \(q(i)\), for \(i = 1, \ldots , n\). Show:

  1. any \(t\) people can compute \(s\), by the formula \(s = \sum _iq(a_i)L_i(0)\) over their indices \(a_i\);

  2. any \(t - 1\) people learn nothing: for every candidate \(s^{\prime } \in \mathbb {F}_p\) there is exactly one polynomial of degree at most \(t - 1\) with constant term \(s^{\prime }\) taking the values they hold.

This is Shamir’s scheme, [Sha79].

Solution â–¼

(1) The indices \(1, \ldots , n\) are distinct in \(\mathbb {F}_p\) because \(n {\lt} p\). Given \(t\) distinct indices \(a_1, \ldots , a_t\) and the values \(q(a_i)\), Lagrange interpolation in \(\mathbb {F}_p[x]_{\leq t-1}\) gives \(q = \sum _iq(a_i)L_i\), and \(s = q(0)\). (2) Given \(t - 1\) indices \(a_1, \ldots , a_{t-1}\), all nonzero, and a candidate \(s^{\prime }\), interpolate at the \(t\) distinct points \(0, a_1, \ldots , a_{t-1}\) with the values \(s^{\prime }, q(a_1), \ldots , q(a_{t-1})\): there is exactly one polynomial of degree at most \(t - 1\) doing this. So, given what the \(t - 1\) people hold, each candidate secret is compatible with exactly one choice of \(c_1, \ldots , c_{t-1}\); since these are chosen uniformly at random, the shares make no value of the secret more likely than any other.
