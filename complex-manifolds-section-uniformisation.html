42.3 Uniformisation of Riemann surfaces02KX

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

A Riemann surface is a connected complex manifold of dimension \(1\). The uniformisation theorem reduces the study of all Riemann surfaces to three simply connected ones, the sphere, the plane and the disc, and to groups of automorphisms acting on them. Combined with the hyperbolic metric of the disc it shows that most Riemann surfaces, among them all compact ones of genus at least \(2\), carry a natural metric of constant curvature \(-1\); and it gives a short proof of Picard’s theorem. Throughout, \(\mathbb {D} = \{ |z| {\lt} 1\} \) and \(\mathrm{Aut}(X)\) is the group of biholomorphisms of \(X\) to itself.

02KY Lemma 42.3.1

Let \(X\) be a Riemann surface.

  1. If \(p \colon Y \to X\) is a covering map with \(Y\) connected, then \(Y\) has a unique structure of Riemann surface for which \(p\) is a local biholomorphism, and the deck transformations are biholomorphisms.

  2. If a group \(\Gamma \) acts on \(X\) freely and properly by biholomorphisms, then \(X/\Gamma \) has a unique structure of Riemann surface for which \(X \to X/\Gamma \) is a local biholomorphism, and a map \(f \colon X/\Gamma \to Z\) to a Riemann surface is holomorphic if and only if its composite with the projection is.

Proof â–¼

These are the proofs of Theorem 32.7.1 and Example 32.7.2, with “holomorphic” in place of “smooth”: the charts of \(Y\) (of \(X/\Gamma \)) are the charts of \(X\) composed with \(p\) on sheets over evenly covered chart domains (with local inverses of the projection), and their transition maps are transition maps of \(X\), composed with the biholomorphisms of \(\Gamma \) in the case of \(X/\Gamma \). Topologically, \(Y\) is a surface by Proposition 19.6.1(4), and \(X/\Gamma \) one by (3). A deck transformation \(\tau \) satisfies \(p \circ \tau = p\), so in these charts it is the identity map of coordinates.

In particular the universal covering \(\widetilde{X}\) of a Riemann surface (Theorem 18.6.5) is a simply connected Riemann surface, \(\pi _1(X)\) acts on it freely and properly by biholomorphisms as the deck group (Proposition 18.6.3), and \(X \cong \widetilde{X}/\pi _1(X)\).

02KZ Theorem 42.3.2 Uniformisation theorem

Every simply connected Riemann surface is biholomorphic to exactly one of \(\mathbb {C}\mathbb {P}^1\), \(\mathbb {C}\) and \(\mathbb {D}\).

This is due to Koebe and Poincaré (1907); see [For81]. The three surfaces are pairwise non-biholomorphic: \(\mathbb {C}\mathbb {P}^1\) is compact and the others are not, and a biholomorphism \(\mathbb {C}\to \mathbb {D}\) would be a bounded nonconstant entire function (Exercise 28.7.1). For open subsets of \(\mathbb {C}\) the theorem is the Riemann mapping theorem (Theorem 28.6.5); the general case constructs the biholomorphism from Green’s functions, using Perron’s method to solve the Dirichlet problem on the surface.

02L0 Lemma 42.3.3

  1. \(\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb {C}\mathbb {P}^1)\) consists of the Möbius maps \(z \mapsto (az + b)/(cz + d)\), \(ad - bc \neq 0\), and each of them has a fixed point.

  2. \(\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb {C})\) consists of the maps \(z \mapsto az + b\), \(a \neq 0\); those without fixed points are the translations \(z \mapsto z + b\), \(b \neq 0\).

  3. \(\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb {D})\) consists of the maps \(z \mapsto \lambda (z - a)/(1 - \bar az)\) with \(|\lambda | = 1\) and \(a \in \mathbb {D}\), and acts transitively on \(\mathbb {D}\).

Proof â–¼

(2) is Exercise 28.7.2, and \(az + b = z\) has the solution \(b/(1 - a)\) when \(a \neq 1\). (3) is Proposition 28.6.2; the map \(\phi _{-a}\) sends \(0\) to \(a\). (1) A Möbius map is holomorphic in the charts of \(\mathbb {C}\mathbb {P}^1\), with the Möbius inverse \(z \mapsto (dz - b)/(-cz + a)\), so it is an automorphism, and the Möbius maps act transitively (\(z \mapsto z + b\) and \(z \mapsto 1/z\) move any point to any other). Given \(f \in \mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb {C}\mathbb {P}^1)\), choose a Möbius \(m\) with \(m(f(\infty )) = \infty \); then \(m \circ f\) restricts to an automorphism of \(\mathbb {C}= \mathbb {C}\mathbb {P}^1 \setminus \{ \infty \} \), hence is \(z \mapsto az + b\) by (2), and \(f = m^{-1} \circ (m \circ f)\) is Möbius. If \(c = 0\), \(\infty \) is a fixed point; if \(c \neq 0\), the equation \(cz^2 + (d - a)z - b = 0\) has a root \(z_0 \in \mathbb {C}\) (Corollary 28.4.3(2)), and \(cz_0 + d \neq 0\), since at \(z_0 = -d/c\) the left side is \((ad - bc)/c \neq 0\); so \(z_0\) is a fixed point.

02L1 Proposition 42.3.4 The Poincaré metric

Let \(g_{\mathbb {D}} = 4|dz|^2/(1 - |z|^2)^2\) on \(\mathbb {D}\), that is \(4(dx^2 + dy^2)/(1 - x^2 - y^2)^2\).

  1. (Schwarz–Pick) For every holomorphic \(f \colon \mathbb {D} \to \mathbb {D}\),

    \[ \frac{|f^{\prime }(z)|}{1 - |f(z)|^2} \leq \frac1{1 - |z|^2}, \]

    that is, \(f^*g_{\mathbb {D}} \leq g_{\mathbb {D}}\): holomorphic self-maps of the disc do not increase lengths of curves, hence distances.

  2. Every automorphism of \(\mathbb {D}\) is an isometry of \(g_{\mathbb {D}}\).

  3. \(g_{\mathbb {D}}\) is complete, with constant curvature \(-1\).

Proof â–¼

(1) Fix \(a \in \mathbb {D}\), let \(b = f(a)\) and \(\phi _a(z) = (z - a)/(1 - \bar az)\). A computation gives \(\phi _a^{\prime }(z) = (1 - |a|^2)/(1 - \bar az)^2\), so \(\phi _{-a}^{\prime }(0) = 1 - |a|^2\) and \(\phi _b^{\prime }(b) = 1/(1 - |b|^2)\). The map \(h = \phi _b \circ f \circ \phi _{-a}\) sends \(\mathbb {D}\) to \(\mathbb {D}\) and \(0\) to \(0\), so \(|h^{\prime }(0)| \leq 1\) by Schwarz’s lemma (Lemma 28.6.1), and by the chain rule \(h^{\prime }(0) = f^{\prime }(a)(1 - |a|^2)/(1 - |b|^2)\). At a point \(z\), \(f^*g_{\mathbb {D}}\) is \(4|f^{\prime }(z)|^2|dz|^2/(1 - |f(z)|^2)^2\), so the inequality says \(f^*g_{\mathbb {D}} \leq g_{\mathbb {D}}\), and lengths of curves decrease.

(2) Apply (1) to an automorphism and to its inverse: \(f^*g_{\mathbb {D}} \leq g_{\mathbb {D}}\) and \(g_{\mathbb {D}} = (f^{-1})^*f^*g_{\mathbb {D}}\) force equality.

(3) The curvature is \(-1\) by Proposition 39.10.6 (Example 39.10.7(2)). For completeness, choose \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\) such that \(\exp _0\) is defined on the ball of radius \(\varepsilon \) in \(T_0\mathbb {D}\). Since \(\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb {D})\) acts transitively by isometries, and isometries map geodesics to geodesics, the same \(\varepsilon \) works at every point: every geodesic of unit speed is defined on an interval of length \(\varepsilon \) around each of its times. So a maximal unit speed geodesic defined on \([0, T)\) with \(T {\lt} \infty \) could be continued beyond \(T\) from its point at time \(T - \varepsilon /2\), a contradiction. Geodesics are defined for all time, and \(g_{\mathbb {D}}\) is complete by Theorem 39.5.7.

02L2 Theorem 42.3.5

Every Riemann surface \(X\) is biholomorphic to \(\widetilde{X}/\Gamma \), where \(\widetilde{X} \in \{ \mathbb {C}\mathbb {P}^1, \mathbb {C}, \mathbb {D}\} \) is its universal covering and \(\Gamma \cong \pi _1(X)\) is a group of automorphisms of \(\widetilde{X}\) acting freely and properly. Moreover:

  1. \(\widetilde{X} = \mathbb {C}\mathbb {P}^1\) if and only if \(X \cong \mathbb {C}\mathbb {P}^1\);

  2. \(\widetilde{X} = \mathbb {C}\) if and only if \(X\) is biholomorphic to \(\mathbb {C}\), to \(\mathbb {C}^\times \) or to a complex torus \(\mathbb {C}/\Lambda \);

  3. in all other cases \(\widetilde{X} = \mathbb {D}\), and \(X\) carries a unique complete metric of constant curvature \(-1\) for which the projection \(\mathbb {D} \to X\) is a local isometry; in holomorphic coordinates it has the form \(\lambda (z)|dz|^2\).

Proof â–¼

The first statement is Theorem 42.3.2 and the remark after Lemma 42.3.1. (1) By Lemma 42.3.3(1) every automorphism of \(\mathbb {C}\mathbb {P}^1\) has a fixed point, so a group acting freely on it is trivial. (2) By Lemma 42.3.3(2), \(\Gamma \) consists of translations \(z \mapsto z + \lambda \), and \(\Lambda = \{ \lambda \} \) is a subgroup of \(\mathbb {C}= \mathbb {R}^2\). It is discrete: it is the orbit of \(0\), which is a fibre of the covering \(\mathbb {C}\to X\). By Lemma 33.3.10, \(\Lambda \) is \(0\), \(\mathbb {Z}\omega \) with \(\omega \neq 0\), or a lattice. In the first case \(X \cong \mathbb {C}\); in the second, \(z \mapsto e^{2\pi iz/\omega }\) is a covering \(\mathbb {C}\to \mathbb {C}^\times \) whose fibres are the cosets of \(\mathbb {Z}\omega \), so it induces a bijective local biholomorphism \(\mathbb {C}/\mathbb {Z}\omega \to \mathbb {C}^\times \); in the third \(X\) is a complex torus. Conversely these surfaces have universal covering \(\mathbb {C}\). (3) The group \(\Gamma \subseteq \mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb {D})\) acts by isometries of \(g_{\mathbb {D}}\) (Proposition 42.3.4), so \(g_{\mathbb {D}}\) descends to a metric on \(X\) making the projection a local isometry (Proposition 39.2.4), of curvature \(-1\); it is complete because geodesics of \(X\) lift to geodesics of the complete \(\mathbb {D}\) (as in the proof of Theorem 39.8.6), and in the holomorphic coordinates given by local inverses of the projection it is \(4|dz|^2/(1 - |z|^2)^2\). It is unique because the projection is a local diffeomorphism, so the metric on \(X\) is determined by its pullback \(g_{\mathbb {D}}\).

02L3 Corollary 42.3.6

Let \(X\) be a compact Riemann surface of genus \(g\) (Theorem 19.7.6; \(X\) is orientable). If \(g = 0\) then \(X \cong \mathbb {C}\mathbb {P}^1\); if \(g = 1\) then \(X\) is a complex torus \(\mathbb {C}/\Lambda \); and if \(g \geq 2\) then \(X \cong \mathbb {D}/\Gamma \), and \(X\) carries a metric of constant curvature \(-1\), compatible with its complex structure, with area \(4\pi (g - 1)\).

Proof â–¼

If \(g = 0\), \(X\) is simply connected (Corollary 18.5.2), so \(X\) is its own universal covering, compact, hence \(\mathbb {C}\mathbb {P}^1\). If \(g \geq 1\), \(\pi _1(X)\) is nontrivial, as its abelianisation \(H_1(X; \mathbb {Z})\) (Theorem 20.9.1) is \(\mathbb {Z}^{2g}\) (Example 20.7.5), so \(\widetilde{X} \neq \mathbb {C}\mathbb {P}^1\) by Theorem 42.3.5(1). If \(\widetilde{X} = \mathbb {D}\), the metric of curvature \(-1\) has area \(A\) with \(-A = 2\pi (2 - 2g)\) by the Gauss–Bonnet theorem (Theorem 39.7.9), so \(A = 4\pi (g - 1)\) and \(g \geq 2\). If \(\widetilde{X} = \mathbb {C}\), then \(X\) is compact among \(\mathbb {C}\), \(\mathbb {C}^\times \) and the tori, so it is a torus, of genus \(1\). Hence \(g = 1\) forces \(\widetilde{X} = \mathbb {C}\) and \(g \geq 2\) forces \(\widetilde{X} = \mathbb {D}\).

02L4 Corollary 42.3.7 Little Picard theorem

A nonconstant entire function takes every complex value with at most one exception.

Proof â–¼

Suppose the entire function \(f\) omits two values; composing with an affine map we may assume they are \(0\) and \(1\), so \(f \colon \mathbb {C}\to X = \mathbb {C}\setminus \{ 0, 1\} \). The fundamental group of \(X\) is not abelian: \(X = U \cup V\) with \(U = \{ \operatorname {Re}z {\lt} \frac34\} \setminus \{ 0\} \) and \(V = \{ \operatorname {Re}z {\gt} \frac14\} \setminus \{ 1\} \), where \(U \cap V\) is a convex strip, hence simply connected, and \(U\), \(V\) deformation retract onto the circles of radius \(\frac14\) around \(0\) and \(1\) (radially, along segments that stay in the convex half planes), so \(\pi _1(U) \cong \pi _1(V) \cong \mathbb {Z}\) (Lemma 18.2.6 and Theorem 18.4.6) and \(\pi _1(X) \cong \mathbb {Z}* \mathbb {Z}\) by Corollary 18.5.2, a non-abelian group (Example 18.5.3). Since \(X\) is not compact and \(\pi _1(X)\) is not abelian, while \(\mathbb {C}\), \(\mathbb {C}^\times \) and the tori have abelian fundamental groups \(0\), \(\mathbb {Z}\) and \(\mathbb {Z}^2\), Theorem 42.3.5 gives a covering map \(p \colon \mathbb {D} \to X\) that is a local biholomorphism. As \(\mathbb {C}\) is simply connected, \(f\) lifts to a continuous \(\tilde f \colon \mathbb {C}\to \mathbb {D}\) with \(p \circ \tilde f = f\) (Proposition 18.6.1), which is holomorphic, being locally a local inverse of \(p\) composed with \(f\). Then \(\tilde f\) is a bounded entire function, hence constant (Corollary 28.4.3), and so is \(f\).

02L5 Remark 42.3.8

For compact surfaces the three cases match the sign of the Euler characteristic: \(\mathbb {C}\mathbb {P}^1\) carries the round metric of curvature \(1\) (Example 39.10.7(3)), complex tori the flat metric \(|dz|^2\), and surfaces of genus \(g \geq 2\) the hyperbolic metric, all compatible with the complex structure; the sign of the curvature is forced by the Gauss–Bonnet theorem. Schwarz–Pick also shows that the hyperbolic metric is intrinsic: a holomorphic map \(f \colon X \to Y\) between Riemann surfaces covered by the disc lifts to a holomorphic map \(\mathbb {D} \to \mathbb {D}\) of the universal coverings (Proposition 18.6.1), so it does not increase the lengths of curves for the hyperbolic metrics of Theorem 42.3.5(3).
