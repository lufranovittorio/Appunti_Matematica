11.11 Complex structures00JR

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

00JS Definition 11.11.1

Let \(V\) be a real vector space. Its complexification is \(V_\mathbb {C}= V \otimes _\mathbb {R}\mathbb {C}\), a complex vector space with the complex conjugation \(\overline{v \otimes z} = v \otimes \bar z\), which is \(\mathbb {R}\)-linear and conjugate-linear over \(\mathbb {C}\). A complex structure on \(V\) is an \(\mathbb {R}\)-linear map \(J \colon V \to V\) with \(J^2 = -\mathrm{id}\).

00JT Proposition 11.11.2

Let \(J\) be a complex structure on a finite-dimensional real vector space \(V\).

  1. \(V\) becomes a complex vector space with \((a + bi)v = av + bJv\); hence \(\dim _\mathbb {R}V = 2\dim _\mathbb {C}V\) is even.

  2. Extend \(J\) \(\mathbb {C}\)-linearly to \(V_\mathbb {C}\). Then \(V_\mathbb {C}= V^{1,0} \oplus V^{0,1}\), where \(V^{1,0} = \ker (J - i)\) and \(V^{0,1} = \ker (J + i)\), and \(\overline{V^{1,0}} = V^{0,1}\).

  3. The map \(V \to V^{1,0}\), \(v \mapsto \frac12(v - iJv)\), is an isomorphism of complex vector spaces from \((V, J)\) to \(V^{1,0}\), and \(v \mapsto \frac12(v + iJv)\) is a conjugate-linear isomorphism \(V \to V^{0,1}\).

Proof â–¼

(1) The axioms of a module over \(\mathbb {C}= \mathbb {R}[x]/(x^2 + 1)\) follow from \(J^2 = -1\) (Example 8.7.2 and Exercise 8.12.2); a complex basis \((v_j)\) gives the real basis \((v_j, Jv_j)\). (2) The polynomial \(x^2 + 1 = (x - i)(x + i)\) kills \(J\) on \(V_\mathbb {C}\), and the kernel lemma (Lemma 11.7.5) gives the decomposition. Conjugation commutes with \(J\) (which is real), so it exchanges the \(\pm i\)-eigenspaces. (3) \(J(v - iJv) = Jv + iv = i(v - iJv)\), so the image lies in \(V^{1,0}\), and \(J\) on \(V\) corresponds to multiplication by \(i\). The map is injective because \(v\) is recovered as twice the real part, and both sides have complex dimension \(\frac12\dim _\mathbb {R}V\). The second map is the conjugate of the first.

00JU Proposition 11.11.3

Let \(J\) be a complex structure on a real inner product space \((V, g)\) with \(g(Jv, Jw) = g(v, w)\). Put \(\omega (v, w) = g(Jv, w)\) and \(h = g - i\omega \). Then \(\omega \) is a nondegenerate alternating form with \(\omega (Jv, Jw) = \omega (v, w)\), and \(h\) is a Hermitian inner product on the complex vector space \((V, J)\), whose real part is \(g\). Conversely, \(g\) and \(J\) determine \(\omega \), and \(\omega \) and \(J\) determine \(g(v, w) = \omega (v, Jw)\).

Proof â–¼

From \(J\)-invariance, \(g(Jv, w) = g(J^2 v, Jw) = -g(v, Jw)\). Hence \(\omega (w, v) = g(Jw, v) = -g(w, Jv) = -\omega (v, w)\), so \(\omega \) is alternating; \(\omega (Jv, Jw) = g(J(Jv), Jw) = g(Jv, w) = \omega (v, w)\) by \(J\)-invariance of \(g\); and \(\omega \) is nondegenerate because \(\omega (v, Jv) = g(Jv, Jv) {\gt} 0\) for \(v \neq 0\). For \(h\): \(h(v, w) = g(v, w) + i g(v, Jw)\). Then \(h(Jv, w) = g(Jv, w) + ig(Jv, Jw) = -g(v, Jw) + ig(v, w) = ih(v, w)\), so \(h\) is complex linear in the first variable; \(h(w, v) = g(v, w) - ig(v, Jw) = \overline{h(v, w)}\); and \(h(v, v) = g(v, v) {\gt} 0\) for \(v \neq 0\) since \(g(v, Jv) = -g(Jv, v) = -g(v, Jv)\) vanishes. Finally \(\omega (v, Jw) = g(Jv, Jw) = g(v, w)\).

00JV Example 11.11.4

On \(\mathbb {R}^{2n}\) with coordinates \((x_1, y_1, \ldots , x_n, y_n)\), the standard complex structure is \(J(\partial _{x_j}) = \partial _{y_j}\), \(J(\partial _{y_j}) = -\partial _{x_j}\) (writing \(\partial _{x_j}\), \(\partial _{y_j}\) for the standard basis vectors), which identifies \((\mathbb {R}^{2n}, J)\) with \(\mathbb {C}^n\) via \(x + iy\). The standard inner product is \(J\)-invariant, the associated form satisfies \(\omega (\partial _{x_j}, \partial _{y_j}) = g(\partial _{y_j}, \partial _{y_j}) = 1\), so the \(\partial _{x_j}\), \(\partial _{y_j}\) form a symplectic basis, and \(h\) is the standard Hermitian inner product of \(\mathbb {C}^n\). The vectors \(\partial _{z_j} = \frac12(\partial _{x_j} - i\partial _{y_j})\) span \(V^{1,0}\). These formulas reappear as the local model of Kähler manifolds (Chapter 47).

Proposition 11.11.3 relates three structures on \(V\): an inner product \(g\), a nondegenerate alternating form \(\omega \) and a complex structure \(J\). We call a triple \((g, \omega , J)\) of such structures compatible if \(g(Jv, Jw) = g(v, w)\) and \(\omega (v, w) = g(Jv, w)\) for all \(v, w\). The next proposition says that any two members of a compatible triple determine the third, and says exactly which pairs occur. This “two out of three” property is the linear algebra behind Hermitian and Kähler manifolds (Chapter 47) and behind the compatible complex structures of symplectic geometry (Chapter 41).

02ZY Proposition 11.11.5 Two out of three

Let \(V\) be a finite-dimensional real vector space.

  1. In a compatible triple \((g, \omega , J)\) each member is determined by the other two:

    \[ \omega (v, w) = g(Jv, w), \qquad g(v, w) = \omega (v, Jw), \qquad J \text{ is the unique endomorphism with } \omega (v, w) = g(Jv, w). \]

  2. A pair extends to a compatible triple exactly in the following cases.

    1. An inner product \(g\) and a complex structure \(J\): if and only if \(g(Jv, Jw) = g(v, w)\).

    2. A nondegenerate alternating form \(\omega \) and a complex structure \(J\): if and only if \(\omega (Jv, Jw) = \omega (v, w)\) and \(\omega (v, Jv) {\gt} 0\) for all \(v \neq 0\).

    3. An inner product \(g\) and a nondegenerate alternating form \(\omega \): let \(A\) be the endomorphism with \(\omega (v, w) = g(Av, w)\); the pair extends if and only if \(A^2 = -1\), equivalently, if and only if \(A\) is orthogonal for \(g\); and then \(J = A\).

  3. Let \((g, \omega , J)\) be compatible, and \(h = g - i\omega \) the Hermitian inner product of Proposition 11.11.3. An automorphism of \(V\) preserving two of \(g\), \(\omega \), \(J\) (preserving \(J\) meaning \(TJ = JT\)) preserves the third. So the three groups \(\mathrm{O}(V, g)\), \(\mathrm{Sp}(V, \omega )\) and \(\mathrm{GL}(V, J)\) of automorphisms preserving \(g\), \(\omega \) and \(J\) satisfy

    \[ \mathrm{O}(V, g) \cap \mathrm{Sp}(V, \omega ) = \mathrm{O}(V, g) \cap \mathrm{GL}(V, J) = \mathrm{Sp}(V, \omega ) \cap \mathrm{GL}(V, J) = \mathrm{U}(V, h), \]

    the unitary group of \(h\) on the complex vector space \((V, J)\). For \(\mathbb {R}^{2n} = \mathbb {C}^n\) with its standard structures this reads \(\mathrm{O}(2n) \cap \mathrm{Sp}_{2n}(\mathbb {R}) = \mathrm{O}(2n) \cap \mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb {C}) = \mathrm{Sp}_{2n}(\mathbb {R}) \cap \mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb {C}) = \mathrm{U}(n)\).

Proof â–¼

(1) The first two formulas are in Proposition 11.11.3. If \(g(Jv, w) = g(J^{\prime }v, w)\) for all \(w\), then \(Jv = J^{\prime }v\), as \(g\) is nondegenerate.

(2)(a) is Proposition 11.11.3. For (b), if the triple is compatible, \(\omega \) is \(J\)-invariant by that proposition and \(\omega (v, Jv) = g(Jv, Jv) {\gt} 0\) for \(v \neq 0\). Conversely, define \(g(v, w) = \omega (v, Jw)\). Using \(J\)-invariance of \(\omega \) and \(J^2 = -1\),

\[ g(w, v) = \omega (w, Jv) = \omega (Jw, J^2v) = -\omega (Jw, v) = \omega (v, Jw) = g(v, w), \]

so \(g\) is symmetric; it is positive definite by hypothesis; \(g(Jv, Jw) = \omega (Jv, J^2w) = -\omega (Jv, w) = \omega (w, Jv) = g(w, v) = g(v, w)\); and \(g(Jv, w) = \omega (Jv, Jw) = \omega (v, w)\). So \((g, \omega , J)\) is compatible. For (c), \(v \mapsto \omega (v, \cdot )\) is a linear form, represented through \(g\) by a unique vector \(Av\) (Proposition 11.4.2(1) and nondegeneracy of \(g\)), and \(A\) is linear. It is skew-adjoint: \(g(Av, w) = \omega (v, w) = -\omega (w, v) = -g(Aw, v) = -g(v, Aw)\). If the pair extends, \(J = A\) by (1), so \(A^2 = -1\). Conversely, if \(A^2 = -1\), then \(J = A\) is a complex structure, \(g(Jv, Jw) = -g(v, J^2w) = g(v, w)\) by skew-adjointness, and \(\omega (v, w) = g(Jv, w)\) by definition. Finally, the adjoint of \(A\) is \(-A\), so \(A\) is orthogonal, \(A^*A = 1\), if and only if \(-A^2 = 1\).

(3) Let \(T\) be an automorphism. If \(T\) preserves \(g\) and commutes with \(J\), then \(\omega (Tv, Tw) = g(JTv, Tw) = g(TJv, Tw) = g(Jv, w) = \omega (v, w)\). If \(T\) preserves \(\omega \) and commutes with \(J\), then \(g(Tv, Tw) = \omega (Tv, JTw) = \omega (Tv, TJw) = \omega (v, Jw) = g(v, w)\). If \(T\) preserves \(g\) and \(\omega \), then for all \(v, w\)

\[ g(JTv, Tw) = \omega (Tv, Tw) = \omega (v, w) = g(Jv, w) = g(TJv, Tw), \]

and since \(Tw\) runs through all of \(V\), \(JTv = TJv\). This proves the equality of the three intersections. An element of \(\mathrm{GL}(V, J)\) is a complex linear automorphism of \((V, J)\); it preserves \(g\) and \(\omega \) if and only if it preserves \(h = g - i\omega \), that is, if and only if it is unitary. For the standard structures on \(\mathbb {R}^{2n}\), \(\mathrm{GL}(V, J) = \mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb {C})\) and \(\mathrm{U}(V, h) = \mathrm{U}(n)\) (Example 11.11.4).

02ZZ Example 11.11.6

  1. On \(\mathbb {R}^2\) with the standard inner product \(g\) and \(\omega = c\, dx \wedge dy\), \(c {\gt} 0\), the endomorphism \(A\) of (2)(c) is \(c\) times the rotation by \(\pi /2\): \(\omega (e_1, e_2) = c = g(Ae_1, e_2)\). So \(A^2 = -c^2\), and the pair \((g, \omega )\) is compatible only for \(c = 1\). For \(c \neq 1\), rescaling \(g\) by \(c\) makes it compatible with \(\omega \) and \(J = A/c\). Similarly \((\omega , J)\) with \(\omega = dx \wedge dy\) and \(J\) the rotation by \(-\pi /2\) satisfies \(\omega (Jv, Jw) = \omega (v, w)\) but \(\omega (v, Jv) {\lt} 0\): the condition of positivity in (2)(b) singles out one of the two orientations.

  2. A pair that is not compatible. On \(\mathbb {R}^4\) with the standard inner product, let \(\omega = dx^1 \wedge dy^1 + 2\, dx^2 \wedge dy^2\). Then \(A\) is the rotation by \(\pi /2\) in the first plane and twice the rotation by \(\pi /2\) in the second, \(A^2 = \operatorname {diag}(-1, -1, -4, -4)\), and no complex structure is compatible with both \(g\) and \(\omega \).

0300 Remark 11.11.7

An incompatible pair \((g, \omega )\) can be repaired canonically. With \(A\) as in (2)(c), \(-A^2 = A^*A\) is self-adjoint and positive definite, so by the spectral theorem (Theorem 11.10.6) it has a unique positive definite square root \(P\), which acts on each eigenspace of \(-A^2\) as the positive square root of the eigenvalue; being a polynomial in \(A^2\), \(P\) commutes with \(A\). Put \(J = AP^{-1}\) (the polar decomposition of \(A\)). Then \(J^2 = A^2P^{-2} = -1\); using that \(P\) is self-adjoint, that \(A\) is skew-adjoint and that \(AP = PA\),

\[ \omega (Jv, Jw) = g(A^2P^{-1}v, AP^{-1}w) = -g(Pv, AP^{-1}w) = -g(v, Aw) = \omega (v, w), \qquad \omega (v, Jv) = g(Av, AP^{-1}v) = g(Pv, v) {\gt} 0. \]

So \(J\) is compatible with \(\omega \) in the sense of (2)(b), with the new inner product \(\omega (v, Jw) = g(Pv, w)\), and \(J = A\) when \((g, \omega )\) was already compatible. Since \(J\) depends only on \(g\) and \(\omega \), and continuously so, this produces compatible complex structures on every symplectic vector space, and indeed compatible almost complex structures on every symplectic manifold, from any Riemannian metric [MS17]. In Example 11.11.6(2), \(P = \operatorname {diag}(1, 1, 2, 2)\) and \(J\) is the standard complex structure.
