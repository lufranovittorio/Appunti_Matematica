17.7 Complete and compact metric spaces00SG

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

00SH Definition 17.7.1

A sequence \((x_n)\) in a metric space is Cauchy if for every \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\) there is \(N\) with \(d(x_m, x_n) {\lt} \varepsilon \) for \(m, n \geq N\). The space is complete if every Cauchy sequence converges. It is totally bounded if for every \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\) it is covered by finitely many balls of radius \(\varepsilon \). A Banach space is a normed space that is complete for the metric of its norm.

00SI Proposition 17.7.2

  1. \(\mathbb {R}^n\) and \(\mathbb {C}^n\) are complete, and so are all their closed subsets.

  2. A complete subspace of a metric space is closed; a closed subspace of a complete space is complete.

  3. For a nonempty set \(X\) and a complete metric space \((Y, d)\), the set \(B(X, Y)\) of maps with bounded image, with \(d_\infty (f, g) = \sup _x d(f(x), g(x))\), is a complete metric space. If \(X\) is a space, the subset \(C_b(X, Y)\) of continuous maps is closed, hence complete. In particular \(C_b(X, \mathbb {K})\) with the norm \(\| f\| _\infty = \sup |f|\) is a Banach space.

Proof â–¼

(2) If \(A \subseteq X\) is complete and \(x \in \overline{A}\), a sequence in \(A\) converging to \(x\) (Proposition 17.3.5) is Cauchy, so it converges in \(A\), and by uniqueness of limits \(x \in A\). If \(X\) is complete and \(A\) closed, a Cauchy sequence in \(A\) converges in \(X\), and its limit lies in \(\overline{A} = A\).

(1) With the maximum norm, a sequence is Cauchy if and only if each coordinate sequence is, and converges if and only if each coordinate does. Real Cauchy sequences converge by Proposition 4.7.6, and complex ones because real and imaginary parts are Cauchy. The second part follows from (2).

(3) For bounded \(f, g\), \(d_\infty (f, g)\) is finite by the triangle inequality, and \(d_\infty \) is a metric. Let \((f_n)\) be Cauchy. For each \(x\), \((f_n(x))\) is Cauchy in \(Y\); let \(f(x)\) be its limit. Given \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\) choose \(N\) with \(d(f_n(x), f_m(x)) {\lt} \varepsilon \) for all \(x\) and \(m, n \geq N\). Since \(|d(a, b) - d(a, c)| \leq d(b, c)\), letting \(m \to \infty \) gives \(d(f_n(x), f(x)) \leq \varepsilon \) for all \(x\) and \(n \geq N\). So \(f\) is bounded and \(f_n \to f\) in \(d_\infty \). Uniform limits of continuous maps are continuous (Proposition 17.3.8), so \(C_b(X, Y)\) is closed.

00SJ Lemma 17.7.3 Lebesgue number lemma

Let \(X\) be a metric space in which every sequence has a convergent subsequence, and \(\mathcal{U}\) an open cover. There is \(\delta {\gt} 0\) such that every ball of radius \(\delta \) is contained in some member of \(\mathcal{U}\).

Proof â–¼

Otherwise choose \(x_n\) such that \(B(x_n, 1/n)\) lies in no member of \(\mathcal{U}\), and a subsequence \(x_{n_k} \to x\). Let \(x \in U \in \mathcal{U}\) and \(B(x, 2\varepsilon ) \subseteq U\). For \(k\) large, \(d(x_{n_k}, x) {\lt} \varepsilon \) and \(1/n_k {\lt} \varepsilon \), so \(B(x_{n_k}, 1/n_k) \subseteq B(x, 2\varepsilon ) \subseteq U\), a contradiction.

00SK Theorem 17.7.4

For a metric space \(X\) the following are equivalent:

  1. \(X\) is compact;

  2. every sequence in \(X\) has a convergent subsequence (\(X\) is sequentially compact);

  3. \(X\) is complete and totally bounded.

Proof â–¼

(1)\(\Rightarrow \)(2): Let \((x_n)\) have no convergent subsequence. Then every \(x \in X\) has an open ball containing \(x_n\) for only finitely many \(n\): otherwise we could choose \(n_1 {\lt} n_2 {\lt} \cdots \) with \(d(x_{n_k}, x) {\lt} 1/k\). Finitely many such balls cover \(X\), but together they contain \(x_n\) for only finitely many \(n\), a contradiction.

(2)\(\Rightarrow \)(3): A Cauchy sequence with a convergent subsequence converges to the same limit. If \(X\) were not totally bounded, for some \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\) we could choose inductively \(x_n \notin \bigcup _{m {\lt} n} B(x_m, \varepsilon )\); this sequence has no Cauchy subsequence.

(3)\(\Rightarrow \)(2): Let \((x_n)\) be a sequence. Cover \(X\) by finitely many balls of radius \(1\); one of them contains \(x_n\) for infinitely many \(n\). Among these indices, cover by balls of radius \(1/2\) and choose again, and so on; this gives nested infinite index sets \(N_1 \supseteq N_2 \supseteq \cdots \) such that \(d(x_m, x_n) {\lt} 2/k\) for \(m, n \in N_k\). Choosing \(n_k \in N_k\) increasing gives a Cauchy subsequence, which converges.

(2)\(\Rightarrow \)(1): Let \(\mathcal{U}\) be an open cover and \(\delta \) a Lebesgue number (Lemma 17.7.3). By (2)\(\Rightarrow \)(3), \(X\) is covered by finitely many balls of radius \(\delta \), each contained in a member of \(\mathcal{U}\).

00SL Corollary 17.7.5

A continuous map \(f \colon X \to Y\) from a compact metric space to a metric space is uniformly continuous: for every \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\) there is \(\delta {\gt} 0\) such that \(d(x, x') {\lt} \delta \) implies \(d(f(x), f(x')) {\lt} \varepsilon \).

Proof â–¼

Let \(\delta \) be a Lebesgue number of the cover by the sets \(f^{-1}(B(y, \varepsilon /2))\). If \(d(x, x') {\lt} \delta \), then \(x, x' \in B(x, \delta )\) lie in one such set, so \(d(f(x), f(x')) {\lt} \varepsilon \).

00SM Theorem 17.7.6 Banach fixed point theorem

Let \(X\) be a nonempty complete metric space and \(T \colon X \to X\) a contraction: \(d(Tx, Ty) \leq c\, d(x, y)\) for some \(0 \leq c {\lt} 1\). Then \(T\) has a unique fixed point, and for every \(x_0\) the sequence \(x_{n+1} = Tx_n\) converges to it.

Proof â–¼

By induction \(d(x_{n+1}, x_n) \leq c^n d(x_1, x_0)\), so for \(m {\gt} n\), \(d(x_m, x_n) \leq \sum _{k=n}^{m-1} c^k d(x_1, x_0) = c^n\frac{1 - c^{m-n}}{1 - c}d(x_1, x_0) \leq \frac{c^n}{1 - c}d(x_1, x_0)\). Since \(c^n \to 0\) (if \(c {\gt} 0\), write \(1/c = 1 + h\) with \(h {\gt} 0\); then \(c^{-n} \geq 1 + nh\)), the sequence is Cauchy; let \(x\) be its limit. A contraction is continuous, so \(Tx = \lim Tx_n = \lim x_{n+1} = x\). If also \(Ty = y\), then \(d(x, y) \leq c\, d(x, y)\), so \(x = y\).

00SN Theorem 17.7.7 Baire category theorem

In a complete metric space \(X\), a countable intersection of dense open sets is dense. Equivalently, a countable union of closed sets with empty interior has empty interior.

Proof â–¼

Let \(U_1, U_2, \ldots \) be dense open and \(W\) nonempty open. Since \(W \cap U_1\) is nonempty and open, it contains a ball \(B(x_1, 2r_1)\) with \(r_1 {\lt} 1\), and then the closed ball \(\bar{B}(x_1, r_1) = \{ y : d(x_1, y) \leq r_1\} \). Inductively, \(B(x_n, r_n) \cap U_{n+1}\) is nonempty and open, so it contains \(\bar{B}(x_{n+1}, r_{n+1})\) with \(r_{n+1} {\lt} 1/(n+1)\). For \(m \geq n\), \(x_m \in B(x_n, r_n)\), so \((x_n)\) is Cauchy; its limit \(x\) lies in every closed set \(\bar{B}(x_n, r_n)\), hence in \(W \cap \bigcap _n U_n\). The second statement is the first for the complements.

00SO Corollary 17.7.8

A nonempty complete metric space in which no point is isolated (no singleton is open) is uncountable.

Proof â–¼

If \(X = \{ x_1, x_2, \ldots \} \), then \(X\) is the countable union of the closed sets \(\{ x_n\} \), which have empty interior, contradicting Theorem 17.7.7.

00SP Counterexample 17.7.9

Completeness is not a topological property: \(t \mapsto t/(1 - |t|)\) is a homeomorphism \((-1, 1) \to \mathbb {R}\) with inverse \(s \mapsto s/(1 + |s|)\), but \((-1, 1)\) is not complete. And \(\mathbb {Q}\), which is not complete, is a countable union of closed sets with empty interior, so the Baire category theorem fails for it.
