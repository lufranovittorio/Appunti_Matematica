50.7 Rational maps and birational equivalence01KF

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

01KG Lemma 50.7.1

Two morphisms \(\varphi , \psi \colon X \to Y\) of varieties that agree on a nonempty open subset are equal.

Proof â–¼

Embed \(Y\) in some \(\mathbb {P}^n\). The set \(E = \{ x : \varphi (x) = \psi (x)\} \) is closed. Indeed, near a point of \(X\), \(\varphi \) and \(\psi \) map into standard open sets \(U_a\) and \(U_b\), so on a neighbourhood \(V\) they are given by regular functions \(\varphi = [f_0 : \cdots : f_n]\) with \(f_a = 1\) and \(\psi = [g_0 : \cdots : g_n]\) with \(g_b = 1\). On \(V\), \(E\) is the common zero set of the regular functions \(f_ig_j - f_jg_i\). The closed set \(E\) contains a dense open subset of the irreducible \(X\), so \(E = X\).

01KH Definition 50.7.2

A rational map \(\varphi \colon X \dashrightarrow Y\) is an equivalence class of pairs \((U, \varphi _U)\), where \(U \subseteq X\) is nonempty open and \(\varphi _U \colon U \to Y\) is a morphism, two pairs being equivalent if the morphisms agree on the intersection (which is possible by Lemma 50.7.1). It is dominant if \(\varphi _U(U)\) is dense. A birational map is a dominant rational map with a dominant rational inverse, and \(X\), \(Y\) are birational if there is one. A variety birational to \(\mathbb {P}^n\) is rational.

01KI Theorem 50.7.3

The functor \(X \mapsto K(X)\) is an anti-equivalence between the category of varieties with dominant rational maps and the category of finitely generated field extensions of \(k\). In particular \(X\) and \(Y\) are birational if and only if \(K(X) \cong K(Y)\) over \(k\), if and only if they have isomorphic nonempty open subsets.

Proof â–¼

A dominant \(\varphi \) defined on \(U\) pulls back regular functions on nonempty open subsets of \(Y\) to regular functions on nonempty open subsets of \(U\), since preimages of dense open sets under a dominant map are nonempty. This gives \(\varphi ^* \colon K(Y) \to K(X)\). Conversely, let \(\theta \colon K(Y) \to K(X)\) be a \(k\)-embedding. Choose an affine open \(V \subseteq Y\) with \(A(V) = k[y_1, \ldots , y_n]\). The elements \(\theta (y_i)\) are regular on some nonempty open \(U \subseteq X\), and \(\theta \) restricts to \(A(V) \to \mathcal{O}(U)\). By Proposition 50.3.5 this is \(\varphi ^*\) for a morphism \(\varphi \colon U \to V\). It is dominant: if its image lay in a proper closed subset \(Z(g)\) with \(0 \neq g \in A(V)\), then \(\theta (g) = 0\), contradicting injectivity. These constructions are inverse to each other by Lemma 50.7.1 and Theorem 50.3.3. Every finitely generated extension \(K = k(y_1, \ldots , y_n)\) is \(K(V)\) for the affine variety with coordinate ring \(k[y_1, \ldots , y_n] \subseteq K\) (Proposition 50.3.5).

If \(\varphi \colon X \dashrightarrow Y\) and \(\psi \colon Y \dashrightarrow X\) are inverse birational maps, defined on \(U\) and \(V\), then \(\psi \circ \varphi = \mathrm{id}\) on \(\varphi ^{-1}(V)\) and \(\varphi \circ \psi = \mathrm{id}\) on \(\psi ^{-1}(U)\). So \(\varphi \) restricts to an isomorphism \(\varphi ^{-1}(\psi ^{-1}(U)) \to \psi ^{-1}(U)\). The converse is clear.

01KJ Proposition 50.7.4

Every variety of dimension \(r\) is birational to a hypersurface in \(\mathbb {A}^{r+1}\).

Proof â–¼

The finitely generated extension \(K(Y)/k\) of transcendence degree \(r\) is separably generated: there are \(x_1, \ldots , x_r\), algebraically independent, such that \(K(Y)\) is a finite separable extension of \(k(x_1, \ldots , x_r)\). In characteristic \(0\) this holds for every transcendence basis. In characteristic \(p\) it holds because \(k\) is perfect [Mat86, Section 26]. By the primitive element theorem (Theorem 9.6.3), \(K(Y) = k(x_1, \ldots , x_r, y)\) with \(y\) separable algebraic over \(k(x)\). Clearing denominators in the minimal polynomial of \(y\) gives an irreducible \(f \in k[x_1, \ldots , x_r, t]\) with \(f(x, y) = 0\), and \(K(Z(f)) \cong K(Y)\). Apply Theorem 50.7.3.

01KK Theorem 50.7.5

The singular points of a variety \(Y\) form a proper closed subset.

Proof â–¼

Closedness is local, so let \(Y \subseteq \mathbb {A}^n\) be affine of dimension \(r\) with \(I(Y) = (f_1, \ldots , f_t)\). By Lemma 50.6.2 and Theorem 50.6.3, \(\operatorname {rank}J(P) \leq n - r\) everywhere, and the singular set is the common zero set of the \((n - r)\)-minors of \(J\), which is closed. It is proper: by Proposition 50.7.4 and Theorem 50.7.3, \(Y\) has an open subset isomorphic to an open subset of a hypersurface \(Z(f) \subseteq \mathbb {A}^{r+1}\) with \(f\) irreducible, so it suffices to find a nonsingular point on \(Z(f)\). The singular points are \(Z(f, \partial f/\partial x_1, \ldots )\) (Example 50.6.4). If they were all of \(Z(f)\), then each \(\partial f/\partial x_j\) would lie in \(I(Z(f)) = (f)\), hence vanish, by degree. In characteristic \(0\) this makes \(f\) constant. In characteristic \(p\) it makes \(f\) a polynomial in \(x_1^p, \ldots , x_{r+1}^p\), hence a \(p\)-th power since \(k\) is perfect, contradicting irreducibility. So the nonsingular points of \(Z(f)\) form a nonempty open set, which meets the open subset isomorphic to an open subset of \(Y\), because \(Z(f)\) is irreducible. Isomorphic open subsets have isomorphic local rings.

01KL Example 50.7.6

  1. \(\mathbb {P}^n\) and \(\mathbb {A}^n\) are birational, and so are \(\mathbb {P}^1 \times \mathbb {P}^1\) and \(\mathbb {P}^2\): all three have function field \(k(x_1, x_2)\) for \(n = 2\). By Corollary 50.5.6, \(\mathbb {P}^1 \times \mathbb {P}^1\) and \(\mathbb {P}^2\) are not isomorphic.

  2. The cuspidal cubic \(Z(y^2 - x^3)\) is rational: \(t = y/x\) gives \(K = k(t)\). It is singular, so it is not isomorphic to \(\mathbb {P}^1\) or \(\mathbb {A}^1\).

  3. The Cremona transformation \(\mathbb {P}^2 \dashrightarrow \mathbb {P}^2\), \([x : y : z] \mapsto [yz : xz : xy]\), is birational and equal to its own inverse, but it is not defined at the three coordinate points.

  4. A nonsingular plane cubic is not rational. This follows from the genus (Chapter 56): rational nonsingular projective curves are isomorphic to \(\mathbb {P}^1\), which has genus \(0\), while plane cubics have genus \(1\).
