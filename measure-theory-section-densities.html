28.22 Densities and rectifiability035E

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

By the Lebesgue density theorem (Corollary 28.9.5) a measurable set \(A \subseteq \mathbb {R}^n\) fills almost all of every small ball centred at almost every one of its points. For a set of dimension \(s {\lt} n\) the analogous question is whether \(\mathcal{H}^s(A \cap \overline{B}(x, r))\) is comparable to \(\alpha (s)(2r)^s\), the \(s\)-dimensional “volume” of a ball of radius \(r\). For smooth curves and surfaces the answer is yes, with ratio tending to \(1\); for the Cantor set the ratio oscillates forever. This difference turns out to be the difference between the sets on which one can do calculus, the rectifiable ones, and the others. Throughout, \(X\) is a metric space and \(\overline{B}(x, r) = \{ y : d(x, y) \leq r\} \).

035F Definition 28.22.1

For \(A \subseteq X\), \(s {\gt} 0\) and \(x \in X\), the upper and lower \(s\)-dimensional densities of \(A\) at \(x\) are

\[ \Theta ^{*s}(A, x) = \limsup _{r \to 0}\frac{\mathcal{H}^s(A \cap \overline{B}(x, r))}{\alpha (s)(2r)^s}, \qquad \Theta ^s_*(A, x) = \liminf _{r \to 0}\frac{\mathcal{H}^s(A \cap \overline{B}(x, r))}{\alpha (s)(2r)^s}. \]

If they are equal, the common value is the density \(\Theta ^s(A, x)\).

The normalisation is chosen so that flat pieces have density \(1\). For \(A \subseteq \mathbb {R}^n\) Lebesgue measurable and \(s = n\), \(\mathcal{H}^n = \lambda ^n\) and \(\alpha (n)(2r)^n = \lambda ^n(\overline{B}(x, r))\), so by the density theorem \(\Theta ^n(A, x) = 1\) for almost every \(x \in A\) and \(\Theta ^n(A, x) = 0\) for almost every \(x \notin A\) (spheres are null, so closed and open balls give the same ratios).

035G Example 28.22.2

Curves have density \(1\). Let \(\gamma \colon (a, b) \to \mathbb {R}^m\) be an injective \(C^1\) map with \(\gamma ^{\prime } \neq 0\) which is a homeomorphism onto its image \(\Gamma \). Then \(\Theta ^1(\Gamma , x) = 1\) for every \(x \in \Gamma \). Let \(x = \gamma (t_0)\), \(c = |\gamma ^{\prime }(t_0)| {\gt} 0\), and \(0 {\lt} \varepsilon {\lt} 1\). Choose \(\delta {\gt} 0\) with \([t_0 - \delta , t_0 + \delta ] \subseteq (a, b)\) and \(|\gamma ^{\prime }(t) - \gamma ^{\prime }(t_0)| \leq \varepsilon c\) there. By the mean value inequality, applied to \(\gamma (t) - \gamma ^{\prime }(t_0)(t - t_0)\),

\[ (1 - \varepsilon )c|t - t_0| \leq |\gamma (t) - x| \leq (1 + \varepsilon )c|t - t_0| \qquad (|t - t_0| \leq \delta ), \]

and \((1 - \varepsilon )c \leq |\gamma ^{\prime }(t)| \leq (1 + \varepsilon )c\) there. Since \(\gamma ^{-1}\) is continuous at \(x\), there is \(r_0 \in (0, (1 - \varepsilon )c\delta ]\) such that \(|\gamma (t) - x| \leq r_0\) implies \(|t - t_0| \leq \delta \). For \(r {\lt} r_0\) the set \(I_r = \{ t : |\gamma (t) - x| \leq r\} \) therefore satisfies

\[ \bigl[t_0 - \tfrac {r}{(1 + \varepsilon )c}, t_0 + \tfrac {r}{(1 + \varepsilon )c}\bigr] \subseteq I_r \subseteq \bigl[t_0 - \tfrac {r}{(1 - \varepsilon )c}, t_0 + \tfrac {r}{(1 - \varepsilon )c}\bigr]. \]

By the area formula (Theorem 28.20.3), \(\mathcal{H}^1(\Gamma \cap \overline{B}(x, r)) = \int _{I_r}|\gamma ^{\prime }|\) lies between \(2r\frac{1 - \varepsilon }{1 + \varepsilon }\) and \(2r\frac{1 + \varepsilon }{1 - \varepsilon }\). As \(\alpha (1) = 1\) and \(\varepsilon \) was arbitrary, the ratio tends to \(1\). The homeomorphism condition matters: for a curve shaped like the digit \(6\), whose end \(\gamma (t)\), \(t \to b\), runs into an interior point \(x\) from one side, a small ball around \(x\) contains the arc of the curve through \(x\), of length about \(2r\), and the end arc, of length about \(r\), and \(\Theta ^1(\Gamma , x) = \frac32\).

035H Example 28.22.3

The Cantor set has no density at \(0\). Let \(C\) be the Cantor set, \(s = \log 2/\log 3\), and \(c = \mathcal{H}^s(C)\), so that \(\alpha (s)/2 \leq c \leq \alpha (s)\) (Example 28.17.6). For \(k \geq 0\), \(C \cap [0, 3^{-k}] = 3^{-k}C\) (the piece \(C_w\) for \(w = 1\cdots 1\) of length \(k\), in the notation of Example 28.18.7), so \(\mathcal{H}^s(C \cap [0, 3^{-k}]) = 3^{-ks}c = 2^{-k}c\). Moreover \(C\) does not meet the gap \((3^{-k-1}, 2 \cdot 3^{-k-1})\), so \(C \cap [0, 2 \cdot 3^{-k-1}]\) is \(C \cap [0, 3^{-k-1}]\) plus the single point \(2 \cdot 3^{-k-1}\). Hence, with \(r = 3^{-k}\) and \(r = 2 \cdot 3^{-k-1}\) respectively,

\[ \frac{\mathcal{H}^s(C \cap \overline{B}(0, 3^{-k}))}{\alpha (s)(2 \cdot 3^{-k})^s} = \frac{2^{-k}c}{\alpha (s)2^s2^{-k}} = \frac{c}{\alpha (s)2^s}, \qquad \frac{\mathcal{H}^s(C \cap \overline{B}(0, 2 \cdot 3^{-k-1}))}{\alpha (s)(4 \cdot 3^{-k-1})^s} = \frac{2^{-k-1}c}{\alpha (s)4^s2^{-k-1}} = \frac{c}{\alpha (s)4^s}. \]

So \(\Theta ^{*s}(C, 0) \geq c/(\alpha (s)2^s)\) and \(\Theta ^s_*(C, 0) \leq c/(\alpha (s)4^s)\), which is smaller by the factor \(2^s \approx 1.55\); as \(c {\gt} 0\), the density does not exist. The same computation works at the left endpoint \(f_w(0)\) of every interval \(I_w\): for \(r {\lt} 3^{-|w|}\) the ball \(\overline{B}(f_w(0), r)\) meets \(C\) in \(f_w(C \cap [0, 3^{|w|}r])\), because \(C\) has a gap of length at least \(3^{-|w|}\) to the left of \(I_w\) (Example 28.17.6(2)), and \(\mathcal{H}^s\) scales by \(3^{-|w|s}\) under \(f_w\). These endpoints are dense in \(C\).

For a general set we cannot expect the density to exist, but the upper density is bounded below almost everywhere, by a short covering argument that uses nothing but the definition of \(\mathcal{H}^s\).

035I Theorem 28.22.4

Let \(A \subseteq X\) with \(\mathcal{H}^s(A) {\lt} \infty \), \(s {\gt} 0\). Then \(\Theta ^{*s}(A, x) \geq 2^{-s}\) for \(\mathcal{H}^s\)-almost every \(x \in A\).

Proof â–¼

For \(0 {\lt} t {\lt} 2^{-s}\) and \(k \geq 1\) let

\[ A_{t, k} = \bigl\{ x \in A : \mathcal{H}^s(A \cap \overline{B}(x, r)) \leq t\, \alpha (s)(2r)^s \text{ for all } 0 {\lt} r {\lt} 1/k\bigr\} . \]

A point \(x \in A\) with \(\Theta ^{*s}(A, x) {\lt} 2^{-s}\) lies in some \(A_{t, k}\) with \(t\) rational; so it suffices to show \(\mathcal{H}^s(A_{t, k}) = 0\) for all \(t\) and \(k\), by countable subadditivity.

Fix \(t, k\), let \(0 {\lt} \delta {\lt} 1/k\) and \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\), and choose a cover \((C_i)\) of \(A_{t, k}\) by sets of diameter at most \(\delta \) with \(\sum _i\alpha (s)(\operatorname {diam}C_i)^s \leq \mathcal{H}^s_\delta (A_{t, k}) + \varepsilon \). We may discard the \(C_i\) that do not meet \(A_{t, k}\). For the others pick \(x_i \in C_i \cap A_{t, k}\) and let \(d_i = \operatorname {diam}C_i\). If \(d_i {\gt} 0\), then \(C_i \subseteq \overline{B}(x_i, d_i)\) with \(d_i {\lt} 1/k\), so by the definition of \(A_{t, k}\)

\[ \mathcal{H}^s(A_{t, k} \cap C_i) \leq \mathcal{H}^s(A \cap \overline{B}(x_i, d_i)) \leq t\, \alpha (s)(2d_i)^s = t2^s\, \alpha (s)d_i^s . \]

If \(d_i = 0\), then \(C_i\) is a point and both sides are \(0\). Summing, and using countable subadditivity of \(\mathcal{H}^s\),

\[ \mathcal{H}^s(A_{t, k}) \leq \sum _i\mathcal{H}^s(A_{t, k} \cap C_i) \leq t2^s\bigl(\mathcal{H}^s_\delta (A_{t, k}) + \varepsilon \bigr) \leq t2^s\bigl(\mathcal{H}^s(A_{t, k}) + \varepsilon \bigr). \]

Let \(\varepsilon \to 0\). Since \(\mathcal{H}^s(A_{t, k}) \leq \mathcal{H}^s(A) {\lt} \infty \) and \(t2^s {\lt} 1\), this forces \(\mathcal{H}^s(A_{t, k}) = 0\).

The bound \(2^{-s}\) comes from the comparison \(C_i \subseteq \overline{B}(x_i, \operatorname {diam}C_i)\), which loses a factor \(2\) in the radius. Inequalities in the other direction, bounding the upper density from above, need a different idea: instead of starting from an arbitrary cover and enclosing its sets in balls, one starts from balls on which the measure is large and extracts disjoint ones. This is a covering lemma of Vitali type, and it works in every metric space. For a closed ball \(B = \overline{B}(x, r)\), given together with its centre and radius, write \(\widehat{B} = \overline{B}(x, 5r)\).

0393 Lemma 28.22.5 \(5r\)-covering lemma

Let \(\mathcal{F}\) be a family of closed balls \(\overline{B}(x, r)\), \(r {\gt} 0\), in a metric space, with \(\sup \{ r\} = R {\lt} \infty \). There is a subfamily \(\mathcal{G}\) of pairwise disjoint balls such that every \(B \in \mathcal{F}\) meets some \(B^{\prime } \in \mathcal{G}\) whose radius is at least half the radius of \(B\); in particular \(B \subseteq \widehat{B^{\prime }}\).

Proof â–¼

Let \(\mathcal{F}_j\) be the set of balls in \(\mathcal{F}\) with radius in \((2^{-j}R, 2^{1-j}R]\), \(j \geq 1\). By Zorn’s lemma choose a maximal pairwise disjoint subfamily \(\mathcal{G}_1\) of \(\mathcal{F}_1\) (the union of a chain of disjoint subfamilies is disjoint), and inductively a maximal pairwise disjoint subfamily \(\mathcal{G}_j\) of the balls in \(\mathcal{F}_j\) that are disjoint from all balls of \(\mathcal{G}_1 \cup \cdots \cup \mathcal{G}_{j-1}\). Let \(\mathcal{G} = \bigcup _j\mathcal{G}_j\); it is pairwise disjoint. Let \(B = \overline{B}(x, r) \in \mathcal{F}_j\). Either \(B\) meets a ball of some \(\mathcal{G}_i\) with \(i {\lt} j\), whose radius exceeds \(2^{-i}R \geq 2^{1-j}R \geq r\); or it is disjoint from all of them, and then by maximality of \(\mathcal{G}_j\) it meets a ball of \(\mathcal{G}_j\), whose radius exceeds \(2^{-j}R \geq r/2\). In both cases \(B\) meets \(B^{\prime } = \overline{B}(x^{\prime }, r^{\prime }) \in \mathcal{G}\) with \(r^{\prime } \geq r/2\). If \(z \in B \cap B^{\prime }\) and \(y \in B\), then \(d(y, x^{\prime }) \leq d(y, x) + d(x, z) + d(z, x^{\prime }) \leq 2r + r^{\prime } \leq 5r^{\prime }\).

0394 Theorem 28.22.6

Let \(\mu \) be an outer measure on a metric space \(X\) for which all Borel sets are \(\mu \)-measurable (for instance \(\mu = \mathcal{H}^s\), by Lemma 28.17.2). Let \(A_1, A_2 \subseteq X\), \(s {\gt} 0\) and \(t \geq 0\), and suppose

\[ \Theta ^{*s}(\mu , A_2, x) = \limsup _{r \to 0}\frac{\mu (A_2 \cap \overline{B}(x, r))}{\alpha (s)(2r)^s} \geq t \qquad \text{for all } x \in A_1 . \]

Then \(t\, \mathcal{H}^s(A_1) \leq \mu (A_2)\).

A Borel measure \(\mu \) on \(X\) defines such an outer measure, \(\mu ^*(E) = \inf \{ \mu (B) : B \supseteq E \text{ Borel}\} \). The infimum is attained, by the intersection of Borel sets \(B_j \supseteq E\) with \(\mu (B_j) \to \mu ^*(E)\); and for \(B\) Borel, \(E \subseteq X\) and a Borel \(E^{\prime } \supseteq E\) with \(\mu (E^{\prime }) = \mu ^*(E)\), \(\mu ^*(E \cap B) + \mu ^*(E \setminus B) \leq \mu (E^{\prime } \cap B) + \mu (E^{\prime } \setminus B) = \mu ^*(E)\), so \(B\) is \(\mu ^*\)-measurable. So the theorem applies to Borel measures as well. This is the form of the theorem in [Sim83], where \(A_1 \subseteq A_2\) is assumed; the proof below does not use this inclusion. The sets \(A_1\) and \(A_2\) need not be measurable. The important special case is \(A_1 = A_2\); the freedom to choose \(A_2\) is used to localise, with \(A_2 = A \cap U\) for an open set \(U \supseteq A_1\). Note the direction: Theorem 28.22.4 and its generalisation Theorem 28.22.14 below bound \(\mu \) from above where the density is small; this theorem bounds \(\mu \) from below where the density is large, and loses no factor \(2^s\).

Proof â–¼

We may assume \(t {\gt} 0\) and \(\mu (A_2) {\lt} \infty \). Let \(0 {\lt} t^{\prime } {\lt} t\) and \(\delta {\gt} 0\), and let \(\mathcal{F}\) be the family of closed balls \(\overline{B}(x, r)\) with \(x \in A_1\), \(0 {\lt} r {\lt} \delta \) and \(\mu (A_2 \cap \overline{B}(x, r)) \geq t^{\prime }\alpha (s)(2r)^s\). By hypothesis every \(x \in A_1\) is the centre of balls of \(\mathcal{F}\) of arbitrarily small radius. Let \(\mathcal{G}\) be as in Lemma 28.22.5.

The balls of \(\mathcal{G}\) are disjoint Borel sets, hence \(\mu \)-measurable, and by the definition of measurability \(\mu (A_2) \geq \sum _{B \in \mathcal{G}^{\prime }}\mu (A_2 \cap B)\) for every finite \(\mathcal{G}^{\prime } \subseteq \mathcal{G}\) (split \(A_2\) successively along the balls). Each term is at least \(t^{\prime }\alpha (s)(2r_B)^s {\gt} 0\), so only finitely many balls of \(\mathcal{G}\) have \(\mu (A_2 \cap B) {\gt} 1/m\), for each \(m\); hence \(\mathcal{G}\) is countable, say \(\mathcal{G} = \{ B_1, B_2, \ldots \} \) with radii \(r_i\), and

\[ \sum _it^{\prime }\alpha (s)(2r_i)^s \leq \sum _i\mu (A_2 \cap B_i) \leq \mu (A_2) {\lt} \infty . \]

We claim that \(A_1 \subseteq \bigcup _{i \leq N}B_i \cup \bigcup _{i {\gt} N}\widehat{B_i}\) for every \(N\). Let \(x \in A_1\) lie outside the closed set \(B_1 \cup \cdots \cup B_N\). Some closed ball around \(x\) misses this set, so there is \(B \in \mathcal{F}\) centred at \(x\) and disjoint from \(B_1, \ldots , B_N\). By the lemma \(B\) meets some \(B_i\) with \(B \subseteq \widehat{B_i}\), and necessarily \(i {\gt} N\); so \(x \in \widehat{B_i}\).

The sets \(B_i\) and \(\widehat{B_i}\) have diameters at most \(2r_i {\lt} 2\delta \) and \(10r_i {\lt} 10\delta \). Hence

\[ \mathcal{H}^s_{10\delta }(A_1) \leq \sum _{i \leq N}\alpha (s)(2r_i)^s + \sum _{i {\gt} N}\alpha (s)(10r_i)^s \leq \frac{\mu (A_2)}{t^{\prime }} + 5^s\sum _{i {\gt} N}\alpha (s)(2r_i)^s . \]

The last sum is the tail of a convergent series, so letting \(N \to \infty \) gives \(\mathcal{H}^s_{10\delta }(A_1) \leq \mu (A_2)/t^{\prime }\). Letting \(\delta \to 0\) and then \(t^{\prime } \to t\) proves the theorem.

Together with Theorem 28.22.4 this gives the basic density estimates for sets of finite Hausdorff measure, in every metric space.

035J Theorem 28.22.7 Upper density theorem

Let \(A \subseteq X\) be \(\mathcal{H}^s\)-measurable with \(\mathcal{H}^s(A) {\lt} \infty \), \(s {\gt} 0\). Then \(2^{-s} \leq \Theta ^{*s}(A, x) \leq 1\) for \(\mathcal{H}^s\)-almost every \(x \in A\), and \(\Theta ^{*s}(A, x) = 0\) for \(\mathcal{H}^s\)-almost every \(x \in X \setminus A\).

Proof â–¼

The lower bound is Theorem 28.22.4. Let \(\nu (E) = \mathcal{H}^s(A \cap E)\) for Borel \(E\); since \(A\) and \(E\) are \(\mathcal{H}^s\)-measurable, \(\nu \) is a finite Borel measure, so by Lemma 28.14.1 every Borel set \(E\) has open neighbourhoods \(U\) with \(\nu (U) {\lt} \nu (E) + \varepsilon \). For \(x\) in an open set \(U\), small balls around \(x\) lie in \(U\), so \(\Theta ^{*s}(\mathcal{H}^s, A \cap U, x) = \Theta ^{*s}(A, x)\).

Upper bound. Let \(t {\gt} 1\) and \(B = \{ x \in A : \Theta ^{*s}(A, x) {\gt} t\} \). By Borel regularity (Remark 28.17.4(2)) there is a Borel set \(E \supseteq B\) with \(\mathcal{H}^s(E) = \mathcal{H}^s(B)\), so \(\nu (E) = \mathcal{H}^s(B)\), as \(B \subseteq A \cap E \subseteq E\). For an open \(U \supseteq E\) with \(\nu (U) {\lt} \nu (E) + \varepsilon \), Theorem 28.22.6 with \(\mu = \mathcal{H}^s\), \(A_1 = B\), \(A_2 = A \cap U\) gives \(t\mathcal{H}^s(B) \leq \mathcal{H}^s(A \cap U) = \nu (U) {\lt} \mathcal{H}^s(B) + \varepsilon \). As \(\varepsilon \) is arbitrary and \(\mathcal{H}^s(B) {\lt} \infty \), \((t - 1)\mathcal{H}^s(B) \leq 0\), so \(\mathcal{H}^s(B) = 0\). Taking \(t = 1 + 1/j\), \(j \geq 1\), gives the upper bound.

Outside \(A\). Let \(t {\gt} 0\) and \(B = \{ x \in X \setminus A : \Theta ^{*s}(A, x) {\gt} t\} \). Choose a Borel set \(A^{\prime } \supseteq A\) with \(\mathcal{H}^s(A^{\prime }) = \mathcal{H}^s(A)\); since \(A\) is measurable and of finite measure, \(\mathcal{H}^s(A^{\prime } \setminus A) = 0\), so \(B \cap A^{\prime }\) is null. The Borel set \(X \setminus A^{\prime }\) has \(\nu (X \setminus A^{\prime }) = 0\), so it has open neighbourhoods \(U\) with \(\nu (U) {\lt} \varepsilon \), and the theorem with \(A_1 = B \setminus A^{\prime }\), \(A_2 = A \cap U\) gives \(t\mathcal{H}^s(B \setminus A^{\prime }) \leq \varepsilon \). Hence \(B\) is null, and taking \(t = 1/j\) gives the last statement.

The name comes from [Sim83]: the theorem is about upper densities only. In \(\mathbb {R}^n\) with \(s = n\) it says less than the Lebesgue density theorem, which gives density exactly \(1\) on \(A\) and \(0\) outside; but for non-integral \(s\) the density need not exist (Example 28.22.3), and the upper density is then the right object. For \(A\) the Cantor set the theorem says that \(\Theta ^{*s}(C, x) \in [2^{-s}, 1]\) at \(\mathcal{H}^s\)-almost every point of \(C\).

So every set of finite \(s\)-dimensional measure is, at almost all of its points, at least somewhat dense and at most as dense as a flat piece. Whether the density exists, that is, whether the lower density equals the upper one, is a deep geometric property, and it is related to the following class of sets.

035K Definition 28.22.8

Let \(k \geq 1\) be an integer. A set \(A \subseteq \mathbb {R}^n\) is countably \(k\)-rectifiable if there are Lipschitz maps \(g_j \colon E_j \to \mathbb {R}^n\), \(E_j \subseteq \mathbb {R}^k\), \(j = 1, 2, \ldots \), such that

\[ \mathcal{H}^k\Bigl(A \setminus \bigcup _jg_j(E_j)\Bigr) = 0 . \]

It is purely \(k\)-unrectifiable if \(\mathcal{H}^k(A \cap g(E)) = 0\) for every Lipschitz map \(g \colon E \to \mathbb {R}^n\) with \(E \subseteq \mathbb {R}^k\).

So a rectifiable set is, up to a null set, a countable union of Lipschitz images of pieces of \(\mathbb {R}^k\), and on such pieces Rademacher’s theorem and the area formula give tangent planes and a calculus. A purely unrectifiable set meets each Lipschitz image of a piece of \(\mathbb {R}^k\) only in a null set.

035L Proposition 28.22.9

  1. Subsets and countable unions of countably \(k\)-rectifiable sets are countably \(k\)-rectifiable.

  2. A countably \(k\)-rectifiable set \(A\) has \(\dim _{\mathcal{H}}A \leq k\), and \(A \cap \bigcup _jg_j(E_j)\) has \(\sigma \)-finite \(\mathcal{H}^k\)-measure.

  3. If \(U \subseteq \mathbb {R}^k\) is open and \(g \colon U \to \mathbb {R}^n\) is of class \(C^1\), then \(g(U)\) is countably \(k\)-rectifiable.

  4. If \(A\) is countably \(k\)-rectifiable and \(B\) is purely \(k\)-unrectifiable, then \(\mathcal{H}^k(A \cap B) = 0\).

Proof â–¼

(1) is clear from the definition: a subset can use the same maps, and countably many countable families of maps form a countable family.

(2) Let \(L_j\) be a Lipschitz constant of \(g_j\). For \(m \geq 1\), Proposition 28.17.3(3) and Theorem 28.17.5 give \(\mathcal{H}^k(g_j(E_j \cap [-m, m]^k)) \leq L_j^k\mathcal{H}^k([-m, m]^k) = L_j^k(2m)^k {\lt} \infty \). So \(\bigcup _jg_j(E_j)\) is a countable union of sets of finite \(\mathcal{H}^k\)-measure, each of which has \(\mathcal{H}^t\)-measure \(0\) for \(t {\gt} k\) (Proposition 28.17.3(4)); the remaining part of \(A\) is \(\mathcal{H}^k\)-null, hence also \(\mathcal{H}^t\)-null for \(t {\gt} k\).

(3) \(U\) is the union of the countably many closed balls \(\overline{B} \subseteq U\) with rational centres and radii. On such a ball, which is compact and convex, \(\| Dg\| \) is bounded, say by \(L\), and the mean value inequality (Proposition 27.10.3(4)) shows that \(g\) is \(L\)-Lipschitz on \(\overline{B}\).

(4) \(A \cap B\) is contained in the union of the \(\mathcal{H}^k\)-null set \(A \setminus \bigcup _jg_j(E_j)\) and the sets \(B \cap g_j(E_j)\), which are \(\mathcal{H}^k\)-null by assumption.

By (3), images of \(C^1\) maps, such as the curves of Example 28.22.2, graphs of \(C^1\) or Lipschitz functions, and \(k\)-dimensional submanifolds of \(\mathbb {R}^n\) (which are covered by countably many images of charts), are countably \(k\)-rectifiable. At the other extreme, the four-corner Cantor set \(K_4\) of Example 28.18.11(2), which has \(0 {\lt} \mathcal{H}^1(K_4) {\lt} \infty \), is purely \(1\)-unrectifiable [Fal85], [Mat95]. The two classes are distinguished by densities and by projections, and these are among the deepest results of the subject.

035M Theorem 28.22.10

Let \(A \subseteq \mathbb {R}^n\) be \(\mathcal{H}^k\)-measurable with \(0 {\lt} \mathcal{H}^k(A) {\lt} \infty \), \(k\) an integer with \(1 \leq k {\lt} n\).

  1. (Densities) \(A\) is countably \(k\)-rectifiable if and only if \(\Theta ^k(A, x) = 1\) for \(\mathcal{H}^k\)-almost every \(x \in A\), and if and only if \(\Theta ^k(A, x)\) exists and is positive for \(\mathcal{H}^k\)-almost every \(x \in A\).

  2. (Projections, Besicovitch–Federer) If \(A\) is countably \(k\)-rectifiable, then its orthogonal projections to almost all \(k\)-dimensional linear subspaces have positive \(\mathcal{H}^k\)-measure. If \(A\) is purely \(k\)-unrectifiable, then its projections to almost all \(k\)-dimensional linear subspaces are \(\mathcal{H}^k\)-null.

Here “almost all” refers to the rotation-invariant measure on the set of \(k\)-dimensional subspaces; for \(k = 1\), \(n = 2\) it means almost all angles. See [Mat95], [Fed69]; the last statement of (1) is Preiss’s theorem [Pre87].

035N Remark 28.22.11

  1. Non-integral dimensions. If \(0 {\lt} \mathcal{H}^s(A) {\lt} \infty \) and the density \(\Theta ^s(A, x)\) exists and is positive at almost every point of \(A\), then \(s\) is an integer (Marstrand, see [Mat95]). So sets of non-integral dimension, such as the Cantor set (Example 28.22.3) and the self-similar sets of Section 28.18, are always irregular: their densities oscillate.

  2. The four-corner set again. Example 28.18.12 computed two projections of \(K_4\): in the direction of the axes it has length \(0\), as Theorem 28.22.10(2) predicts for almost all directions, while in the direction of \((2, 1)\) it is an interval. So the exceptional set of directions in the Besicovitch–Federer theorem is not empty. For a segment the situation is reversed: its projection has positive length in every direction except the perpendicular one.

  3. Besicovitch and Kakeya sets. A Besicovitch set is a compact subset of \(\mathbb {R}^n\) that contains a segment of length \(1\) in every direction. Besicovitch showed that such sets exist in \(\mathbb {R}^2\) with Lebesgue measure \(0\); correspondingly, a needle of length \(1\) can be turned around completely inside a region of arbitrarily small area (Kakeya’s needle problem). Every Besicovitch set in \(\mathbb {R}^2\) has Hausdorff dimension \(2\) (Davies), see [Fal85]. Whether Besicovitch sets in \(\mathbb {R}^n\) must have Hausdorff dimension \(n\) is the Kakeya conjecture, a central problem linking geometric measure theory with harmonic analysis and analytic number theory.

28.22.1 Densities of measures038W

Nothing in the definition of densities is special to the Hausdorff measure of a set: one can ask how much mass any measure puts in small balls. This point of view, in which the set \(A\) is replaced by the measure \(\mathcal{H}^s\) restricted to \(A\), is the one used in modern geometric measure theory, and it simplifies the statements. Throughout, \(\mu \) is a Borel measure on the metric space \(X\) that is finite on bounded Borel sets.

038X Definition 28.22.12

For \(s {\gt} 0\) and \(x \in X\), the upper and lower \(s\)-dimensional densities of \(\mu \) at \(x\) are

\[ \Theta ^{*s}(\mu , x) = \limsup _{r \to 0}\frac{\mu (\overline{B}(x, r))}{\alpha (s)(2r)^s}, \qquad \Theta ^s_*(\mu , x) = \liminf _{r \to 0}\frac{\mu (\overline{B}(x, r))}{\alpha (s)(2r)^s}, \]

and the density \(\Theta ^s(\mu , x)\) is their common value when they agree.

038Y Example 28.22.13

  1. If \(A \subseteq X\) is a Borel set with \(\mathcal{H}^s(A) {\lt} \infty \), then \(\mu = \mathcal{H}^s\llcorner A\), \(\mu (B) = \mathcal{H}^s(A \cap B)\), is such a measure (Borel sets are \(\mathcal{H}^s\)-measurable by Lemma 28.17.2), and \(\Theta ^{*s}(\mu , x) = \Theta ^{*s}(A, x)\): the densities of Definition 28.22.1 are those of this measure.

  2. For \(f \in L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb {R}^n)\) with \(f \geq 0\) and \(\mu = f\lambda \), \(\Theta ^n(\mu , x) = f(x)\) for almost every \(x\), since \(\alpha (n)(2r)^n = \lambda (\overline{B}(x, r))\) and the averages of \(f\) over balls converge to \(f(x)\) almost everywhere (Theorem 28.9.4; closed and open balls differ by a null sphere). For \(s {\lt} n\), \(\Theta ^s(\mu , x) = 0\) wherever \(f\) is bounded near \(x\).

  3. For a point mass \(\mu = \delta _a\), \(\Theta ^{*s}(\mu , a) = \infty \) for every \(s {\gt} 0\), and \(\Theta ^s(\mu , x) = 0\) for \(x \neq a\).

  4. For the Cantor measure \(\mu \) of Example 28.17.6(3) and \(s = \log 2/\log 3\), part (4) of that example gives \(\mu (\overline{B}(x, r)) \leq 2(2r)^s\) for \(2r {\lt} 1\), so \(\Theta ^{*s}(\mu , x) \leq 2/\alpha (s)\) at every point. At \(0\) the density does not exist: \(\mu ([0, 3^{-k}]) = 2^{-k}\), and \(\mu ([0, 2 \cdot 3^{-k-1}]) = 2^{-k-1}\) because \(\mu \) gives no mass to the gap \((3^{-k-1}, 2 \cdot 3^{-k-1})\) and none to points (the Cantor function is continuous), so the computation of Example 28.22.3 applies with \(c = 1\).

The basic theorem compares \(\mu \) with \(\mathcal{H}^s\) on the set where the density of \(\mu \) is bounded.

038Z Theorem 28.22.14

Let \(\mu \) be a Borel measure on \(X\), finite on bounded Borel sets, let \(A \subseteq X\) be a Borel set, \(s {\gt} 0\) and \(0 {\lt} t {\lt} \infty \). If \(\Theta ^{*s}(\mu , x) \leq t\) for every \(x \in A\), then

\[ \mu (A) \leq 2^st\, \mathcal{H}^s(A). \]
Proof â–¼

Measurability. For fixed \(r {\gt} 0\) the function \(x \mapsto \mu (\overline{B}(x, r))\) is upper semicontinuous: if \(x_j \to x\), then \(\overline{B}(x_j, r) \subseteq \overline{B}(x, r + \eta )\) for large \(j\), so \(\limsup _j\mu (\overline{B}(x_j, r)) \leq \mu (\overline{B}(x, r + \eta ))\) for every \(\eta {\gt} 0\), and \(\mu (\overline{B}(x, r + \eta )) \to \mu (\overline{B}(x, r))\) as \(\eta \to 0\) by continuity from above, since \(\overline{B}(x, r) = \bigcap _{\eta {\gt} 0}\overline{B}(x, r + \eta )\) and these balls have finite measure. So the sets \(\{ x : \mu (\overline{B}(x, r)) {\lt} c\} \) are open and the sets \(\{ x : \mu (\overline{B}(x, r)) \leq c\} \) are Borel. The same continuity shows that \(r \mapsto \mu (\overline{B}(x, r))\) is right continuous.

Covering. Let \(t^{\prime } {\gt} t\) and, for \(k \geq 1\),

\[ A_k = \bigl\{ x \in A : \mu (\overline{B}(x, r)) \leq t^{\prime }\alpha (s)(2r)^s \text{ for all } 0 {\lt} r {\lt} 1/k\bigr\} . \]

By right continuity it suffices to require the inequality for rational \(r\), so \(A_k\) is Borel. The sets \(A_k\) increase, and their union is \(A\), because \(\Theta ^{*s}(\mu , x) \leq t {\lt} t^{\prime }\). Fix \(k\), \(0 {\lt} \delta {\lt} 1/k\) and \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\), and assume \(\mathcal{H}^s(A_k) {\lt} \infty \) (otherwise there is nothing to prove). Choose a cover of \(A_k\) by closed sets \(C_i\) of diameter \(d_i \leq \delta \) with \(\sum _i\alpha (s)d_i^s \leq \mathcal{H}^s_\delta (A_k) + \varepsilon \), discard those not meeting \(A_k\), and pick \(x_i \in C_i \cap A_k\). If \(d_i {\gt} 0\), then \(C_i \subseteq \overline{B}(x_i, d_i)\) with \(d_i {\lt} 1/k\), so \(\mu (C_i) \leq t^{\prime }\alpha (s)(2d_i)^s = 2^st^{\prime }\alpha (s)d_i^s\). If \(d_i = 0\), then \(C_i = \{ x_i\} \) and \(\mu (C_i) \leq \mu (\overline{B}(x_i, r)) \leq t^{\prime }\alpha (s)(2r)^s\) for all small \(r\), so \(\mu (C_i) = 0\). Summing,

\[ \mu (A_k) \leq \sum _i\mu (C_i) \leq 2^st^{\prime }\bigl(\mathcal{H}^s_\delta (A_k) + \varepsilon \bigr) \leq 2^st^{\prime }\bigl(\mathcal{H}^s(A) + \varepsilon \bigr). \]

Letting \(\varepsilon \to 0\), then \(k \to \infty \) (continuity from below), then \(t^{\prime } \to t\) gives the claim.

For \(\mu = \mathcal{H}^s\llcorner A\) the argument is the proof of Theorem 28.22.4: on the set where \(\Theta ^{*s}(A, \cdot ) \leq t\) with \(t {\lt} 2^{-s}\) the theorem gives \(\mathcal{H}^s \leq 2^st\mathcal{H}^s\), which forces measure zero. The hypothesis \(t {\gt} 0\) cannot be dropped: Lebesgue measure on \(\mathbb {R}\) has \(\Theta ^{1/2}(\lambda , x) = 0\) everywhere, while \(\lambda (\mathbb {R}) {\gt} 0\) and \(\mathcal{H}^{1/2}(\mathbb {R}) = \infty \).

0390 Corollary 28.22.15

Let \(\mu \) be as in the theorem and \(s {\gt} 0\).

  1. (Absolute continuity) If \(\Theta ^{*s}(\mu , x) {\lt} \infty \) for every \(x\) in a Borel set \(A\), then every Borel set \(E \subseteq A\) with \(\mathcal{H}^s(E) = 0\) has \(\mu (E) = 0\).

  2. (Mass distribution principle) If \(A\) is a Borel set and there are \(c, r_0 {\gt} 0\) with \(\mu (\overline{B}(x, r)) \leq cr^s\) for all \(x \in A\) and \(0 {\lt} r {\lt} r_0\), then \(\mathcal{H}^s(A) \geq \alpha (s)\mu (A)/c\). In particular, if \(\mu (A) {\gt} 0\) then \(\dim _{\mathcal{H}}A \geq s\).

Proof â–¼

(1) For integers \(m, k \geq 1\) let \(E_{m,k}\) be the set of \(x \in E\) with \(\mu (\overline{B}(x, r)) \leq m\alpha (s)(2r)^s\) for \(0 {\lt} r {\lt} 1/k\), a Borel set as in the proof above, on which \(\Theta ^{*s}(\mu , \cdot ) \leq m\). By the theorem \(\mu (E_{m,k}) \leq 2^sm\mathcal{H}^s(E_{m,k}) = 0\), and \(E = \bigcup _{m,k}E_{m,k}\) because the upper density is finite on \(E\). (2) On \(A\), \(\Theta ^{*s}(\mu , x) \leq c/(2^s\alpha (s))\), so the theorem gives \(\mu (A) \leq (c/\alpha (s))\mathcal{H}^s(A)\). If \(\mu (A) {\gt} 0\), then \(\mathcal{H}^s(A) {\gt} 0\), so \(\dim _{\mathcal{H}}A \geq s\).

For the Cantor measure, (2) with \(c = 2^{1+s}\) gives \(\mathcal{H}^s(C) \geq \alpha (s)2^{-1-s}\), a little weaker than the bound \(\alpha (s)/2\) of Example 28.17.6, which estimated \(\mu \) on arbitrary small sets rather than on balls. Statement (1) says that a measure with finite upper \(s\)-density cannot charge \(\mathcal{H}^s\)-null sets; for instance a measure with finite upper \(1\)-density everywhere on \(\mathbb {R}^2\) gives no mass to the Cantor set \(C \times \{ 0\} \), of dimension \(\log 2/\log 3 {\lt} 1\).

0391 Remark 28.22.16

  1. The reverse inequality. Theorem 28.22.6 is the reverse of the theorem above: for \(\mu \) as in the theorem, a Borel set \(A\) and an open \(U \supseteq A\) with \(t_1 \leq \Theta ^{*s}(\mu , x) \leq t_2\) on \(A\), they give \(t_1\mathcal{H}^s(A) \leq \mu (U)\) (as \(\mu (U \cap \overline{B}(x, r)) = \mu (\overline{B}(x, r))\) for \(x \in A\) and small \(r\)) and \(\mu (A) \leq 2^st_2\mathcal{H}^s(A)\); taking the infimum over \(U\), which is \(\mu (A)\) by outer regularity (Lemma 28.14.1, applied on bounded pieces), \(t_1\mathcal{H}^s(A) \leq \mu (A) \leq 2^st_2\mathcal{H}^s(A)\). The factor \(2^s\) on the right is lost because the proof encloses arbitrary sets of a cover in balls; the left inequality is proved with the \(5r\)-covering lemma, which works with balls from the start. For \(\mu = \mathcal{H}^s\llcorner A\) the two inequalities are the two halves of the upper density theorem (Theorem 28.22.7).

  2. Differentiation of one measure by another. For two Radon measures \(\mu \), \(\nu \) on \(\mathbb {R}^n\), the limit \(\lim _{r \to 0}\nu (\overline{B}(x, r))/\mu (\overline{B}(x, r))\) exists for \(\mu \)-almost every \(x\) and is the Radon–Nikodym derivative of the absolutely continuous part of \(\nu \) with respect to \(\mu \) [EG92, Chapter 1]. For \(\mu = \lambda \) this is Theorem 28.16.3. For a general \(\mu \) the Vitali covering lemma is useless, since \(\mu (B(x, 3r))\) need not be comparable to \(\mu (B(x, r))\), and it is replaced by the Besicovitch covering theorem, which uses the geometry of \(\mathbb {R}^n\): if every point of a bounded set \(A\) is the centre of some ball of a family of closed balls with bounded radii, one can extract a subfamily that still covers \(A\) and in which no point lies in more than \(N(n)\) balls, a number depending only on \(n\). In a general metric space the Vitali argument survives when \(\mu \) is doubling, \(\mu (\overline{B}(x, 2r)) \leq C\mu (\overline{B}(x, r))\); see Subsection 28.22.2.

  3. Rectifiable measures. The rectifiability criteria have their natural form for measures. A Radon measure \(\mu \) on \(\mathbb {R}^n\) is \(k\)-rectifiable if \(\mu = \theta \mathcal{H}^k\llcorner E\) with \(E\) countably \(k\)-rectifiable and \(\theta \) a positive Borel function. Preiss’s theorem says that \(\mu \) is \(k\)-rectifiable if and only if \(0 {\lt} \Theta ^k(\mu , x) {\lt} \infty \) for \(\mu \)-almost every \(x\) [Pre87], and Marstrand’s theorem says that if \(0 {\lt} \Theta ^s(\mu , x) {\lt} \infty \) on a set of positive \(\mu \)-measure, then \(s\) is an integer [Mat95]. Preiss’s proof studies the tangent measures of \(\mu \) at \(x\), the weak limits (Section 28.15) of the rescaled measures \(A \mapsto \mu (x + rA)/r^k\) as \(r \to 0\); existence of the density forces them to be flat.

  4. Frostman’s lemma. The mass distribution principle has a converse: if \(A \subseteq \mathbb {R}^n\) is a Borel set with \(\mathcal{H}^s(A) {\gt} 0\), there is a nonzero finite Borel measure \(\mu \) supported on a compact subset of \(A\) with \(\mu (\overline{B}(x, r)) \leq r^s\) for all \(x\) and \(r\) [Mat95]. So \(\dim _{\mathcal{H}}A\) is the supremum of the \(s\) for which \(A\) carries a nonzero measure with \(\mu (\overline{B}(x, r)) \leq r^s\): Hausdorff dimension can be computed with measures instead of covers.

28.22.2 Differentiation with respect to doubling measures0395

Densities measure a measure \(\mu \) against the function \(r \mapsto \alpha (s)(2r)^s\). Measuring it against another measure \(\nu \) instead, through the ratios \(\mu (\overline{B}(x, r))/\nu (\overline{B}(x, r))\), leads to the general form of the Radon–Nikodym theorem in which the derivative \(d\mu /d\nu \) is computed as a limit, as in Theorem 28.16.3 for \(\nu = \lambda \). The proof there used \(\lambda (3B) = 3^n\lambda (B)\), and the \(5r\)-covering lemma (Lemma 28.22.5) shows that a weak form of this property is all one needs, in any metric space. Throughout, \(X\) is a nonempty metric space, and for a Borel measure \(\nu \) on \(X\) and any \(A \subseteq X\) we write \(\nu ^*(A) = \inf \{ \nu (E) : E \supseteq A \text{ Borel}\} \). This outer measure is countably subadditive (take a union of Borel supersets), and a set with \(\nu ^*(A) = 0\) lies in a Borel set of measure \(0\).

0396 Definition 28.22.17

A Borel measure \(\nu \) on \(X\) is doubling if \(0 {\lt} \nu (\overline{B}(x, r)) {\lt} \infty \) for all \(x \in X\) and \(r {\gt} 0\), and there is a constant \(C\) with

\[ \nu (\overline{B}(x, 2r)) \leq C\, \nu (\overline{B}(x, r)) \qquad \text{for all } x \in X,\ r {\gt} 0 . \]

Iterating, \(\nu (\overline{B}(x, 2^kr)) \leq C^k\nu (\overline{B}(x, r))\); in particular, for the enlarged ball \(\widehat{B} = \overline{B}(x, 5r)\) of \(B = \overline{B}(x, r)\),

\begin{equation} \nu (\widehat{B}) \leq \nu (\overline{B}(x, 8r)) \leq C^3\nu (B) . \end{equation}
28.6

A doubling measure is finite on bounded sets, since every bounded set lies in a closed ball, and hence \(\sigma \)-finite: \(X = \bigcup _k\overline{B}(x_0, k)\) for any \(x_0 \in X\).

0398 Example 28.22.18

  1. Lebesgue measure on \(\mathbb {R}^n\) is doubling with \(C = 2^n\), since \(\lambda (\overline{B}(x, r)) = \alpha (n)(2r)^n\).

  2. If \(w\) is a Borel function on \(\mathbb {R}^n\) with \(0 {\lt} m \leq w \leq M\), then \(\nu = w\lambda \) is doubling with \(C = 2^nM/m\): \(\nu (\overline{B}(x, 2r)) \leq M\lambda (\overline{B}(x, 2r)) = 2^nM\lambda (\overline{B}(x, r)) \leq 2^n(M/m)\nu (\overline{B}(x, r))\).

  3. The Gaussian measure \(\gamma = e^{-y^2/2}\lambda \) on \(\mathbb {R}\) is not doubling. For \(x {\gt} 2\) the density \(e^{-y^2/2}\) is decreasing on \([x - 2, x + 2]\), so

    \[ \frac{\gamma ([x - 2, x + 2])}{\gamma ([x - 1, x + 1])} \geq \frac{\frac12e^{-(x - 3/2)^2/2}}{2e^{-(x - 1)^2/2}} = \frac14e^{(x - 5/4)/2}, \]

    estimating the numerator by the integral over \([x - 2, x - \frac32]\); the right side is unbounded as \(x \to \infty \). Far out, the measure of a ball is concentrated near its left end.

  4. A point mass \(\delta _a\) on \(\mathbb {R}\) is not doubling, since balls missing \(a\) have measure \(0\); but \(\delta _a\) is doubling on the one-point metric space \(\{ a\} \). Doubling is a property of the measure together with the metric space it lives on.

We need one approximation lemma, which replaces the role of \(C_c(\mathbb {R}^n)\) in the Lebesgue differentiation theorem.

0399 Lemma 28.22.19

Let \(\nu \) be a finite Borel measure on a metric space \(X\) and \(1 \leq p {\lt} \infty \). The bounded Lipschitz functions are dense in \(L^p(\nu )\).

Proof â–¼

By Proposition 28.2.6(4) and dominated convergence, simple functions \(\sum _ia_i\mathbf{1}_{E_i}\) with \(E_i\) Borel are dense, so it suffices to approximate \(\mathbf{1}_E\) for a Borel set \(E\). Let \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\). By Lemma 28.14.1 there is a closed set \(F \subseteq E\) with \(\nu (E \setminus F) {\lt} \varepsilon \), so \(\| \mathbf{1}_E - \mathbf{1}_F\| _p^p {\lt} \varepsilon \). If \(F = \emptyset \), then \(\| \mathbf{1}_E\| _p^p {\lt} \varepsilon \) and \(0\) approximates. Otherwise let \(g_k(x) = \max (0, 1 - k\, d(x, F))\). It is Lipschitz with constant \(k\), takes values in \([0, 1]\), equals \(1\) on \(F\), and \(g_k(x) \to 0\) for \(x \notin F\) because \(d(x, F) {\gt} 0\) for such \(x\), \(F\) being closed. So \(g_k \to \mathbf{1}_F\) pointwise with \(|g_k - \mathbf{1}_F| \leq 1\), and as \(\nu \) is finite, dominated convergence gives \(\| g_k - \mathbf{1}_F\| _p \to 0\).

From now on \(\nu \) is a doubling measure on \(X\) with constant \(C\).

039A Theorem 28.22.20 Maximal inequality for doubling measures

Let \(f \in L^1(\nu )\) and \(t {\gt} 0\), and let \(A\) be the set of \(x \in X\) such that

\[ \frac{1}{\nu (\overline{B}(x, r))}\int _{\overline{B}(x, r)}|f|\, d\nu {\gt} t \qquad \text{for some } 0 {\lt} r \leq 1 . \]

Then \(\nu ^*(A) \leq C^3\| f\| _1/t\).

Proof â–¼

Let \(\mathcal{F}\) be the family of balls \(B = \overline{B}(x, r)\) with \(x \in A\), \(0 {\lt} r \leq 1\) and \(\int _B|f|\, d\nu {\gt} t\nu (B)\); every point of \(A\) is the centre of a ball of \(\mathcal{F}\). Let \(\mathcal{G}\) be the disjoint subfamily of Lemma 28.22.5. For finitely many balls of \(\mathcal{G}\) the integrals \(\int _B|f|\, d\nu \) add up to at most \(\| f\| _1\), and each of them is \({\gt} t\nu (B) {\gt} 0\); so for each \(m\) only finitely many exceed \(1/m\), and \(\mathcal{G} = \{ B_1, B_2, \ldots \} \) is countable. Every ball of \(\mathcal{F}\) lies in some \(\widehat{B_i}\), so \(A \subseteq \bigcup _i\widehat{B_i}\), and by 28.6

\[ \nu ^*(A) \leq \sum _i\nu (\widehat{B_i}) \leq C^3\sum _i\nu (B_i) {\lt} \frac{C^3}{t}\sum _i\int _{B_i}|f|\, d\nu \leq \frac{C^3}{t}\| f\| _1 . \]

(If \(A\) is empty there is nothing to prove.)

039B Theorem 28.22.21 Lebesgue differentiation theorem for doubling measures

Let \(f \colon X \to \mathbb {R}\) be a Borel function with \(\int _B|f|\, d\nu {\lt} \infty \) for every closed ball \(B\). For \(\nu \)-almost every \(x \in X\),

\[ \lim _{r \to 0}\frac{1}{\nu (\overline{B}(x, r))}\int _{\overline{B}(x, r)}|f(y) - f(x)|\, d\nu (y) = 0, \]

and in particular the averages of \(f\) over \(\overline{B}(x, r)\) tend to \(f(x)\).

Proof â–¼

Write \(\langle g \rangle _{x, r}\) for the average of a function \(g\) over \(\overline{B}(x, r)\) with respect to \(\nu \), and \(\Omega (x) = \limsup _{r \to 0}\langle |f - f(x)|\rangle _{x, r}\). Fix \(x_0 \in X\), an integer \(k \geq 1\), \(t {\gt} 0\) and \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\), and let \(D = \overline{B}(x_0, k + 1)\). The measure \(E \mapsto \nu (E \cap D)\) is finite, so by Lemma 28.22.19 there is a bounded continuous \(g\) with \(\int _D|f - g|\, d\nu {\lt} \varepsilon \). Let \(h = (f - g)\mathbf{1}_D\), so \(\| h\| _1 {\lt} \varepsilon \).

Let \(x \in B(x_0, k)\) and \(0 {\lt} r \leq 1\). Then \(\overline{B}(x, r) \subseteq D\), and for \(y \in \overline{B}(x, r)\)

\[ |f(y) - f(x)| \leq |h(y)| + |h(x)| + |g(y) - g(x)| . \]

Averaging over \(y\) gives \(\langle |f - f(x)|\rangle _{x, r} \leq \langle |h|\rangle _{x, r} + |h(x)| + \sup _{\overline{B}(x, r)}|g - g(x)|\), and the last term tends to \(0\) as \(r \to 0\) because \(g\) is continuous. So, if \(\Omega (x) {\gt} 2t\), then either \(\langle |h|\rangle _{x, r} {\gt} t\) for some \(r \leq 1\), or \(|h(x)| {\gt} t\). By Theorem 28.22.20 and Markov’s inequality (Corollary 28.4.4(4)),

\[ \nu ^*\bigl(\{ x \in B(x_0, k) : \Omega (x) {\gt} 2t\} \bigr) \leq \frac{C^3\varepsilon }{t} + \frac{\varepsilon }{t} . \]

As \(\varepsilon \) is arbitrary, this set has outer measure \(0\). The set where \(\Omega {\gt} 0\) is the union of these sets over \(k \geq 1\) and \(t = 1/j\), \(j \geq 1\), so it has outer measure \(0\) and lies in a \(\nu \)-null Borel set. Finally \(|\langle f \rangle _{x, r} - f(x)| \leq \langle |f - f(x)|\rangle _{x, r}\).

039C Theorem 28.22.22 Differentiation of measures with respect to a doubling measure

Let \(\mu \) be a Borel measure on \(X\) that is finite on bounded Borel sets, and let \(\mu = h\nu + \mu _s\) be its Lebesgue decomposition with respect to \(\nu \) (Theorem 28.12.3), with \(h \geq 0\) Borel and \(\mu _s \perp \nu \). Then for \(\nu \)-almost every \(x\)

\[ \lim _{r \to 0}\frac{\mu (\overline{B}(x, r))}{\nu (\overline{B}(x, r))} = h(x) . \]

In particular, if \(\mu \ll \nu \), the Radon–Nikodym derivative is \(\frac{d\mu }{d\nu }(x) = \lim _{r \to 0}\mu (\overline{B}(x, r))/\nu (\overline{B}(x, r))\) for \(\nu \)-almost every \(x\); and \(\mu \perp \nu \) if and only if this limit is \(0\) \(\nu \)-almost everywhere.

Proof â–¼

Both measures are \(\sigma \)-finite, \(X\) being the union of the balls \(\overline{B}(x_0, k)\), so the decomposition exists. For every closed ball \(B\), \(\int _Bh\, d\nu \leq \mu (B) {\lt} \infty \), so by Theorem 28.22.21 the averages of \(h\) over \(\overline{B}(x, r)\) tend to \(h(x)\) for almost every \(x\). Since

\[ \frac{\mu (\overline{B}(x, r))}{\nu (\overline{B}(x, r))} = \frac{1}{\nu (\overline{B}(x, r))}\int _{\overline{B}(x, r)}h\, d\nu + \frac{\mu _s(\overline{B}(x, r))}{\nu (\overline{B}(x, r))}, \]

it remains to show that the last ratio tends to \(0\) for almost every \(x\).

Let \(S\) be a Borel set with \(\nu (S) = 0\) and \(\mu _s(X \setminus S) = 0\). Fix \(x_0 \in X\), \(k \geq 1\), \(t {\gt} 0\) and \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\), and let \(A\) be the set of \(x \in B(x_0, k) \setminus S\) with \(\limsup _{r \to 0}\mu _s(\overline{B}(x, r))/\nu (\overline{B}(x, r)) {\gt} t\). The measure \(E \mapsto \mu _s(E \cap B(x_0, k + 1))\) is finite and vanishes on the Borel set \(B(x_0, k) \setminus S\), so by Lemma 28.14.1 there is an open \(U^{\prime } \supseteq B(x_0, k) \setminus S\) with \(\mu _s(U^{\prime } \cap B(x_0, k + 1)) {\lt} \varepsilon \). The set \(U = U^{\prime } \cap B(x_0, k + 1)\) is open, contains \(A\), and has \(\mu _s(U) {\lt} \varepsilon \).

Let \(\mathcal{F}\) be the family of balls \(B = \overline{B}(x, r)\) with \(x \in A\), \(r \leq 1\), \(B \subseteq U\) and \(\mu _s(B) {\gt} t\nu (B)\). Each \(x \in A\) is the centre of such balls, because \(\overline{B}(x, r) \subseteq U\) for small \(r\). Let \(\mathcal{G}\) be the disjoint subfamily of Lemma 28.22.5. Its balls lie in the bounded set \(U\), which has finite \(\nu \)-measure, and have positive \(\nu \)-measure, so \(\mathcal{G} = \{ B_1, B_2, \ldots \} \) is countable, as in the proof of Theorem 28.22.20. As there, \(A \subseteq \bigcup _i\widehat{B_i}\) and

\[ \nu ^*(A) \leq C^3\sum _i\nu (B_i) {\lt} \frac{C^3}{t}\sum _i\mu _s(B_i) \leq \frac{C^3}{t}\mu _s(U) {\lt} \frac{C^3\varepsilon }{t} . \]

So \(\nu ^*(A) = 0\). Taking the union over \(k\) and \(t = 1/j\), and adding the null set \(S\), the ratio \(\mu _s(\overline{B}(x, r))/\nu (\overline{B}(x, r))\) tends to \(0\) outside a \(\nu \)-null set.

If \(\mu \ll \nu \), then \(\mu _s = 0\) by uniqueness of the decomposition, and \(h = d\mu /d\nu \) is the Radon–Nikodym derivative (Theorem 28.12.2). If \(\mu \perp \nu \), then \(h\nu = 0\) by uniqueness, so \(h = 0\) almost everywhere; conversely, if the limit is \(0\) almost everywhere, then \(h = 0\) almost everywhere and \(\mu = \mu _s\).

So the Radon–Nikodym derivative, obtained abstractly in Theorem 28.12.2, is a limit of ratios of measures of balls whenever the reference measure is doubling. For \(f \in L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}\) and \(\mu = f\nu \) the theorem is the Lebesgue differentiation theorem again; for \(\mu = \nu \llcorner E\), \(E\) Borel, it is a density theorem: \(\nu (E \cap \overline{B}(x, r))/\nu (\overline{B}(x, r))\) tends to \(1\) at almost every point of \(E\) and to \(0\) at almost every point outside \(E\).

039D Remark 28.22.23

  1. Densities as derivatives. The density \(\Theta ^s(\mu , x)\) is the derivative of \(\mu \) with respect to the function \(r \mapsto \alpha (s)(2r)^s\) in place of a measure \(\nu \). Since this function is not the measure of the balls of a fixed measure, there is no Lebesgue decomposition to recover, and one only gets the inequalities of Theorems 28.22.6 and 28.22.14. The proofs are parallel: the singular part was handled above exactly as in Theorem 28.22.6, with \(\nu (B)\) in place of \(\alpha (s)(2r)^s\) for \(B = \overline{B}(x, r)\), and with 28.6 in place of \(\alpha (s)(10r)^s = 5^s\alpha (s)(2r)^s\).

  2. Beyond doubling. Doubling is sufficient, not necessary. On \(\mathbb {R}^n\) every Radon measure can serve as \(\nu \), by the Besicovitch covering theorem (Remark 28.16.6 and Remark 28.22.16(2)); so the non-doubling Gaussian measure of Example 28.22.18(3) still differentiates. In general metric spaces some covering property is needed; spaces with a doubling measure, the spaces of homogeneous type, are the standard setting for analysis on metric spaces [Hei01, Chapter 1].

  3. Ahlfors regularity. A measure \(\nu \) with \(c^{-1}r^s \leq \nu (\overline{B}(x, r)) \leq cr^s\) for all \(x \in X\) and \(0 {\lt} r \leq \operatorname {diam}X\) (so \(c \geq 1\)) is doubling with \(C = 2^sc^2\): if \(2r \leq \operatorname {diam}X\), then \(\nu (\overline{B}(x, 2r)) \leq c2^sr^s \leq 2^sc^2\nu (\overline{B}(x, r))\); if \(r \leq \operatorname {diam}X {\lt} 2r\), then \(\nu (\overline{B}(x, 2r)) = \nu (\overline{B}(x, \operatorname {diam}X)) \leq c(2r)^s \leq 2^sc^2\nu (\overline{B}(x, r))\); and if \(r {\gt} \operatorname {diam}X\), both balls are \(X\). For such a measure, \(\nu \) and \(\mathcal{H}^s\) are comparable on Borel sets \(A\): \(\nu (A) \leq (c/\alpha (s))\mathcal{H}^s(A)\) by Theorem 28.22.14, and \(\mathcal{H}^s(A) \leq 2^sc\, \alpha (s)\nu (U)\) for every open \(U \supseteq A\) by Theorem 28.22.6 with \(A_1 = A\), \(A_2 = U\), which gives \(\mathcal{H}^s(A) \leq 2^sc\, \alpha (s)\nu (A)\) by outer regularity (Lemma 28.14.1, applied on bounded pieces). The Cantor measure on the Cantor set \(C\) is of this kind with \(s = \log 2/\log 3\): Example 28.22.13(4) gives the upper bound, and for \(x \in C\) and \(3^{-k} \leq r {\lt} 3^{1-k}\) the ball \(\overline{B}(x, r)\) contains the interval of the \(k\)-th stage of the construction that contains \(x\), of measure \(2^{-k} = (3^{-k})^s \geq 3^{-s}r^s\).

039E Exercise 28.22.24

Let \(\nu \) be a doubling measure on \(X\) with constant \(C\).

  1. Let \(0 {\lt} \varepsilon \leq R\) and let \(m \geq 0\) be an integer with \(2^m\varepsilon \geq 2R\). Show that a set of points of \(\overline{B}(x, R)\) with pairwise distances \({\gt} 2\varepsilon \) has at most \(C^{m+1}\) elements.

  2. Deduce that closed balls in \(X\) are totally bounded, that \(X\) is separable, and that if \(X\) is complete, closed balls are compact.

  3. Deduce that the Hilbert space \(\ell ^2\) of square-summable real sequences carries no doubling measure.

Solution â–¼

(1) Let \(y_1, \ldots , y_N \in \overline{B}(x, R)\) with \(d(y_i, y_j) {\gt} 2\varepsilon \) for \(i \neq j\). The balls \(\overline{B}(y_i, \varepsilon )\) are pairwise disjoint and lie in \(\overline{B}(x, R + \varepsilon ) \subseteq \overline{B}(x, 2R)\). Moreover \(\overline{B}(x, R) \subseteq \overline{B}(y_i, 2R)\), so \(\nu (\overline{B}(x, R)) \leq \nu (\overline{B}(y_i, 2R)) \leq C^m\nu (\overline{B}(y_i, 2^{-m}2R)) \leq C^m\nu (\overline{B}(y_i, \varepsilon ))\). Summing over \(i\),

\[ NC^{-m}\nu (\overline{B}(x, R)) \leq \sum _i\nu (\overline{B}(y_i, \varepsilon )) \leq \nu (\overline{B}(x, 2R)) \leq C\nu (\overline{B}(x, R)), \]

and dividing by \(\nu (\overline{B}(x, R)) {\gt} 0\) gives \(N \leq C^{m+1}\).

(2) Given \(\varepsilon \leq R\), choose a set \(\{ y_1, \ldots , y_N\} \subseteq \overline{B}(x, R)\) with pairwise distances \({\gt} 2\varepsilon \) and with \(N\) as large as possible, which is possible by (1). Every \(y \in \overline{B}(x, R)\) has \(d(y, y_i) \leq 2\varepsilon \) for some \(i\), since otherwise \(y\) could be added. So finitely many open balls of radius \(3\varepsilon \) cover \(\overline{B}(x, R)\); for \(\varepsilon {\gt} R\) the single ball \(B(x, 3\varepsilon )\) does. Hence \(\overline{B}(x, R)\) is totally bounded. A totally bounded metric space is separable (the centres of finite \(1/j\)-nets, \(j \geq 1\), form a dense countable set), and \(X = \bigcup _k\overline{B}(x_0, k)\) is a countable union of such spaces, so \(X\) is separable. A closed subset of a complete space is complete, so if \(X\) is complete, closed balls are compact by Theorem 18.7.4.

(3) The unit vectors \(e_1, e_2, \ldots \) lie in the closed unit ball of \(\ell ^2\) and have pairwise distances \(\| e_i - e_j\| = \sqrt2\). So the closed unit ball is not totally bounded: no finitely many balls of radius \(\frac12\) cover it, since each such ball contains at most one \(e_i\). By (2), there is no doubling measure on \(\ell ^2\). Doubling measures live on spaces that are “finite-dimensional” in a metric sense.
