43.3 Divisors01EO

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

01EP Definition 43.3.1

A divisor on \(X\) is a locally finite formal sum \(D = \sum _ia_iZ_i\) with \(a_i \in \mathbb {Z}\) and \(Z_i\) irreducible analytic hypersurfaces; they form the group \(\mathrm{Div}(X)\). A meromorphic function is a section of the sheaf \(\mathcal{M}_X\) of quotients \(f/g\) of holomorphic functions with \(g \not\equiv 0\); the order \(\operatorname {ord}_Z(f) \in \mathbb {Z}\) of a meromorphic function along an irreducible hypersurface \(Z\) is defined by writing \(f = u\, h^m\) near a regular point of \(Z\), where \(h\) is a local defining function of \(Z\) and \(u\) is a unit, using that \(\mathcal{O}_{X,x}\) is a unique factorisation domain (Theorem 30.3.4). The divisor of \(f\) is \((f) = \sum _Z\operatorname {ord}_Z(f)\, Z\), and divisors of this form are principal.

01EQ Proposition 43.3.2

There is a homomorphism \(\mathrm{Div}(X) \to \operatorname {Pic}(X)\), \(D \mapsto \mathcal{O}(D)\), defined by taking local defining functions \(f_i\) for \(D\) on a cover and the cocycle \(g_{ij} = f_i/f_j \in \mathcal{O}^*\). Its kernel is the group of principal divisors, and \(\mathcal{O}(D)\) has a canonical meromorphic section with divisor \(D\); for \(D\) effective (all \(a_i \geq 0\)) this section is holomorphic and vanishes exactly on \(D\). Conversely a nonzero meromorphic section of a line bundle \(L\) has a divisor \(D\) with \(L \cong \mathcal{O}(D)\).

Proof â–¼

The functions \(f_i/f_j\) are nowhere vanishing and holomorphic on overlaps, because two local defining functions of the same divisor differ by a unit (Theorem 30.3.4), and they clearly form a cocycle; changing the \(f_i\) changes the cocycle by a coboundary. The local functions \(f_i\) glue to a meromorphic section of \(\mathcal{O}(D)\), whose divisor is \(D\). If \(\mathcal{O}(D)\) is trivial, the cocycle is a coboundary \(f_i/f_j = u_i/u_j\), so the \(f_i/u_i\) glue to a global meromorphic function with divisor \(D\); conversely, principal divisors give trivial cocycles. The last statement follows by taking the divisor of the section and comparing cocycles.

01ER Example 43.3.3

On \(\mathbb {C}\mathbb {P}^n\) every irreducible hypersurface is the zero set of an irreducible homogeneous polynomial, and \(\mathcal{O}(D) \cong \mathcal{O}(\deg D)\); the map \(\deg \colon \mathrm{Div}(\mathbb {C}\mathbb {P}^n) \to \mathbb {Z}\) induces the isomorphism \(\operatorname {Pic}(\mathbb {C}\mathbb {P}^n) \cong \mathbb {Z}\), which also follows from Theorem 43.2.4 since \(H^1(\mathbb {C}\mathbb {P}^n, \mathcal{O}) = H^2(\mathbb {C}\mathbb {P}^n, \mathcal{O}) = 0\) (Chapter 44). On a complex torus \(X = \mathbb {C}^n/\Lambda \), by contrast, \(H^1(X, \mathcal{O}_X) \neq 0\) and \(\operatorname {Pic}^0(X)\) is a nontrivial complex torus, the dual torus.

02AY Counterexample 43.3.4

Holomorphic line bundles are not determined by their topology. Let \(X = \mathbb {C}/\Lambda \) with \(\Lambda = \mathbb {Z}\omega _1 + \mathbb {Z}\omega _2\) a lattice, numbered so that \(\operatorname {Im}(\omega _2/\omega _1) {\gt} 0\), \(\pi \colon \mathbb {C}\to X\) the projection, and \(p \neq q\) points of \(X\). The line bundle \(L = \mathcal{O}(p - q) = \mathcal{O}(p) \otimes \mathcal{O}(q)^\vee \) is trivial as a smooth bundle but not as a holomorphic bundle.

Smooth triviality. Lift \(q - p\) to \(c \in \mathbb {C}\) and let \(\tau (x) = x + c\), a biholomorphism of \(X\) with \(\tau (p) = q\). A local defining function of \(q\) composed with \(\tau \) is a local defining function of \(p\), so \(\tau ^*\mathcal{O}(q) \cong \mathcal{O}(p)\). The translations \(x \mapsto x + tc\), \(0 \leq t \leq 1\), form a homotopy from the identity to \(\tau \), so by Theorem 34.4.2 and homotopy invariance (Theorem 21.2.2), \(c_1(\mathcal{O}(p)) = \tau ^*c_1(\mathcal{O}(q)) = c_1(\mathcal{O}(q))\). Hence \(c_1(L) = 0\) and \(L\) is smoothly trivial, by the same theorem.

Holomorphic nontriviality. If \(L\) were holomorphically trivial, the divisor \(p - q\) would be principal (Proposition 43.3.2): there would be a meromorphic function \(f\) on \(X\) whose only pole is a simple pole at \(q\). Then \(F = f \circ \pi \) is meromorphic on \(\mathbb {C}\), invariant under translation by \(\Lambda \), with simple poles exactly at the points of \(\pi ^{-1}(q)\). Choose a lift \(\tilde q\) of \(q\) and let \(P\) be the parallelogram with vertices \(a\), \(a + \omega _1\), \(a + \omega _1 + \omega _2\), \(a + \omega _2\), where \(a = \tilde q - \frac12(\omega _1 + \omega _2)\). A boundary point \(\tilde q + (s - \frac12)\omega _1 + (t - \frac12)\omega _2\) has \(s\) or \(t\) in \(\{ 0, 1\} \), so it is not in \(\tilde q + \Lambda \); and \(\tilde q\) is the only point of \(\tilde q + \Lambda \) inside \(P\). The boundary \(\partial P\), traversed through the vertices in the order listed, is positively oriented, with winding number \(1\) about \(\tilde q\) and \(0\) about the points outside \(P\). By the residue theorem (Theorem 28.5.8), \(\int _{\partial P}F\, dz = 2\pi i\operatorname {Res}_{\tilde q}F \neq 0\), the residue at a simple pole being nonzero. On the other hand the integrals over opposite sides of \(P\) cancel: the side from \(a + \omega _2\) to \(a + \omega _1 + \omega _2\) is the translate by \(\omega _2\) of the side from \(a\) to \(a + \omega _1\), traversed in the opposite direction, and \(F(z + \omega _2) = F(z)\); similarly for the other pair. So \(\int _{\partial P}F\, dz = 0\), a contradiction.

Thus \(L\) is a nontrivial element of \(\operatorname {Pic}^0(X)\), the kernel of \(c_1\) in Theorem 43.2.4. The same argument shows that no meromorphic function on \(X\) has a single pole which is simple.
