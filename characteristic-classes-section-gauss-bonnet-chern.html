38.6 The Euler form and the Gauss–Bonnet–Chern theorem02N4

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

Chern–Weil theory for complex bundles uses polynomials that are invariant under all of \(\mathrm{GL}_r(\mathbb {C})\). A real oriented vector bundle with a metric has more structure: in oriented orthonormal frames the curvature of a metric connection is a skew-symmetric matrix of \(2\)-forms, and the changes of frame take values in \(\mathrm{SO}(n)\). Skew-symmetric matrices have an invariant that is not a polynomial in the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial, the Pfaffian (Definition 11.4.7), a square root of the determinant. Applied to the curvature it gives a closed form, the Euler form. This section proves, following Chern [Che44], that when the rank of the bundle equals the dimension of the base, the integral of the Euler form counts the zeros of any section, with signs:

\[ \int _Me(\nabla ) = \sum _{s(z) = 0}\operatorname {ind}_z(s) . \]

For the tangent bundle the right side is the Euler characteristic by the Poincaré–Hopf theorem, and the formula becomes the Gauss–Bonnet–Chern theorem \(\int _Me(TM, \nabla ) = \chi (M)\), the higher-dimensional Gauss–Bonnet theorem. Chern’s proof is intrinsic: earlier proofs, by Allendoerfer, Fenchel and Weil, used isometric embeddings of \(M\), or of pieces of it, into Euclidean spaces, while Chern only computes on the unit sphere bundle of \(E\), where the pullback of the Euler form becomes exact, with an explicit primitive. The surface case, in which the whole argument fits in a few lines, is worked out in Example 38.6.11, and it may help to read it first.

On a first reading one can read the definitions and the statements, the example of surfaces, and Corollary 38.6.13; the transgression computation (Lemma 38.6.9 and Proposition 38.6.10) is not used elsewhere.

Throughout, \(E \to M\) is an oriented real vector bundle of rank \(n\) with a metric \(\langle \ ,\ \rangle \) on its fibres, and \(\nabla \) is a metric connection (Proposition 37.6.2). Near every point there are oriented orthonormal frames \(e = (e_1, \ldots , e_n)\), obtained from any oriented frame by the Gram–Schmidt process. Since such a frame is orthonormal we write its connection form and curvature with lower indices, \(\theta _{ij} = \theta ^i_j\) and \(F_{ij} = F^i_j\), so that

\[ \nabla e_j = \sum _i\theta _{ij} \otimes e_i, \qquad F_{ij} = d\theta _{ij} + \sum _k\theta _{ik} \wedge \theta _{kj}, \qquad dF = F \wedge \theta - \theta \wedge F \]

(Propositions 37.3.4 and 37.4.3). Both matrices are skew-symmetric, \(\theta _{ji} = -\theta _{ij}\) and \(F_{ji} = -F_{ij}\), by Proposition 37.6.2. Another oriented orthonormal frame is \(e^{\prime } = eg\) with \(g \colon U \to \mathrm{SO}(n)\), and then \(\theta ^{\prime } = g^{-1}\theta g + g^{-1}dg\) and \(F^{\prime } = g^{-1}Fg = g^TFg\).

02N5 Lemma 38.6.1

Let \(x \in M\) and let \((b_1, \ldots , b_n)\) be an oriented orthonormal basis of \(E_x\). There is an oriented orthonormal frame \(e\) on a neighbourhood of \(x\) with \(e_i(x) = b_i\) and whose connection form vanishes at \(x\), \(\theta (x) = 0\).

Proof â–¼

Let \(f\) be an oriented orthonormal frame on a coordinate neighbourhood of \(x\), with coordinates \(x^1, \ldots , x^d\) vanishing at \(x\), and connection form \(\theta _f = \sum _k\theta _k\, dx^k\) with skew-symmetric matrices of functions \(\theta _k\). Let \(A_k = \theta _k(x)\) and let \(h \in \mathrm{SO}(n)\) be the matrix with \(f(x)h = (b_1, \ldots , b_n)\). Put \(g = \exp \bigl(-\sum _kx^kA_k\bigr)h\). The exponential of a skew-symmetric matrix \(B\) is orthogonal, as \(\exp (B)^T = \exp (-B) = \exp (B)^{-1}\), with determinant \(e^{\operatorname {tr}B} = 1\); so \(g\) takes values in \(\mathrm{SO}(n)\) and \(e = fg\) is an oriented orthonormal frame with \(e(x) = f(x)h = b\). At \(x\) we have \(g(x) = h\) and \(dg(x) = -\sum _kA_k\, dx^k\, h = -\theta _f(x)h\), so \(\theta _e(x) = h^{-1}\theta _f(x)h + h^{-1}dg(x) = 0\).

Such a frame is the analogue of normal coordinates: at the single point \(x\) the connection looks like the trivial one, while the curvature, which is a tensor, cannot be removed. It simplifies computations of quantities that do not depend on the frame: the second Bianchi identity says that \(dF(x) = 0\) in such a frame.

Let \(n = 2m\) be even. Since \(2\)-forms commute, the Pfaffian can be applied to the skew-symmetric matrix of \(2\)-forms \(F\), using the formula of Proposition 11.4.8(1):

\[ \mathrm{Pf}(F) = \frac{1}{2^mm!}\sum _{\sigma \in S_n}\operatorname {sgn}(\sigma )\, F_{\sigma (1)\sigma (2)} \wedge F_{\sigma (3)\sigma (4)} \wedge \cdots \wedge F_{\sigma (n-1)\sigma (n)} . \]
02N6 Definition 38.6.2

For \(n = 2m\), the Euler form of \((E, \nabla )\) is the \(n\)-form

\[ e(\nabla ) = \mathrm{Pf}\Bigl(\frac{F}{2\pi }\Bigr) = \frac{1}{(2\pi )^m}\mathrm{Pf}(F) \in \Omega ^n(M), \]

computed in any oriented orthonormal frame. For \(n = 2\), \(e(\nabla ) = \frac{1}{2\pi }F_{12}\); for \(n = 4\), \(e(\nabla ) = \frac{1}{4\pi ^2}(F_{12} \wedge F_{34} - F_{13} \wedge F_{24} + F_{14} \wedge F_{23})\).

02N7 Proposition 38.6.3

Let \(n = 2m\).

  1. \(e(\nabla )\) does not depend on the oriented orthonormal frame, so it is a global \(n\)-form, and reversing the orientation of \(E\) changes its sign.

  2. \(e(\nabla )\) is closed.

  3. Its class in \(H^n_{\mathrm{dR}}(M)\) does not depend on the metric connection \(\nabla \).

  4. It is natural: \(e(f^*\nabla ) = f^*e(\nabla )\) for a smooth map \(f \colon N \to M\).

  5. If \(E\) is the underlying oriented real bundle of a complex line bundle \(L\) with a Hermitian metric \(h\), with the metric \(\operatorname {Re}h\), and \(\nabla \) is a metric connection on \(L\), then \(e(\nabla ) = c_1(L, \nabla )\).

Proof â–¼

(1) For \(e^{\prime } = eg\) with \(g\) in \(\mathrm{SO}(n)\), \(\mathrm{Pf}(F^{\prime }) = \mathrm{Pf}(g^TFg) = \det (g)\mathrm{Pf}(F) = \mathrm{Pf}(F)\) by Proposition 11.4.8(2), which applies to matrices with entries in the commutative algebra of even forms at a point. Reversing the orientation replaces oriented frames by frames \(eg\) with \(\det g = -1\).

(2) \(\mathrm{Pf}(F)\) is a polynomial with constant coefficients in the \(2\)-forms \(F_{ij}\), which commute, so by the Leibniz rule \(d\, \mathrm{Pf}(F)\) is a sum of terms each containing one factor \(dF_{ij}\). At a point \(x\) compute in a frame as in Lemma 38.6.1: there \(dF(x) = (F \wedge \theta - \theta \wedge F)(x) = 0\), so \(d\, \mathrm{Pf}(F)\) vanishes at \(x\). As \(x\) is arbitrary and \(\mathrm{Pf}(F)\) does not depend on the frame, \(d\, \mathrm{Pf}(F) = 0\).

(3) Let \(\widetilde{\mathrm{Pf}}\) be the polarisation of \(\mathrm{Pf}\), the symmetric \(m\)-linear form on skew-symmetric matrices with \(\widetilde{\mathrm{Pf}}(A, \ldots , A) = \mathrm{Pf}(A)\); it is given by a universal formula in terms of \(\mathrm{Pf}\) (for instance \(m!\, \widetilde{\mathrm{Pf}}(A_1, \ldots , A_m)\) is the coefficient of \(t_1t_2 \cdots t_m\) in \(\mathrm{Pf}(t_1A_1 + \cdots + t_mA_m)\)), so it is invariant under \(A_i \mapsto g^TA_ig\) for \(g \in \mathrm{SO}(n)\). Differentiating this invariance along \(g = \exp (tB)\) with \(B\) skew-symmetric, for which \(\frac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0}\exp (tB)^TA\exp (tB) = AB - BA\), gives \(\sum _i\widetilde{\mathrm{Pf}}(A_1, \ldots , [B, A_i], \ldots , A_m) = 0\) for skew-symmetric \(B\) and \(A_i\). The proof of Lemma 38.2.3 uses only this infinitesimal invariance, for \(B\) the components of the connection form, and these are skew-symmetric for a metric connection in an orthonormal frame. So that lemma holds for \(\widetilde{\mathrm{Pf}}\) and skew-symmetric matrices of forms, and the proof of Theorem 38.2.4(2) applies word for word: for metric connections \(\nabla _0\), \(\nabla _1\) the connections \(\nabla _t = \nabla _0 + t(\nabla _1 - \nabla _0)\) are metric, the matrix of \(\alpha = \nabla _1 - \nabla _0\) in an orthonormal frame is skew-symmetric, and \(\mathrm{Pf}(F_1) - \mathrm{Pf}(F_0) = d\bigl(m\int _0^1\widetilde{\mathrm{Pf}}(\alpha , F_t, \ldots , F_t)\, dt\bigr)\), where the form in brackets does not depend on the oriented orthonormal frame by the invariance of \(\widetilde{\mathrm{Pf}}\).

(4) The pullback of an oriented orthonormal frame is one for \(f^*E\), with connection form \(f^*\theta \) and curvature \(f^*F\).

(5) Let \(s\) be a local frame of \(L\) with \(h(s, s) = 1\). Then \((e_1, e_2) = (s, is)\) is an oriented orthonormal frame of \(E\) for \(\operatorname {Re}h\). The connection form of \(\nabla \) on \(L\) in the frame \(s\) is \(i\omega \) for a real \(1\)-form \(\omega \) (it is skew-Hermitian), so \(\nabla e_1 = i\omega \otimes s = \omega \otimes e_2\) and \(\nabla e_2 = i\nabla s = -\omega \otimes e_1\). Hence \(\theta _{21} = \omega \), \(\theta _{12} = -\omega \), and \(F_{12} = d\theta _{12} = -d\omega \) (for \(n = 2\) the term \(\theta \wedge \theta \) has zero off-diagonal entries). The curvature of \(L\) is \(d(i\omega ) = i\, d\omega \), so \(c_1(L, \nabla ) = \frac{i}{2\pi }\, i\, d\omega = -\frac{1}{2\pi }d\omega = \frac{1}{2\pi }F_{12} = e(\nabla )\).

By (5) and Example 38.2.6(2), for the round sphere \(S^2\) the Euler form of the projection connection on \(TS^2\) is \(\frac{1}{2\pi }dA\), with integral \(2 = \chi (S^2)\). The rest of the section shows that this is no accident.

Next we define the index of an isolated zero of a section, which generalises the winding number of a vector field in the plane around a zero.

Write \(\sigma \) for the \((n-1)\)-form

\[ \sigma = \sum _{i=1}^n(-1)^{i-1}y^i\, dy^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \widehat{dy^i} \wedge \cdots \wedge dy^n \]

on \(\mathbb {R}^n\), and also for its restriction to the unit sphere \(S^{n-1}\), oriented as the boundary of the unit ball. At \(y\) it is the interior product of \(dy^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge dy^n\) with the position vector \(y\): \(\sigma _y(v_2, \ldots , v_n) = \det (y, v_2, \ldots , v_n)\). Hence \(\sigma \) restricts to the area form of \(S^{n-1}\), and for a linear map \(h \in \mathrm{O}(n)\), which maps \(S^{n-1}\) to itself, \(h^*\sigma = \det (h)\, \sigma \), since \(\det (hy, hv_2, \ldots , hv_n) = \det (h)\det (y, v_2, \ldots , v_n)\). Write \(|S^{n-1}| = \int _{S^{n-1}}\sigma \) for the area of the unit sphere.

02N8 Lemma 38.6.4

\(|S^{n-1}| = n\, |B^n|\), where \(|B^n|\) is the volume of the unit ball, and \(|B^{2m}| = \pi ^m/m!\). Hence \(|S^{2m-1}| = 2\pi ^m/(m-1)!\); for instance \(|S^1| = 2\pi \) and \(|S^3| = 2\pi ^2\).

Proof â–¼

On \(\mathbb {R}^n\), \(d\sigma = \sum _i(-1)^{i-1}dy^i \wedge dy^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \widehat{dy^i} \wedge \cdots \wedge dy^n = n\, dy^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge dy^n\), so Stokes’ theorem (Theorem 35.4.8) on the unit ball gives \(\int _{S^{n-1}}\sigma = n|B^n|\). For the volumes, slice \(B^{n+2} \subseteq \mathbb {R}^2 \times \mathbb {R}^n\) by the first two coordinates: for \(w \in \mathbb {R}^2\) with \(|w| {\lt} 1\) the slice is a ball of radius \((1 - |w|^2)^{1/2}\) in \(\mathbb {R}^n\), of volume \(|B^n|(1 - |w|^2)^{n/2}\) (a linear change of variables, Lemma 27.10.1). By Fubini’s theorem (Theorem 27.9.3) and polar coordinates in the plane (Example 27.10.3),

\[ |B^{n+2}| = |B^n|\int _0^{2\pi }\! \! \int _0^1(1 - r^2)^{n/2}r\, dr\, d\varphi = |B^n|\, \frac{2\pi }{n + 2} . \]

Starting from \(|B^2| = \pi \), induction gives \(|B^{2m}| = \pi ^m/m!\), and then \(|S^{2m-1}| = 2m\, \pi ^m/m! = 2\pi ^m/(m-1)!\).

Now let \(M\) be oriented of dimension \(n\), equal to the rank of \(E\), and let \(s\) be a section of \(E\) with an isolated zero \(z\). Choose a positively oriented chart \(\varphi \colon V \to \mathbb {R}^n\) with \(\varphi (z) = 0\) and a positively oriented frame \(e\) of \(E\) on \(V\), not necessarily orthonormal, and write \(s = \sum _is^ie_i\). For small \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\) let \(\bar B_\varepsilon = \varphi ^{-1}(\{ |u| \leq \varepsilon \} )\), let \(B_\varepsilon \) be its interior, assume \(z\) is the only zero of \(s\) in \(\bar B_\varepsilon \), and let \(S_\varepsilon = \partial \bar B_\varepsilon \), oriented as the boundary of \(\bar B_\varepsilon \). On \(V \setminus \{ z\} \) define \(f = (s^1, \ldots , s^n)/|(s^1, \ldots , s^n)|\), with values in \(S^{n-1}\).

02N9 Definition 38.6.5

The index of \(s\) at the isolated zero \(z\) is

\[ \operatorname {ind}_z(s) = \frac{1}{|S^{n-1}|}\int _{S_\varepsilon }f^*\sigma . \]

For \(E = TM\) this is the index of a vector field at an isolated zero.

02NA Lemma 38.6.6

  1. The index does not depend on \(\varepsilon \) nor on the positively oriented frame \(e\).

  2. If \(V \subseteq \mathbb {R}^n\), \(E = V \times \mathbb {R}^n\) with the standard frame and \(s(u) = hu\) near \(z = 0\) for some \(h \in \mathrm{O}(n)\), then \(\operatorname {ind}_0(s) = \det h\). In particular a source, \(s(u) = u\), has index \(1\), and a sink, \(s(u) = -u\), has index \((-1)^n\).

Proof â–¼

Two facts are used. First, \(f^*\sigma \) is closed on \(V \setminus \{ z\} \), being the pullback of a form of top degree on \(S^{n-1}\). Second, if \(f_t \colon S_\varepsilon \to S^{n-1}\), \(t \in [0, 1]\), is a smooth homotopy, then \(\int _{S_\varepsilon }f_0^*\sigma = \int _{S_\varepsilon }f_1^*\sigma \): by Theorem 36.2.4 the closed forms \(f_0^*\sigma \) and \(f_1^*\sigma \) differ by an exact form, whose integral over the compact manifold \(S_\varepsilon \) without boundary vanishes by Stokes’ theorem.

(1) For \(\varepsilon ^{\prime } {\lt} \varepsilon \), the region \(\varepsilon ^{\prime } \leq |\varphi | \leq \varepsilon \) is a compact manifold with boundary \(S_\varepsilon \) and \(S_{\varepsilon ^{\prime }}\) with the opposite orientation, so Stokes’ theorem and \(d(f^*\sigma ) = 0\) give the same integral over both spheres. If \(e^{\prime } = eg\) is another positively oriented frame, with \(g\) taking values in invertible matrices of positive determinant, then the components are related by \((s^i) = g\, (s^{\prime i})\). Suppose first that both frames are orthonormal, so that \(g\) takes values in \(\mathrm{SO}(n)\). Then \(f^{\prime } = g^Tf\), and \(f_t(q) = g(\varphi ^{-1}(t\varphi (q)))^Tf(q)\), \(t \in [0, 1]\), is a smooth homotopy on \(S_\varepsilon \) from \(f_0 = g(z)^T \circ f\) to \(f_1 = f^{\prime }\). Since \(g(z)^T \in \mathrm{SO}(n)\), \(f_0^*\sigma = f^*(g(z)^{T*}\sigma ) = f^*\sigma \), and the claim follows. In general, apply the Gram–Schmidt process to \(e\): it gives an oriented orthonormal frame \(e^{\prime } = eg\) with \(g\) upper triangular with positive diagonal entries, so \((s^i) = g\, (s^{\prime i})\). The matrices \(g_t = (1 - t)I + tg\) are again upper triangular with positive diagonal, hence invertible, and \(f_t = g_t(s^{\prime i})/|g_t(s^{\prime i})|\) is a homotopy from the map \(f^{\prime }\) computed in \(e^{\prime }\) to the map \(f\) computed in \(e\).

(2) Here \(f(u) = hu/|u|\), and on \(S_\varepsilon \) this is \(h\) composed with the dilation \(u \mapsto u/\varepsilon \) onto \(S^{n-1}\), which preserves orientations. So \(\int _{S_\varepsilon }f^*\sigma = \int _{S^{n-1}}h^*\sigma = \det (h)|S^{n-1}|\). The sink is \(h = -I\), of determinant \((-1)^n\).

The index is the degree of \(f \colon S_\varepsilon \to S^{n-1}\) (Exercise 36.5.3), so it is an integer, and it does not depend on the chart either [Mil97, Chapter 6]; neither fact is needed below, where any chart may be used at each zero. For a vector field in the plane, \(f\) is the direction of the field along a small circle, and the index is the number of turns it makes: for \(X(z) = z^k\) (in complex notation) it is \(k\), and for \(X(z) = \bar z^k\) it is \(-k\) (Exercise 38.8.9).

We now come to Chern’s construction, which takes place on the unit sphere bundle of \(E\).

The unit sphere bundle of \(E\) is \(SE = \{ v \in E : |v| = 1\} \), with projection \(\pi \colon SE \to M\). Over an open set \(U\) with an oriented orthonormal frame \(e\), the map \(U \times S^{n-1} \to SE|_U\), \((x, y) \mapsto \sum _iy^ie_i(x)\), is a bijection; for another such frame \(e^{\prime } = eg\) the transition map is \((x, y) \mapsto (x, g(x)^Ty)\), a diffeomorphism. So \(SE\) is a manifold of dimension \(\dim M + n - 1\) (a closed submanifold of \(E\)), \(\pi \) is a submersion, and the fibre \(S_xE\) is the unit sphere of \(E_x\). On \(SE|_U\) we have the functions \(y^1, \ldots , y^n\), with \(\sum _i(y^i)^2 = 1\), and we define the \(1\)-forms

\[ \eta ^i = dy^i + \sum _j\theta _{ij}\, y^j, \qquad i = 1, \ldots , n, \]

where, here and below, forms on \(M\) are pulled back to \(SE\) by \(\pi \) without changing the notation. The meaning of \(\eta \) is the following. On \(SE\) the bundle \(\pi ^*E\) has the tautological section \(v \mapsto v\), which is \(\sum _iy^i\, e_i\) in the frame \(\pi ^*e\), and \(\eta ^i\) are the components of its covariant derivative for the pullback connection (Proposition 37.3.4(2)): \(\eta \) measures how the point \(v\) of the sphere bundle moves compared with parallel transport.

02NB Lemma 38.6.7

  1. \(\sum _iy^i\eta ^i = 0\); in particular \(\eta ^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \eta ^n = 0\).

  2. For another oriented orthonormal frame \(e^{\prime } = eg\), \(y^{\prime } = g^Ty\) and \(\eta ^{\prime } = g^T\eta \) (as columns, \(g\) being composed with \(\pi \)).

  3. \(d\eta ^i = \sum _jF_{ij}\, y^j - \sum _j\theta _{ij} \wedge \eta ^j\).

  4. On a fibre \(S_xE\), identified with \(S^{n-1}\) by the frame at \(x\), \(\eta ^i\) restricts to \(dy^i\), and \(F\) and \(\theta \) restrict to \(0\).

Proof â–¼

(1) \(\sum _iy^i\eta ^i = \frac12d\bigl(\sum _i(y^i)^2\bigr) + \sum _{i,j}\theta _{ij}y^iy^j = 0\), the first term because \(\sum _i(y^i)^2 = 1\) and the second because \(\theta \) is skew-symmetric. At each point \(y \neq 0\), so the \(\eta ^i\) are linearly dependent and their wedge product vanishes (Proposition 11.3.3(4)). (2) From \(\sum _iy^ie_i = \sum _jy^{\prime j}e^{\prime }_j = \sum _{i,j}y^{\prime j}g_{ij}e_i\) we get \(y = gy^{\prime }\), so \(y^{\prime } = g^{-1}y = g^Ty\). Then, in vector notation, \(\eta ^{\prime } = dy^{\prime } + \theta ^{\prime }y^{\prime } = d(g^{-1})y + g^{-1}dy + (g^{-1}\theta g + g^{-1}dg)g^{-1}y = g^{-1}(dy + \theta y)\), because \(d(g^{-1}) = -g^{-1}dg\, g^{-1}\). (3) \(d\eta ^i = \sum _jd\theta _{ij}\, y^j - \sum _j\theta _{ij} \wedge dy^j\). Substitute \(d\theta _{ij} = F_{ij} - \sum _k\theta _{ik} \wedge \theta _{kj}\) and \(dy^j = \eta ^j - \sum _k\theta _{jk}y^k\): the two terms \(\mp \sum _{j,k}\theta _{ik} \wedge \theta _{kj}\, y^j\) cancel, leaving the formula. (4) The composite of the inclusion of \(S_xE\) with \(\pi \) is constant, so pulled back forms from \(M\) of positive degree restrict to zero.

Now we build forms on \(SE\) out of \(y\), \(\eta \) and \(F\), contracting all indices with the sign symbol \(\varepsilon _{i_1 \ldots i_n}\), which is the sign of the permutation \((i_1, \ldots , i_n)\) of \((1, \ldots , n)\), and \(0\) if two indices are equal. Let \(n = 2m\), and for \(0 \leq k \leq m - 1\) put

\[ \Phi _k = \sum \varepsilon _{i_1 \ldots i_n}\, y^{i_1}\, \eta ^{i_2} \wedge \cdots \wedge \eta ^{i_{n-2k}} \wedge F_{i_{n-2k+1}i_{n-2k+2}} \wedge \cdots \wedge F_{i_{n-1}i_n}, \]

with \(n - 2k - 1\) factors \(\eta \) and \(k\) factors \(F\), a form of degree \(n - 1\); and for \(-1 \leq k \leq m - 1\) put

\[ \Psi _k = \sum \varepsilon _{i_1 \ldots i_n}\, \eta ^{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge \eta ^{i_{n-2k-2}} \wedge F_{i_{n-2k-1}i_{n-2k}} \wedge \cdots \wedge F_{i_{n-1}i_n}, \]

with \(n - 2k - 2\) factors \(\eta \) and \(k + 1\) factors \(F\), a form of degree \(n\). All sums run over all \((i_1, \ldots , i_n)\).

02NC Lemma 38.6.8

  1. The forms \(\Phi _k\) and \(\Psi _k\) do not depend on the oriented orthonormal frame, so they are global forms on \(SE\).

  2. \(\Psi _{-1} = 0\) and \(\Psi _{m-1} = 2^mm!\, \pi ^*\mathrm{Pf}(F) = (4\pi )^mm!\, \pi ^*e(\nabla )\).

  3. On a fibre \(S_xE \cong S^{n-1}\), \(\Phi _k\) restricts to \(0\) for \(k \geq 1\), and \(\Phi _0\) restricts to \((n-1)!\, \sigma \).

Proof â–¼

(1) By Lemma 38.6.7(2), in the frame \(eg\) we have \(y^{\prime i} = \sum _ag_{ai}y^a\), \(\eta ^{\prime i} = \sum _ag_{ai}\eta ^a\) and \(F^{\prime }_{ij} = \sum _{a,b}g_{ai}g_{bj}F_{ab}\). Substituting, every index \(i_l\) in \(\Phi ^{\prime }_k\) becomes a sum over an index \(a_l\) with a factor \(g_{a_li_l}\), and the order of the factors is unchanged; so \(\Phi ^{\prime }_k\) is the same expression as \(\Phi _k\) with \(\varepsilon _{a_1 \ldots a_n}\) replaced by \(\sum _{i_1, \ldots , i_n}\varepsilon _{i_1 \ldots i_n}g_{a_1i_1} \cdots g_{a_ni_n}\). By the Leibniz formula this is the determinant of the matrix whose rows are the rows \(a_1, \ldots , a_n\) of \(g\), that is \(\varepsilon _{a_1 \ldots a_n}\det g = \varepsilon _{a_1 \ldots a_n}\). The same argument applies to \(\Psi _k\). (2) \(\Psi _{-1} = \sum \varepsilon _{i_1 \ldots i_n}\eta ^{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge \eta ^{i_n} = n!\, \eta ^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \eta ^n = 0\) by Lemma 38.6.7(1). And \(\Psi _{m-1} = \sum _\sigma \operatorname {sgn}(\sigma )F_{\sigma (1)\sigma (2)} \wedge \cdots \wedge F_{\sigma (n-1)\sigma (n)} = 2^mm!\, \mathrm{Pf}(F)\). (3) By Lemma 38.6.7(4) the factors \(F\) restrict to \(0\) and \(\eta ^i\) to \(dy^i\). So \(\Phi _0\) restricts to \(\sum \varepsilon _{i_1 \ldots i_n}y^{i_1}dy^{i_2} \wedge \cdots \wedge dy^{i_n}\). For fixed \(i_1 = i\), the \((n-1)!\) orderings of the remaining indices all give \(\varepsilon _{i,1, \ldots , \hat{\imath }, \ldots , n}\, dy^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \widehat{dy^i} \wedge \cdots \wedge dy^n\), and \(\varepsilon _{i,1, \ldots , \hat{\imath }, \ldots , n} = (-1)^{i-1}\); the result is \((n-1)!\, \sigma \).

02ND Lemma 38.6.9 Chern

For \(0 \leq k \leq m - 1\),

\[ d\Phi _k = \Psi _{k-1} - \frac{2m - 2k - 1}{2k + 2}\, \Psi _k . \]
Proof â–¼

Both sides are global forms on \(SE\), so it suffices to compare them at an arbitrary point \(v \in SE\), in a frame adapted to \(v\). Let \(x = \pi (v)\) and choose, by Lemma 38.6.1, an oriented orthonormal frame near \(x\) with \(\theta (x) = 0\) and \(e_n(x) = v\). Then at the point \(v\):

  • \(y = (0, \ldots , 0, 1)\), and the pulled back forms \(\theta _{ij}\) vanish (their value at \(v\) is \(\theta _{ij}(x) \circ d\pi = 0\));

  • hence \(\eta ^i = dy^i\), and \(\eta ^n = dy^n = 0\), because \(\sum _iy^idy^i = \frac12d\sum _i(y^i)^2 = 0\) and \(y = (0, \ldots , 0, 1)\);

  • \(d\eta ^i = \sum _jF_{ij}y^j = F_{in}\) by Lemma 38.6.7(3), and \(dF = F \wedge \theta - \theta \wedge F = 0\).

Write \(r = n - 2k\) and \(G = F_{i_{r+1}i_{r+2}} \wedge \cdots \wedge F_{i_{n-1}i_n}\) for the product of the \(k\) factors \(F\) in \(\Phi _k\). By the Leibniz rule,

\[ d\bigl(y^{i_1}\eta ^{i_2} \wedge \cdots \wedge \eta ^{i_r} \wedge G\bigr) = dy^{i_1} \wedge \eta ^{i_2} \wedge \cdots \wedge \eta ^{i_r} \wedge G + y^{i_1}\sum _{l=2}^r(-1)^{l}\, \eta ^{i_2} \wedge \cdots \wedge d\eta ^{i_l} \wedge \cdots \wedge \eta ^{i_r} \wedge G \pm y^{i_1}\eta ^{i_2} \wedge \cdots \wedge \eta ^{i_r} \wedge dG, \]

since \(d\eta ^{i_l}\) is preceded by \(l - 2\) factors of degree \(1\). We evaluate the three resulting sums at \(v\).

The terms with \(dG\) vanish at \(v\), since \(dF = 0\) there.

The terms with \(dy^{i_1}\). At \(v\), \(dy^{i_1} = \eta ^{i_1}\), so their sum is \(\sum \varepsilon _{i_1 \ldots i_n}\eta ^{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge \eta ^{i_r} \wedge G\), which has \(n - 2k\) factors \(\eta \) and \(k\) factors \(F\): it is \(\Psi _{k-1}\) at \(v\).

The terms with \(d\eta ^{i_l}\). At \(v\), \(y^{i_1} = \delta _{i_1n}\) and \(d\eta ^{i_l} = F_{i_ln}\). Moving the \(2\)-form \(F_{i_ln}\) to the right of the remaining factors \(\eta \) costs no sign, so these terms add up to

\[ \sum _{l=2}^r(-1)^l\sum \varepsilon _{n\, i_2 \ldots i_n}\, \eta ^{i_2} \wedge \cdots \widehat{\eta ^{i_l}} \cdots \wedge \eta ^{i_r} \wedge F_{i_ln} \wedge G . \]

In the \(l\)-th term rename the indices so that \(i_l\) comes last among \(i_2, \ldots , i_r\); this moves it past \(r - l\) indices and multiplies \(\varepsilon \) by \((-1)^{r-l}\). Since \(r\) is even, every \(l\) contributes \((-1)^l(-1)^{r-l} = 1\) times the same sum, so the terms with \(d\eta \) give \((r-1)\, T\) with

\[ T = \sum \varepsilon _{n\, j_1 \ldots j_{r-2}\, a\, j_{r+1} \ldots j_n}\, \eta ^{j_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge \eta ^{j_{r-2}} \wedge F_{an} \wedge F_{j_{r+1}j_{r+2}} \wedge \cdots \wedge F_{j_{n-1}j_n} . \]

Now look at \(\Psi _k\) at \(v\). Its factors \(\eta \) carry the indices in the positions \(1, \ldots , r - 2\), and since \(\eta ^n = 0\) at \(v\), the index \(n\) must occur in one of the \(k + 1\) factors \(F\). Exchanging two pairs of positions among the factors \(F\) is an even permutation of the indices and does not change the product of the commuting \(2\)-forms; exchanging the two positions of one pair changes the sign of \(\varepsilon \) and of that factor \(F\). So the terms in which \(n\) occupies any of the \(2(k+1)\) positions of the factors \(F\) add up to the same as those in which it occupies position \(r\), and

\[ \Psi _k = 2(k+1)\sum \varepsilon _{j_1 \ldots j_{r-2}\, a\, n\, j_{r+1} \ldots j_n}\, \eta ^{j_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge \eta ^{j_{r-2}} \wedge F_{an} \wedge F_{j_{r+1}j_{r+2}} \wedge \cdots \wedge F_{j_{n-1}j_n} \quad \text{at } v. \]

Moving \(n\) from the first position to position \(r\) passes \(r - 1\) indices, an odd number, so \(\varepsilon _{n\, j_1 \ldots j_{r-2}\, a \ldots } = -\varepsilon _{j_1 \ldots j_{r-2}\, a\, n \ldots }\) and \(T = -\Psi _k/(2(k+1))\) at \(v\). Altogether, at \(v\) we get \(d\Phi _k = \Psi _{k-1} - \frac{r - 1}{2(k+1)}\Psi _k\), and \(r - 1 = 2m - 2k - 1\).

Write \((2m-1)!! = 1 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdots (2m-1)\).

02NE Proposition 38.6.10 Chern

Let \(n = 2m\), \(c_k = \frac{2m-2k-1}{2k+2}\), \(a_0 = 1\) and \(a_k = c_0c_1 \cdots c_{k-1}\), and put

\[ \Pi = -\frac{1}{(2\pi )^m(2m-1)!!}\sum _{k=0}^{m-1}a_k\Phi _k \in \Omega ^{n-1}(SE) . \]

Then \(d\Pi = \pi ^*e(\nabla )\), and the restriction of \(\Pi \) to every fibre \(S_xE \cong S^{n-1}\) is \(-\sigma /|S^{n-1}|\); in particular \(\int _{S_xE}\Pi = -1\).

Proof â–¼

By Lemma 38.6.9,

\[ d\sum _{k=0}^{m-1}a_k\Phi _k = \sum _{k=0}^{m-1}a_k\Psi _{k-1} - \sum _{k=0}^{m-1}a_kc_k\Psi _k = \sum _{k=0}^{m-2}(a_{k+1} - a_kc_k)\Psi _k - a_{m-1}c_{m-1}\Psi _{m-1} = -a_m\Psi _{m-1}, \]

using \(\Psi _{-1} = 0\) and \(a_{k+1} = a_kc_k\). Here \(a_m = \prod _{k=0}^{m-1}\frac{2m-2k-1}{2k+2} = \frac{(2m-1)(2m-3) \cdots 1}{2^mm!} = \frac{(2m-1)!!}{2^mm!}\), and \(\Psi _{m-1} = 2^mm!(2\pi )^m\pi ^*e(\nabla )\) by Lemma 38.6.8(2). So \(d\sum _ka_k\Phi _k = -(2m-1)!!(2\pi )^m\pi ^*e(\nabla )\), which is \(d\Pi = \pi ^*e(\nabla )\). On a fibre only \(\Phi _0\) survives, with \(a_0 = 1\), by Lemma 38.6.8(3), so \(\Pi \) restricts to \(-\frac{(2m-1)!}{(2\pi )^m(2m-1)!!}\sigma \). Now \((2m-1)!/(2m-1)!! = 2 \cdot 4 \cdots (2m-2) = 2^{m-1}(m-1)!\), and by Lemma 38.6.4, \(2^{m-1}(m-1)!/(2\pi )^m = (m-1)!/(2\pi ^m) = 1/|S^{2m-1}|\).

So the pullback of the Euler form to the sphere bundle is exact, although the Euler form itself is in general not exact on \(M\); a form \(\Pi \) with these two properties is called a global angular form [BT82, Section 11], because on each fibre it is (minus) the normalised angular form of the sphere. As a consequence, \(\pi ^*d\, e(\nabla ) = d\, d\Pi = 0\), and \(\pi ^*\) is injective on forms because \(\pi \) is a submersion; this gives a second proof that \(e(\nabla )\) is closed.

02NF Example 38.6.11

Let \(n = 2\), so \(m = 1\), \(\Pi = -\frac{1}{2\pi }\Phi _0\) and \(\Phi _0 = y^1\eta ^2 - y^2\eta ^1\). Over a set \(U\) with an oriented orthonormal frame, a point of \(SE|_U\) is \(v = \cos \phi \, e_1 + \sin \phi \, e_2\), where the angle \(\phi \) is a local function on \(SE|_U\) defined up to adding constant multiples of \(2\pi \), so \(d\phi \) is well defined on \(SE|_U\). Then \(y = (\cos \phi , \sin \phi )\), and with \(\theta _{21} = -\theta _{12}\),

\[ \eta ^1 = -\sin \phi \, d\phi + \theta _{12}\sin \phi , \qquad \eta ^2 = \cos \phi \, d\phi + \theta _{21}\cos \phi , \qquad \Phi _0 = d\phi + \theta _{21} . \]

So \(\Pi = -\frac{1}{2\pi }(d\phi + \theta _{21})\) and \(d\Pi = -\frac{1}{2\pi }d\theta _{21} = \frac{1}{2\pi }F_{12} = \pi ^*e(\nabla )\), as \(F_{12} = d\theta _{12}\) for \(n = 2\). The form \(d\phi + \theta _{21}\) measures the turning of the unit vector \(v\) relative to parallel transport: along a curve, \(v\) is parallel exactly when \(\dot\phi = -\theta _{21}(\dot\gamma )\), since \(\nabla e_1 = \theta _{21} \otimes e_2\).

The proof of Theorem 38.6.12 below becomes, in this case, the following classical argument. Let \(s\) be a section with isolated zeros, and on \(M \setminus \{ s = 0\} \) use the frame \(e_1 = s/|s|\), \(e_2\) its rotation by \(+\pi /2\). Then \(s/|s|\) has angle \(\phi = 0\) in this frame, so \(\int _{M \setminus \cup B_\varepsilon }e(\nabla ) = \frac{1}{2\pi }\sum _z\int _{S_\varepsilon (z)}\theta _{21}\), by Stokes’ theorem and \(\frac{1}{2\pi }F_{12} = -\frac{1}{2\pi }d\theta _{21}\). Near a zero \(z\) compare with a frame \((e^{\prime }_1, e^{\prime }_2)\) defined on all of \(B_\varepsilon (z)\): if \(e_1 = \cos \psi \, e^{\prime }_1 + \sin \psi \, e^{\prime }_2\), the connection forms are related by \(\theta _{21} = \theta ^{\prime }_{21} + d\psi \) (Exercise 38.8.11). The integral of \(d\psi \) over \(S_\varepsilon (z)\) is \(2\pi \operatorname {ind}_z(s)\), since \(\psi \) is the angle of \(s\) in the frame \(e^{\prime }\), and the integral of the smooth form \(\theta ^{\prime }_{21}\) tends to \(0\) with \(\varepsilon \). So \(\int _Me(\nabla ) = \sum _z\operatorname {ind}_z(s)\).

02NG Theorem 38.6.12 Chern

Let \(M\) be a compact oriented manifold of even dimension \(n = 2m\), \(E \to M\) an oriented real vector bundle of rank \(n\) with a metric and a metric connection \(\nabla \), and \(s\) a section of \(E\) whose zeros are isolated. Then \(s\) has finitely many zeros, and

\[ \int _Me(\nabla ) = \sum _{s(z) = 0}\operatorname {ind}_z(s) . \]
Proof â–¼

The zero set \(Z\) of \(s\) is closed in the compact space \(M\) and discrete, hence finite. For each \(z \in Z\) choose a positively oriented chart \(\varphi _z\) centred at \(z\) and an oriented orthonormal frame \(e\) of \(E\) on its domain, and for small \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\) let \(\bar B_\varepsilon (z)\) and \(S_\varepsilon (z)\) be as in Definition 38.6.5, with the balls pairwise disjoint. The section \(\hat s = s/|s|\) of \(SE\) over \(M \setminus Z\) is a smooth map \(\hat s \colon M \setminus Z \to SE\) with \(\pi \circ \hat s = \mathrm{id}\), so \(\hat s^*\pi ^*e(\nabla ) = e(\nabla )\). Let \(M_\varepsilon = M \setminus \bigcup _zB_\varepsilon (z)\), a compact manifold with boundary the union of the spheres \(S_\varepsilon (z)\), each with the orientation opposite to its orientation as the boundary of \(\bar B_\varepsilon (z)\). By Proposition 38.6.10 and Stokes’ theorem,

\[ \int _{M_\varepsilon }e(\nabla ) = \int _{M_\varepsilon }\hat s^*d\Pi = \int _{M_\varepsilon }d(\hat s^*\Pi ) = -\sum _{z \in Z}\int _{S_\varepsilon (z)}\hat s^*\Pi . \]

As \(\varepsilon \to 0\) the left side tends to \(\int _Me(\nabla )\), since \(e(\nabla )\) is a continuous form and the volumes of the balls in the charts tend to \(0\). It remains to show that \(\int _{S_\varepsilon (z)}\hat s^*\Pi \to -\operatorname {ind}_z(s)\) for each zero \(z\).

Fix \(z\), write \(S_\varepsilon = S_\varepsilon (z)\), and use the frame \(e\) to identify \(SE\) over the chart domain with the product of the domain and \(S^{n-1}\). In this identification \(\hat s(q) = (q, f(q))\), with \(f\) as in Definition 38.6.5. Define

\[ H \colon S_\varepsilon \times [0, 1] \to SE, \qquad H(q, t) = \bigl(\varphi _z^{-1}(t\varphi _z(q)),\, f(q)\bigr), \]

so that \(H(q, 1) = \hat s(q)\) and \(H(q, 0) = \iota _z(f(q))\), where \(\iota _z \colon S^{n-1} \to S_zE\) is the identification of the fibre given by the frame at \(z\). By Stokes’ theorem on the compact manifold \(S_\varepsilon \times [0, 1]\), whose boundary is \(S_\varepsilon \times \{ 1\} \) and \(S_\varepsilon \times \{ 0\} \) with opposite orientations,

\[ \int _{S_\varepsilon }\hat s^*\Pi - \int _{S_\varepsilon }f^*\iota _z^*\Pi = \pm \int _{S_\varepsilon \times [0, 1]}H^*d\Pi = \pm \int _{S_\varepsilon \times [0, 1]}(\pi \circ H)^*e(\nabla ) . \]

The map \(\pi \circ H(q, t) = \varphi _z^{-1}(t\varphi _z(q))\) restricts to a diffeomorphism from \(S_\varepsilon \times (0, 1]\) onto \(\bar B_\varepsilon (z) \setminus \{ z\} \) (in the chart it is \((u, t) \mapsto tu\), with inverse \(w \mapsto (\varepsilon w/|w|, |w|/\varepsilon )\)), and \(S_\varepsilon \times \{ 0\} \) has measure zero; so the right side is \(\pm \int _{B_\varepsilon (z)}e(\nabla )\), which tends to \(0\) with \(\varepsilon \). On the other hand, by Proposition 38.6.10, \(\iota _z^*\Pi = -\sigma /|S^{n-1}|\), so \(\int _{S_\varepsilon }f^*\iota _z^*\Pi = -\frac{1}{|S^{n-1}|}\int _{S_\varepsilon }f^*\sigma = -\operatorname {ind}_z(s)\) by Lemma 38.6.6(1). Hence \(\int _{S_\varepsilon }\hat s^*\Pi \to -\operatorname {ind}_z(s)\), and \(\int _Me(\nabla ) = \sum _z\operatorname {ind}_z(s)\).

The theorem has a striking structure: the left side does not depend on the section, and the right side does not depend on the metric or the connection. So both are invariants of the oriented bundle \(E\) alone; the left side shows it is the integral of a characteristic form, the right side that it is an integer. A section without zeros forces it to vanish, in accordance with Proposition 38.4.7(3). The de Rham class of \(e(\nabla )\) is the Euler class of Definition 38.4.6 [MS74, Appendix C]; we do not need this.

02NH Corollary 38.6.13 Gauss–Bonnet–Chern

Let \(M\) be a compact oriented manifold of dimension \(2m\) with a Riemannian metric, and \(\nabla \) a metric connection on \(TM\), for instance the Levi-Civita connection.

  1. For every vector field \(X\) on \(M\) with isolated zeros, \(\int _Me(TM, \nabla ) = \sum _z\operatorname {ind}_z(X)\).

  2. (Hopf) The sum of the indices of a vector field with isolated zeros is the same for all such vector fields.

  3. \(\int _Me(TM, \nabla ) = \chi (M)\). In particular the integral does not depend on the metric, and if \(M\) admits a nowhere vanishing vector field, then \(\chi (M) = 0\).

Proof â–¼

(1) is Theorem 38.6.12 for \(E = TM\), and (2) follows because the left side of (1) does not involve \(X\). For (3), the sum of the indices equals \(\chi (M)\) by the Poincaré–Hopf theorem, Theorem 38.5.1(3), applied to any vector field with isolated zeros (for instance the gradient of a Morse function [Mil63]). A nowhere vanishing vector field has no zeros, so the sum of its indices is \(0\).

02PM Example 38.6.14

Let \(M\) be a parallelizable manifold (Definition 34.5.1), with a global frame \((X_1, \ldots , X_n)\). The connection on \(TM\) for which the \(X_i\) are parallel, with connection form \(0\) in this frame, is flat, so all the Chern–Weil forms of \(TM \otimes \mathbb {C}\) vanish, and so do the Pontryagin classes of \(TM\) in de Rham cohomology. Choosing the metric for which the frame is orthonormal, this connection is also metric (Proposition 37.6.2), so its Euler form is \(0\); if \(M\) is compact, oriented and of even dimension, Corollary 38.6.13 gives \(\chi (M) = 0\). This also follows directly from Corollary 38.6.13(3), since \(X_1\) vanishes nowhere. So \(S^{2m}\), \(\mathbb {C}\mathbb {P}^n\) (with \(\chi = n + 1\)) and the compact orientable surfaces of genus \(g \neq 1\) (with \(\chi = 2 - 2g\)) are not parallelizable. The converse fails: the spheres are stably parallelizable (Example 34.3.9), so by the Whitney formula \(c(E \oplus \mathbb {C}) = c(E)\) of Theorem 38.3.1 their Pontryagin classes vanish, and the odd-dimensional ones have \(\chi = 0\); yet \(S^5\) is not parallelizable (Theorem 34.5.3). Characteristic classes detect the nontriviality of \(TS^{2m}\), through the Euler class, but not that of \(TS^5\).

For surfaces, \(e(TM, \nabla ) = \frac{1}{2\pi }F_{12}\), and for the Levi-Civita connection \(F_{12} = K\, dA\) with \(K\) the Gaussian curvature: this is the Gauss–Bonnet theorem \(\int _MK\, dA = 2\pi \chi (M)\), proved again in Chapter 39 together with its higher-dimensional Riemannian form. There the curvature of the round sphere \(S^{2m}\) is computed, and Corollary 38.6.13 gives a formula for its volume.
