27.8 Differentiation of integrals02HG

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

For a continuous function \(f\) on \(\mathbb {R}\), the fundamental theorem of calculus says that \(\frac{1}{2r}\int _{x-r}^{x+r}f \to f(x)\). We show that the averages of an integrable function over small balls converge to the function almost everywhere. Let \(B(x, r)\) be the open Euclidean ball. The map \(y \mapsto x + ry\) multiplies the volume of boxes by \(r^n\), hence Lebesgue measure by \(r^n\), so \(\lambda (B(x, r)) = r^n\lambda (B(0, 1))\). A measurable function is locally integrable, \(f \in L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb {R}^n)\), if \(\int _K|f| {\lt} \infty \) for every compact \(K\).

02HH Lemma 27.8.1 Vitali covering lemma

Every finite family of open balls \(B_1, \ldots , B_m\) in \(\mathbb {R}^n\) has a pairwise disjoint subfamily \(B_{i_1}, \ldots , B_{i_k}\) with \(\bigcup _iB_i \subseteq \bigcup _j3B_{i_j}\), where \(3B(x, r) = B(x, 3r)\). Consequently \(\lambda (\bigcup _iB_i) \leq 3^n\sum _j\lambda (B_{i_j})\).

Proof â–¼

Choose a ball of largest radius, discard all the balls that meet it, choose a ball of largest radius among the remaining ones, and so on. The chosen balls are pairwise disjoint. A discarded ball \(B = B(c, r)\) meets a chosen ball \(B' = B(c', r')\) with \(r' \geq r\), namely the one chosen at the step where \(B\) was discarded. If \(z \in B \cap B'\) and \(y \in B\), then \(\| y - c'\| \leq \| y - c\| + \| c - z\| + \| z - c'\| {\lt} r + r + r' \leq 3r'\), so \(B \subseteq 3B'\). The inequality follows from \(\lambda (3B') = 3^n\lambda (B')\) and subadditivity.

02HI Definition 27.8.2

For \(f \in L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb {R}^n)\), \(x \in \mathbb {R}^n\) and \(r {\gt} 0\), the average of \(f\) over \(B(x, r)\) is \(A_rf(x) = \frac{1}{\lambda (B(x, r))}\int _{B(x, r)}f\, d\lambda \), and the Hardy–Littlewood maximal function is \(Mf(x) = \sup _{r {\gt} 0}A_r|f|(x) \in [0, \infty ]\).

02HJ Theorem 27.8.3 Hardy–Littlewood maximal inequality

Let \(f \in L^1(\mathbb {R}^n)\) and \(t {\gt} 0\). The set \(\{ Mf {\gt} t\} \) is open, and \(\lambda (\{ Mf {\gt} t\} ) \leq \frac{3^n}{t}\| f\| _1\).

Proof â–¼

If \(A_r|f|(x) {\gt} t\), choose \(s {\gt} r\) with \((r/s)^nA_r|f|(x) {\gt} t\). For \(\| x' - x\| {\lt} s - r\) we have \(B(x', s) \supseteq B(x, r)\), so \(A_s|f|(x') \geq (r/s)^nA_r|f|(x) {\gt} t\); hence \(\{ Mf {\gt} t\} \) is open. Let \(K \subseteq \{ Mf {\gt} t\} \) be compact. Every \(x \in K\) is the centre of a ball \(B_x\) with \(\int _{B_x}|f| {\gt} t\lambda (B_x)\), and finitely many of these balls cover \(K\). By Lemma 27.8.1 they contain disjoint balls \(B_1, \ldots , B_k\) with

\[ \lambda (K) \leq 3^n\sum _j\lambda (B_j) {\lt} \frac{3^n}{t}\sum _j\int _{B_j}|f| \leq \frac{3^n}{t}\| f\| _1. \]

By inner regularity (Theorem 27.3.4), the same bound holds for \(\{ Mf {\gt} t\} \).

02HK Theorem 27.8.4 Lebesgue differentiation theorem

Let \(f \in L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb {R}^n)\). For almost every \(x \in \mathbb {R}^n\),

\[ \lim _{r \to 0}\frac{1}{\lambda (B(x, r))}\int _{B(x, r)}|f(y) - f(x)|\, d\lambda (y) = 0, \]

and in particular \(A_rf(x) \to f(x)\) as \(r \to 0\).

Proof â–¼

For \(x \in B(0, R)\) and \(r {\lt} 1\) the quantity only involves \(f\) on \(B(0, R + 1)\), so, replacing \(f\) by \(f\mathbf{1}_{B(0, R+1)}\) and letting \(R\) run through \(\mathbb {N}\), we may assume \(f \in L^1(\mathbb {R}^n)\). Let \(\Omega _f(x) = \limsup _{r \to 0}A_r|f - f(x)|(x)\). Fix \(t {\gt} 0\) and \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\), and choose \(g \in C_c(\mathbb {R}^n)\) with \(\| f - g\| _1 {\lt} \varepsilon \) (Proposition 27.5.4). For the continuous \(g\), \(A_r|g - g(x)|(x) \leq \sup _{B(x, r)}|g - g(x)| \to 0\), so \(\Omega _g = 0\). With \(h = f - g\) we have \(|f(y) - f(x)| \leq |h(y)| + |h(x)| + |g(y) - g(x)|\), hence \(\Omega _f(x) \leq Mh(x) + |h(x)|\), and

\[ \{ \Omega _f {\gt} 2t\} \subseteq \{ Mh {\gt} t\} \cup \{ |h| {\gt} t\} . \]

By Theorem 27.8.3 and Chebyshev’s inequality (Corollary 27.4.3(4)), the right side has measure at most \(3^n\varepsilon /t + \varepsilon /t\). As \(\varepsilon \) is arbitrary, \(\{ \Omega _f {\gt} 2t\} \) has outer measure \(0\), so it is a null set (Lebesgue measure is complete), and so is the union of these sets for \(t = 1/k\), \(k \geq 1\). Finally \(|A_rf(x) - f(x)| \leq A_r|f - f(x)|(x)\).

The points where the conclusion holds are the Lebesgue points of \(f\); changing \(f\) on a null set does not change the averages, so almost every point is a Lebesgue point of every representative.

02HL Corollary 27.8.5 Lebesgue density theorem

Let \(A \subseteq \mathbb {R}^n\) be Lebesgue measurable. Then \(\lambda (A \cap B(x, r))/\lambda (B(x, r)) \to 1\) as \(r \to 0\) for almost every \(x \in A\), and \(\to 0\) for almost every \(x \notin A\).

Proof â–¼

Apply Theorem 27.8.4 to the bounded, hence locally integrable, function \(\mathbf{1}_A\), whose average over \(B(x, r)\) is the ratio in question.

02HM Corollary 27.8.6 Fundamental theorem of calculus for Lebesgue integrals

Let \(f \in L^1([a, b])\) and \(F(x) = \int _{[a, x]}f\, d\lambda \). Then \(F\) is continuous, and at almost every \(x \in (a, b)\) it is differentiable with \(F'(x) = f(x)\).

Proof â–¼

Extend \(f\) by \(0\) to \(\mathbb {R}\). For \(y \to x\), \(|F(y) - F(x)| \leq \int \mathbf{1}_{[x, y]}|f| \to 0\) by dominated convergence, where \([x, y]\) means the interval between \(x\) and \(y\). For \(h \neq 0\) let \(I_h\) be the closed interval between \(x\) and \(x + h\), of length \(|h|\). Then \(F(x + h) - F(x) = \pm \int _{I_h}f\), with the sign of \(h\), so in both cases \(\frac{F(x + h) - F(x)}{h} - f(x) = \frac{1}{|h|}\int _{I_h}(f - f(x))\). As \(I_h \subseteq \overline{B}(x, |h|)\), an interval of length \(2|h|\) whose endpoints are null,

\[ \Bigl|\frac{F(x + h) - F(x)}{h} - f(x)\Bigr| \leq \frac{1}{|h|}\int _{B(x, |h|)}|f - f(x)| = 2A_{|h|}|f - f(x)|(x), \]

which tends to \(0\) at every Lebesgue point of \(f\).

02HN Counterexample 27.8.7

The Cantor function. A continuous nondecreasing function can have derivative \(0\) almost everywhere without being constant, so the formula \(F(b) - F(a) = \int _a^bF'\) fails for such functions. Let \(C \subseteq [0, 1]\) be the Cantor set, the set of numbers \(\sum _{k \geq 1}a_k3^{-k}\) with all \(a_k \in \{ 0, 2\} \); these expansions are unique, and \(C\) is compact of measure zero, being covered, for each \(k\), by \(2^k\) intervals of length \(3^{-k}\) (Exercise 27.13.1). Define \(c \colon C \to [0, 1]\) by \(c(\sum _ka_k3^{-k}) = \sum _k(a_k/2)2^{-k}\).

  1. \(c\) is nondecreasing and surjective. If \(x {\lt} y\) in \(C\), their digits first differ at some \(k\) with \(a_k = 0\) and \(b_k = 2\) (a difference \(2 \cdot 3^{-k}\) in position \(k\) cannot be compensated by the later digits, which contribute at most \(\sum _{j {\gt} k}2 \cdot 3^{-j} = 3^{-k}\)); then \(c(y) - c(x) \geq 2^{-k} - \sum _{j {\gt} k}2^{-j} = 0\). Every \(t \in [0, 1]\) has a binary expansion \(\sum _kb_k2^{-k}\), and \(t = c(\sum _k2b_k3^{-k})\).

  2. Let \(F(x) = \sup \{ c(y) : y \in C, y \leq x\} \) for \(x \in [0, 1]\). Then \(F\) is nondecreasing, \(F = c\) on \(C\), and \(F([0, 1]) = [0, 1]\). A nondecreasing function whose image is an interval has no jumps, so \(F\) is continuous.

  3. The complement \([0, 1] \setminus C\) is a countable union of open intervals \((\alpha , \beta )\) with \(\alpha \in C\), removed in the construction; on each of them \(F = c(\alpha )\) is constant, as \(C\) has no point in \((\alpha , \beta )\). So \(F' = 0\) on \([0, 1] \setminus C\), that is, almost everywhere, while \(F(1) - F(0) = 1\).

So \(F\) is not the integral of its derivative. The functions that are integrals of their derivatives are exactly the absolutely continuous ones [Rud87, Chapter 7]. Note also that the continuous map \(F\) sends the null set \(C\) onto \([0, 1]\).

02HO Counterexample 27.8.8

A Lebesgue measurable set that is not a Borel set. First, every \(A \subseteq \mathbb {R}\) with \(\lambda ^*(A) {\gt} 0\) contains a non-measurable subset. Let \(V\) be as in Counterexample 27.3.5. If \(S\) is a measurable subset of a translate \(V + q\), \(q \in \mathbb {Q}\), then the sets \(S - q + q'\), \(q' \in \mathbb {Q}\cap [-1, 1]\), are pairwise disjoint (they lie in the disjoint translates \(V + q'\)), contained in \([-1, 2]\), and all of measure \(\lambda (S)\); so \(\lambda (S) = 0\). The sets \(A \cap (V + q)\), \(q \in \mathbb {Q}\), cover \(A\), since every real number differs from an element of \(V\) by a rational; if they were all measurable, \(A\) would be a countable union of null sets.

Now let \(F\) be the Cantor function and \(h(x) = x + F(x)\) on \([0, 1]\). It is continuous and strictly increasing from \([0, 1]\) onto \([0, 2]\), hence a homeomorphism. It maps each interval \((\alpha , \beta )\) of \([0, 1] \setminus C\) onto an interval of the same length, since \(F\) is constant there; these images are disjoint, so \(\lambda (h([0, 1] \setminus C)) = 1\) and \(\lambda (h(C)) = 1\). Choose a non-measurable \(N \subseteq h(C)\). Then \(h^{-1}(N) \subseteq C\) is a subset of a null set, hence Lebesgue measurable. It is not a Borel set: otherwise \(N\), its preimage under the continuous map \(h^{-1}\), would be Borel, hence measurable. In particular, a composite of a Lebesgue measurable function with a continuous one need not be Lebesgue measurable: \(\mathbf{1}_{h^{-1}(N)} \circ h^{-1} = \mathbf{1}_N\).
