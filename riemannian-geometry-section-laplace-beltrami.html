40.14 The Laplace–Beltrami operator02WT

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

The Laplacian \(\Delta _g = \operatorname {div}\operatorname {grad}\) of Definition 40.5.1, called the Laplace–Beltrami operator when one wants to stress that it lives on a Riemannian manifold, has already appeared several times: in coordinates (Proposition 40.5.2(1)),

\[ \Delta _gf = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\det g}}\sum _{i,j}\partial _i\bigl(\sqrt{\det g}\, g^{ij}\partial _jf\bigr), \]

as the trace of the Hessian (Proposition 40.5.2(3)), and in the Jacobi operator of a minimal hypersurface (Remark 40.13.11). This section collects its basic properties: it is symmetric and nonpositive, it measures how \(f\) differs from its averages, it detects minimal submanifolds, and on spheres its eigenfunctions are the spherical harmonics. We use the analysts’ sign, so that \(\Delta _g = \sum _i\partial _i^2\) on \(\mathbb {R}^n\); the Hodge Laplacian of Section 40.15 below is \(-\Delta _g\) on functions. Throughout, \((M, g)\) is a Riemannian manifold of dimension \(n\) and we write \(\Delta \) for \(\Delta _g\).

02WU Proposition 40.14.1 Green’s identities

Let \(M\) be oriented and without boundary, and let \(f\), \(h\) be smooth functions, one of which has compact support. Then

\[ \int _Mf\, \Delta h\, \mathrm{vol}_g = -\int _M\langle \operatorname {grad}f, \operatorname {grad}h\rangle \, \mathrm{vol}_g = \int _Mh\, \Delta f\, \mathrm{vol}_g . \]

In particular \(\int _M\Delta f\, \mathrm{vol}_g = 0\) and \(\int _Mf\, \Delta f\, \mathrm{vol}_g = -\int _M|\operatorname {grad}f|^2\, \mathrm{vol}_g \leq 0\) for compactly supported \(f\): the operator \(\Delta \) is symmetric and nonpositive. Moreover \(\Delta \) is the only operator \(P \colon C^\infty (M) \to C^\infty (M)\) with \(\int _MfPh\, \mathrm{vol}_g = -\int _M\langle \operatorname {grad}f, \operatorname {grad}h\rangle \, \mathrm{vol}_g\) for all \(h\) and all compactly supported \(f\).

Proof â–¼

The vector field \(X = f\operatorname {grad}h\) has compact support and \(\operatorname {div}X = \langle \operatorname {grad}f, \operatorname {grad}h\rangle + f\Delta h\), by the Leibniz rule \(\nabla _v(fY) = v(f)Y + f\nabla _vY\) inside the trace. By Proposition 40.5.2(5), \((\operatorname {div}X)\, \mathrm{vol}_g = d(\iota _X\mathrm{vol}_g)\) is the exterior derivative of a compactly supported form, so its integral vanishes by Stokes’ theorem (Theorem 36.4.8) with \(\partial M = \emptyset \). This is the first equality; the second follows by exchanging \(f\) and \(h\), as the middle term is symmetric. For the special cases take \(h = 1\), so that \(\int \Delta f = \int f\, \Delta 1 = 0\), and \(h = f\). For uniqueness, if \(P\) has the property, then \(u = Ph - \Delta h\) is smooth and \(\int _Mfu\, \mathrm{vol}_g = 0\) for all compactly supported \(f\). Taking \(f = \rho u\) for a bump function \(\rho \geq 0\) gives \(\int _M\rho u^2\, \mathrm{vol}_g = 0\), so \(u = 0\) where \(\rho {\gt} 0\); as \(\rho \) is arbitrary, \(u = 0\).

The identity \(\int f\Delta h = \int h\Delta f\) is the analogue for \(\Delta \) of the symmetry of a symmetric matrix, with the \(L^2\) inner product \((f, h) = \int _Mfh\, \mathrm{vol}_g\) in place of the dot product; \(\int f\Delta f \leq 0\) says that \(\Delta \) is “negative semidefinite”. As for symmetric matrices, this has consequences for eigenvalues.

02WV Corollary 40.14.2

Let \(M\) be compact, oriented and without boundary.

  1. A function with \(\Delta f \geq 0\) everywhere (a subharmonic function), in particular a harmonic function, \(\Delta f = 0\), is locally constant.

  2. If \(\Delta f = -\lambda f\) with \(f \neq 0\), then \(\lambda = \int |\operatorname {grad}f|^2/\int f^2 \geq 0\), and \(\lambda = 0\) only for locally constant \(f\). So the eigenvalues of \(-\Delta \) are nonnegative.

  3. Eigenfunctions \(\Delta f_1 = -\lambda _1f_1\), \(\Delta f_2 = -\lambda _2f_2\) with \(\lambda _1 \neq \lambda _2\) are orthogonal: \(\int _Mf_1f_2\, \mathrm{vol}_g = 0\).

  4. If the equation \(\Delta u = h\) has a solution, then \(\int _Mh\, \mathrm{vol}_g = 0\).

Proof â–¼

All functions have compact support. (1) \(\int \Delta f = 0\) and \(\Delta f \geq 0\) force \(\Delta f = 0\); then \(\int |\operatorname {grad}f|^2 = -\int f\Delta f = 0\), so \(df = 0\) and \(f\) is constant on each connected component. (2) \(\lambda \int f^2 = -\int f\Delta f = \int |\operatorname {grad}f|^2\); if \(\lambda = 0\) then \(df = 0\). (3) \(-\lambda _1\int f_1f_2 = \int f_2\Delta f_1 = \int f_1\Delta f_2 = -\lambda _2\int f_1f_2\). (4) \(\int h = \int \Delta u = 0\).

02WW Example 40.14.3

  1. On the circle \(\mathbb {R}/2\pi \mathbb {Z}\) with \(g = d\theta ^2\), \(\Delta = d^2/d\theta ^2\); the functions \(\cos k\theta \) and \(\sin k\theta \) are eigenfunctions of \(-\Delta \) with eigenvalue \(k^2\), and part (3) of the corollary is the orthogonality of the Fourier basis. On the flat torus \(\mathbb {R}^n/2\pi \mathbb {Z}^n\) the functions \(\cos \langle k, x\rangle \) and \(\sin \langle k, x\rangle \), \(k \in \mathbb {Z}^n\), have eigenvalue \(|k|^2\).

  2. On the round sphere \(S^n\), the restriction of a linear function \(\langle a, x\rangle \) is an eigenfunction of \(-\Delta \) with eigenvalue \(n\) (Exercise 40.17.25); more eigenfunctions are found in Corollary 40.14.9 below.

  3. On a noncompact manifold part (1) fails: \(x\) is a nonconstant harmonic function on \(\mathbb {R}^n\). Compactness enters through the absence of boundary terms in Green’s identities.

What the Laplacian measures. On \(\mathbb {R}\), \(f^{\prime \prime }(p) {\gt} 0\) means that \(f\) bends upwards, so that \(\frac12(f(p + r) + f(p - r)) {\gt} f(p)\) for small \(r\): the average of \(f\) over the two points at distance \(r\) exceeds \(f(p)\), and \(f^{\prime \prime }(p) = \lim _{r\to 0}\frac{2}{r^2}\bigl(\frac12(f(p + r) + f(p - r)) - f(p)\bigr)\). The Laplacian is the same quantity in higher dimension, with the two points replaced by a small sphere.

02WX Proposition 40.14.4 The Laplacian as a mean value

Let \(p \in M\), identify \(T_pM\) with \(\mathbb {R}^n\) by an orthonormal basis, let \(S \subseteq T_pM\) be the unit sphere with its Riemannian measure \(\sigma \), and for small \(r {\gt} 0\) let \(m_f(r) = \frac{1}{\sigma (S)}\int _Sf(\exp _p(rv))\, d\sigma (v)\), the average of \(f\) over the points at distance \(r\) from \(p\) in all directions. Then

\[ m_f(r) = f(p) + \frac{r^2}{2n}\Delta f(p) + O(r^3), \qquad \text{so}\qquad \Delta f(p) = \lim _{r\to 0}\frac{2n}{r^2}\bigl(m_f(r) - f(p)\bigr). \]
Proof â–¼

The map \((r, v) \mapsto f(\exp _p(rv))\) is smooth on \([0, r_0] \times S\) for small \(r_0\), as \(\exp \) is smooth on an open set containing \(0 \in T_pM\). For fixed \(v\), the curve \(\gamma (r) = \exp _p(rv)\) is the geodesic with \(\gamma (0) = p\) and \(\dot\gamma (0) = v\), so by Proposition 40.5.2(4) and Taylor’s formula, \(f(\exp _p(rv)) = f(p) + r\, df_p(v) + \frac{r^2}2\operatorname {Hess}f_p(v, v) + R(r, v)\) with \(|R(r, v)| \leq Cr^3\), where \(C\) bounds the third derivative in \(r\) on the compact set \([0, r_0] \times S\). Now average over \(v \in S\). The orthogonal maps of \(\mathbb {R}^n\) preserve \(S\) and are isometries of it, so they preserve \(\sigma \). The reflection \(v_i \mapsto -v_i\) shows \(\int _Sv_i\, d\sigma = 0\) and \(\int _Sv_iv_j\, d\sigma = 0\) for \(i \neq j\); the coordinate permutations show that \(\int _Sv_i^2\, d\sigma \) does not depend on \(i\), and as \(\sum _iv_i^2 = 1\) on \(S\) it equals \(\sigma (S)/n\). Writing \(df_p(v) = \sum _ia_iv_i\) and \(\operatorname {Hess}f_p(v, v) = \sum _{i,j}b_{ij}v_iv_j\), the averages are \(0\) and \(\frac1n\sum _ib_{ii} = \frac1n\Delta f(p)\) (Proposition 40.5.2(3)).

So \(\Delta f(p) {\gt} 0\) means that near \(p\) the values of \(f\) are on average larger than \(f(p)\): \(p\) lies in a “valley” of \(f\). A harmonic function equals its averages up to order \(r^3\); on \(\mathbb {R}^2\) it equals them exactly (the mean value property of harmonic functions, Chapter 29). At a local maximum, \(\operatorname {Hess}f_p \leq 0\) (Proposition 40.5.2(4)), so \(\Delta f(p) \leq 0\); hence a function with \(\Delta f {\gt} 0\) everywhere has no local maximum. This explains the heat equation \(\partial _tu = \Delta u\): the temperature rises at points whose surroundings are warmer on average.

The Laplacian of the coordinates of a submanifold. For a submanifold \(M \subseteq \mathbb {R}^N\) with the induced metric, the coordinates \(x^1, \ldots , x^N\) restrict to functions on \(M\), and we write \(\Delta x = (\Delta x^1, \ldots , \Delta x^N)\), a map \(M \to \mathbb {R}^N\). Let \(\mathrm{II}\) be the second fundamental form (Theorem 40.11.2) and \(\vec{H} = \frac1n\sum _a\mathrm{II}(e_a, e_a)\) the mean curvature vector, a normal vector; for a hypersurface with unit normal \(\nu \), \(\vec{H} = H\nu \) (Definition 40.11.9).

02WY Proposition 40.14.5 Beltrami’s formula

For a submanifold \(M^n \subseteq \mathbb {R}^N\), \(\operatorname {Hess}x^k(X, Y) = \langle \mathrm{II}(X, Y), e_k\rangle \) for each coordinate \(x^k\), and

\[ \Delta x = n\vec{H} . \]

In particular \(M\) is minimal (\(\vec{H} = 0\)) if and only if its coordinate functions are harmonic on \(M\).

Proof â–¼

For a tangent vector field \(Y\), regarded as a map \(M \to \mathbb {R}^N\), \(Yx^k = \langle Y, e_k\rangle \) is the \(k\)-th component of \(Y\). Differentiating along a tangent field \(X\), \(X(Yx^k) = \langle D_XY, e_k\rangle \), where \(D_XY\) is the ordinary derivative of the vector-valued function \(Y\) along \(X\). By the Gauss formula and Corollary 40.11.3, \(D_XY = \nabla _XY + \mathrm{II}(X, Y)\), so \(\operatorname {Hess}x^k(X, Y) = X(Yx^k) - (\nabla _XY)x^k = \langle D_XY - \nabla _XY, e_k\rangle = \langle \mathrm{II}(X, Y), e_k\rangle \). Taking the trace, \(\Delta x^k = \sum _a\langle \mathrm{II}(e_a, e_a), e_k\rangle = n\langle \vec{H}, e_k\rangle \).

02WZ Example 40.14.6

  1. The sphere. For the unit sphere \(S^n \subseteq \mathbb {R}^{n+1}\) and tangent fields \(X\), \(Y\), differentiating \(\langle Y, x\rangle = 0\) gives \(\langle D_XY, x\rangle = -\langle Y, X\rangle \), so \(\mathrm{II}(X, Y) = -\langle X, Y\rangle x\), \(\vec{H} = -x\) and \(\Delta x = -nx\): each coordinate function is an eigenfunction of \(-\Delta \) with eigenvalue \(n\), as in Example 40.14.3(2).

  2. The catenoid \(x(u, v) = (\cosh v\cos u, \cosh v\sin u, v)\) has \(E = G = \cosh ^2v\), \(F = 0\) (Example 40.13.4), so in these conformal coordinates \(\Delta = \cosh ^{-2}v\, (\partial _u^2 + \partial _v^2)\) (Example 40.5.3(3)). The coordinates \(\cosh v\cos u\), \(\cosh v\sin u\) and \(v\) are harmonic for \(\partial _u^2 + \partial _v^2\) (for the first, \(\partial _u^2\) gives \(-\cosh v\cos u\) and \(\partial _v^2\) gives \(+\cosh v\cos u\)), confirming that the catenoid is minimal.

02X0 Corollary 40.14.7

There is no compact oriented minimal submanifold \(M \subseteq \mathbb {R}^N\) without boundary and of positive dimension.

Proof â–¼

By Proposition 40.14.5 the coordinate functions are harmonic on \(M\), hence constant on each component of \(M\) (Corollary 40.14.2(1)). So each component of \(M\) is a point, which contradicts \(\dim M {\gt} 0\).

So a soap film always needs a wire: a closed soap bubble is not a minimal surface, and indeed it has constant mean curvature \(H \neq 0\), balancing the pressure difference between inside and outside.

Spherical coordinates and spherical harmonics. On \(\mathbb {R}^n \setminus \{ 0\} \) write \(x = r\theta \) with \(r = |x| {\gt} 0\) and \(\theta \in S^{n-1}\), and let \(\theta ^1, \ldots , \theta ^{n-1}\) be local coordinates on \(S^{n-1}\). The map \((r, \theta ) \mapsto r\theta \) sends \(\partial _r\) to \(\theta \), a unit vector, and \(\partial _{\theta ^a}\) to \(r\, \partial _a\theta \), which is orthogonal to \(\theta \) (differentiate \(|\theta |^2 = 1\)). So the Euclidean metric is

\[ g = dr^2 + r^2g_{S^{n-1}}, \]

with \(\sqrt{\det g} = r^{n-1}\sqrt{\det g_{S^{n-1}}}\), \(g^{rr} = 1\), \(g^{ra} = 0\) and \(g^{ab} = r^{-2}g_{S^{n-1}}^{ab}\).

02X1 Proposition 40.14.8

For a smooth function \(f\) on \(\mathbb {R}^n \setminus \{ 0\} \),

\[ \Delta f = \frac{\partial ^2f}{\partial r^2} + \frac{n-1}{r}\frac{\partial f}{\partial r} + \frac{1}{r^2}\Delta _{S^{n-1}}f, \]

where \(\Delta _{S^{n-1}}f\) is the Laplacian of the round unit sphere applied to \(\theta \mapsto f(r\theta )\) for fixed \(r\).

Proof â–¼

Insert the metric above into the coordinate formula for \(\Delta \). The terms with \(i = j = r\) give \(r^{1-n}\partial _r(r^{n-1}\partial _rf) = \partial _r^2f + \frac{n-1}r\partial _rf\), and the terms with \(i, j\) among the \(\theta ^a\) give \(r^{-2}\) times the coordinate formula for \(\Delta _{S^{n-1}}\), since the factor \(r^{n-1}\) in \(\sqrt{\det g}\) does not depend on \(\theta \) and cancels.

For \(n = 2\) this is the formula \(f_{rr} + \frac1rf_r + \frac1{r^2}f_{\theta \theta }\) of Example 40.5.3(2), and for radial functions it is Exercise 40.17.24.

02X2 Corollary 40.14.9 Spherical harmonics

Let \(P\) be a homogeneous polynomial of degree \(k\) on \(\mathbb {R}^n\) with \(\Delta P = 0\), and \(Y = P|_{S^{n-1}}\). Then

\[ \Delta _{S^{n-1}}Y = -k(k + n - 2)\, Y . \]
Proof â–¼

By homogeneity \(P(r\theta ) = r^kY(\theta )\). By Proposition 40.14.8, \(0 = \Delta P = \bigl(k(k - 1) + (n - 1)k\bigr)r^{k-2}Y + r^{k-2}\Delta _{S^{n-1}}Y\), and \(k(k - 1) + (n - 1)k = k(k + n - 2)\).

For \(k = 1\) the eigenvalue of \(-\Delta _{S^{n-1}}\) is \(n - 1 = \dim S^{n-1}\), as in Example 40.14.6(1). For \(k = 2\) and \(n = 3\), the harmonic polynomials \(xy\), \(yz\), \(zx\), \(x^2 - y^2\) and \(x^2 + y^2 - 2z^2\) give eigenfunctions on \(S^2\) with eigenvalue \(6\); in physics these are the orbitals with angular momentum \(\ell = 2\), and the eigenvalue \(\ell (\ell + 1)\) is the square of the angular momentum.

02X3 Remark 40.14.10

Other Laplacians. The formula \(\Delta f = \operatorname {tr}_g\operatorname {Hess}f = \sum _a(\nabla ^2f)(e_a, e_a)\) makes sense for any tensor field \(T\) in place of \(f\): \(\nabla ^*\nabla T = -\sum _a(\nabla _{e_a}\nabla _{e_a}T - \nabla _{\nabla _{e_a}e_a}T)\) is the rough or connection Laplacian (with the geometers’ sign). On differential forms there is also the Hodge Laplacian \(d\delta + \delta d\) of the next section, which equals \(-\Delta \) on functions; on forms of positive degree the two differ by a curvature term (the Weitzenböck formula), which is the source of the Bochner vanishing theorems mentioned there.
