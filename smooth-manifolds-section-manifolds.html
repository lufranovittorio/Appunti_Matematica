32.2 Charts, atlases and smooth structures017M

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

Throughout this section \(M\) is a topological manifold of dimension \(n\): a Hausdorff, second countable space in which every point has an open neighbourhood homeomorphic to an open subset of \(\mathbb {R}^n\) (Definition 19.5.1).

017N Definition 32.2.1

Let \(M\) be a topological \(n\)-manifold.

  1. A chart is a pair \((U, \varphi )\) of an open set \(U \subseteq M\) and a homeomorphism \(\varphi \colon U \to \varphi (U)\) onto an open subset of \(\mathbb {R}^n\). Writing \(\varphi (q) = (x^1(q), \ldots , x^n(q))\) defines functions \(x^i \colon U \to \mathbb {R}\), the coordinates of the chart, and we also write \(\varphi = (x^1, \ldots , x^n)\). The chart is centred at \(p\) if \(\varphi (p) = 0\).

  2. Two charts \((U, \varphi )\) and \((V, \psi )\) are compatible if the transition map

    \[ \psi \circ \varphi ^{-1} \colon \varphi (U \cap V) \to \psi (U \cap V) \]

    is a diffeomorphism (if \(U \cap V = \emptyset \) there is nothing to check). Its inverse is the transition map \(\varphi \circ \psi ^{-1}\), so compatibility is a symmetric relation.

  3. A smooth atlas is a set of pairwise compatible charts whose domains cover \(M\).

  4. A smooth structure is a maximal smooth atlas: one that is not contained in any strictly larger smooth atlas.

  5. A smooth manifold of dimension \(n\) is a topological \(n\)-manifold together with a smooth structure \(\mathcal{A}\). The charts in \(\mathcal{A}\) are its smooth charts, or simply its charts. We usually omit \(\mathcal{A}\) from the notation.

The sets \(\varphi (U \cap V)\) and \(\psi (U \cap V)\) are open in \(\mathbb {R}^n\), because \(U \cap V\) is open and \(\varphi \), \(\psi \) are homeomorphisms onto open sets; so it makes sense to ask whether the transition map is smooth. It is always a homeomorphism, being a composite of homeomorphisms. Compatibility asks for smoothness in both directions, and one direction does not imply the other: see Example 32.2.5(3).

02DM Remark 32.2.2

Here is why compatibility is the right condition. Let \(f \colon M \to \mathbb {R}\) be a function and let \(p\) lie in the domains of two charts \((U, \varphi )\) and \((V, \psi )\). If \(f \circ \varphi ^{-1}\) is smooth near \(\varphi (p)\), then near \(\psi (p)\)

\[ f \circ \psi ^{-1} = (f \circ \varphi ^{-1}) \circ (\varphi \circ \psi ^{-1}), \]

which is smooth by the chain rule (Proposition 26.10.3) if the transition map \(\varphi \circ \psi ^{-1}\) is smooth; and symmetrically with the roles of \(\varphi \) and \(\psi \) exchanged. So for compatible charts the two tests for smoothness of \(f\) near \(p\) give the same answer. For incompatible charts they can give different answers (Example 32.2.5(3)).

A smooth atlas is the data one writes down in practice; a smooth structure is what one wants to work with, because it contains all the charts that are compatible with the given ones, and so lets us shrink charts or change coordinates freely (Lemma 32.2.6). The next lemma shows that the two carry the same information.

0274 Lemma 32.2.3

Every smooth atlas \(\mathcal{A}\) on a topological manifold is contained in a unique maximal smooth atlas, namely the set \(\mathcal{A}^{\max }\) of all charts compatible with every chart of \(\mathcal{A}\).

Proof â–¼

First, \(\mathcal{A}^{\max }\) is a smooth atlas. It contains \(\mathcal{A}\), since the charts of \(\mathcal{A}\) are pairwise compatible, so its domains cover \(M\). Let \((U, \varphi )\) and \((V, \psi )\) be in \(\mathcal{A}^{\max }\); we show that \(\psi \circ \varphi ^{-1}\) is smooth on \(\varphi (U \cap V)\). Smoothness is a local property, so it suffices to check it near \(\varphi (p)\) for each \(p \in U \cap V\). Choose a chart \((W, \chi )\) of \(\mathcal{A}\) with \(p \in W\). On the open set \(\varphi (U \cap V \cap W)\), which contains \(\varphi (p)\),

\[ \psi \circ \varphi ^{-1} = (\psi \circ \chi ^{-1}) \circ (\chi \circ \varphi ^{-1}), \]

and both factors are smooth because \(\varphi \) and \(\psi \) are compatible with \(\chi \); so \(\psi \circ \varphi ^{-1}\) is smooth near \(\varphi (p)\) by the chain rule. Exchanging \(\varphi \) and \(\psi \) shows that the inverse \(\varphi \circ \psi ^{-1}\) is smooth as well, so \(\varphi \) and \(\psi \) are compatible.

Second, \(\mathcal{A}^{\max }\) is maximal: if \(\mathcal{B} \supseteq \mathcal{A}^{\max }\) is a smooth atlas, every chart of \(\mathcal{B}\) is compatible with the charts of \(\mathcal{A} \subseteq \mathcal{B}\), hence lies in \(\mathcal{A}^{\max }\), so \(\mathcal{B} = \mathcal{A}^{\max }\). Third, it is the only maximal atlas containing \(\mathcal{A}\): if \(\mathcal{B}\) is one, its charts are compatible with those of \(\mathcal{A}\), so \(\mathcal{B} \subseteq \mathcal{A}^{\max }\), and maximality of \(\mathcal{B}\) gives equality.

We say that \(\mathcal{A}\) generates the smooth structure \(\mathcal{A}^{\max }\).

02DN Corollary 32.2.4

  1. Let \(\mathcal{A}\) be a smooth structure and \(\mathcal{A}_0 \subseteq \mathcal{A}\) a smooth atlas. A chart belongs to \(\mathcal{A}\) if and only if it is compatible with every chart of \(\mathcal{A}_0\).

  2. Two smooth atlases \(\mathcal{A}\) and \(\mathcal{B}\) generate the same smooth structure if and only if every chart of \(\mathcal{A}\) is compatible with every chart of \(\mathcal{B}\).

Proof â–¼

(1) The smooth structure \(\mathcal{A}\) is a maximal atlas containing \(\mathcal{A}_0\), so \(\mathcal{A} = \mathcal{A}_0^{\max }\) by the uniqueness in Lemma 32.2.3, and \(\mathcal{A}_0^{\max }\) consists of the charts compatible with every chart of \(\mathcal{A}_0\).

(2) If \(\mathcal{A}^{\max } = \mathcal{B}^{\max }\), the charts of \(\mathcal{B}\) lie in \(\mathcal{A}^{\max }\), so they are compatible with the charts of \(\mathcal{A}\). Conversely, if every chart of \(\mathcal{B}\) is compatible with every chart of \(\mathcal{A}\), then \(\mathcal{B} \subseteq \mathcal{A}^{\max }\), so \(\mathcal{A}^{\max }\) is a maximal atlas containing \(\mathcal{B}\), and \(\mathcal{A}^{\max } = \mathcal{B}^{\max }\) by uniqueness.

02DO Example 32.2.5

Let \(M = \mathbb {R}\).

  1. The single chart \((\mathbb {R}, \mathrm{id})\) is a smooth atlas, since the only transition map is \(\mathrm{id}\). The smooth structure it generates is the standard smooth structure on \(\mathbb {R}\). In the same way \((\mathbb {R}^n, \mathrm{id})\) generates the standard smooth structure on \(\mathbb {R}^n\), and whenever we speak of \(\mathbb {R}^n\) as a smooth manifold we mean this one.

  2. The map \(\varphi (t) = t^3\) is a homeomorphism \(\mathbb {R}\to \mathbb {R}\): it is continuous, strictly increasing and surjective, and its inverse \(s \mapsto s^{1/3}\) is continuous. So \((\mathbb {R}, \varphi )\) is a chart, and by itself it is a smooth atlas.

  3. The charts \(\mathrm{id}\) and \(\varphi \) are not compatible. The transition map \(\varphi \circ \mathrm{id}^{-1}(t) = t^3\) is smooth, but its inverse \(\mathrm{id}\circ \varphi ^{-1}(s) = s^{1/3}\) is not differentiable at \(0\): its difference quotient \(s^{1/3}/s = s^{-2/3}\) tends to \(+\infty \) as \(s \to 0\). By Corollary 32.2.4(2), the atlases \(\{ \mathrm{id}\} \) and \(\{ \varphi \} \) generate two different smooth structures on \(\mathbb {R}\). They have different smooth functions in the sense of Remark 32.2.2: the function \(f(t) = t\) satisfies \(f \circ \mathrm{id}^{-1} = f\), which is smooth, while \(f \circ \varphi ^{-1}(s) = s^{1/3}\) is not.

  4. The chart \(\chi (t) = t + t^3\) is compatible with \(\mathrm{id}\). Indeed \(\chi \) is smooth with \(\chi '(t) = 1 + 3t^2 {\gt} 0\), so it is strictly increasing, and it is surjective since it tends to \(\pm \infty \) at \(\pm \infty \). Its inverse is smooth near every point by the inverse function theorem (Theorem 26.11.2), because \(\chi '\) never vanishes. So \(\chi \) belongs to the standard smooth structure of \(\mathbb {R}\).

We shall see in Example 32.3.5 that the two smooth structures of (3), although different, are diffeomorphic.

02DP Lemma 32.2.6

Let \(M\) be a smooth manifold and \((U, \varphi )\) a chart.

  1. If \(W \subseteq U\) is open, then \((W, \varphi |_W)\) is a chart.

  2. If \(\theta \colon \varphi (U) \to \Omega \) is a diffeomorphism onto an open set \(\Omega \subseteq \mathbb {R}^n\), then \((U, \theta \circ \varphi )\) is a chart.

  3. Every \(p \in M\) lies in the domain of a chart \((U, \varphi )\) centred at \(p\) with \(\varphi (U) = B(0, 1)\), and of a chart centred at \(p\) with \(\varphi (U) = \mathbb {R}^n\). These charts can be taken with \(U\) inside any given open neighbourhood of \(p\).

Proof â–¼

By Corollary 32.2.4(1), it suffices in (1) and (2) to show that the new chart is compatible with every chart \((V, \psi )\) of the smooth structure.

(1) The map \(\varphi |_W\) is a homeomorphism onto the open set \(\varphi (W)\). Its transition maps with \(\psi \) are the restrictions of \(\psi \circ \varphi ^{-1}\) and \(\varphi \circ \psi ^{-1}\) to the open sets \(\varphi (W \cap V)\) and \(\psi (W \cap V)\), so they are smooth.

(2) The map \(\theta \circ \varphi \) is a homeomorphism onto \(\Omega \). Its transition maps with \(\psi \) are \(\psi \circ (\theta \circ \varphi )^{-1} = (\psi \circ \varphi ^{-1}) \circ \theta ^{-1}\) and \((\theta \circ \varphi ) \circ \psi ^{-1} = \theta \circ (\varphi \circ \psi ^{-1})\), which are smooth by the chain rule.

(3) Let \((U, \varphi )\) be a chart with \(p \in U\), and \(W\) a given open neighbourhood of \(p\). By (1) we may replace \(U\) by \(U \cap W\). Choose \(r {\gt} 0\) with \(B(\varphi (p), r) \subseteq \varphi (U)\), restrict \(\varphi \) to \(\varphi ^{-1}(B(\varphi (p), r))\) by (1), and compose with the diffeomorphism \(x \mapsto (x - \varphi (p))/r\) of \(B(\varphi (p), r)\) onto \(B(0, 1)\) by (2). This gives a chart centred at \(p\) with image \(B(0, 1)\). Finally the map \(x \mapsto x/(1 - \| x\| ^2)^{1/2}\) is a diffeomorphism \(B(0, 1) \to \mathbb {R}^n\): it is smooth because \(1 - \| x\| ^2 {\gt} 0\) on the ball, and the smooth map \(y \mapsto y/(1 + \| y\| ^2)^{1/2}\) is its inverse, since for \(y = x/(1 - \| x\| ^2)^{1/2}\) we have \(1 + \| y\| ^2 = 1/(1 - \| x\| ^2)\). Composing with it gives a chart centred at \(p\) with image \(\mathbb {R}^n\).

Often we are given a set, not yet a topological space, together with candidate charts. The following lemma builds the topology and the smooth structure at once.

0275 Lemma 32.2.7 Smooth manifold chart lemma

Let \(M\) be a set and \((U_\alpha , \varphi _\alpha )_{\alpha \in A}\) a family of subsets \(U_\alpha \subseteq M\) with injections \(\varphi _\alpha \colon U_\alpha \to \mathbb {R}^n\) such that

  1. each \(\varphi _\alpha (U_\alpha )\) and each \(\varphi _\alpha (U_\alpha \cap U_\beta )\) is open in \(\mathbb {R}^n\);

  2. each \(\varphi _\beta \circ \varphi _\alpha ^{-1} \colon \varphi _\alpha (U_\alpha \cap U_\beta ) \to \varphi _\beta (U_\alpha \cap U_\beta )\) is smooth;

  3. countably many of the \(U_\alpha \) cover \(M\);

  4. for \(p \neq q\) in \(M\), either some \(U_\alpha \) contains both, or there are disjoint \(U_\alpha \ni p\) and \(U_\beta \ni q\).

Then \(M\) has a unique topology and smooth structure for which the \((U_\alpha , \varphi _\alpha )\) are smooth charts.

Proof â–¼

The topology. Declare \(W \subseteq M\) open if \(\varphi _\alpha (W \cap U_\alpha )\) is open in \(\mathbb {R}^n\) for every \(\alpha \). This is a topology: \(\emptyset \) and \(M\) are open by (1); since each \(\varphi _\alpha \) is injective, \(\varphi _\alpha \) commutes with finite intersections, and images always commute with unions.

Each \(U_\beta \) is open and \(\varphi _\beta \) is a homeomorphism onto \(\varphi _\beta (U_\beta )\). The set \(U_\beta \) is open since \(\varphi _\alpha (U_\beta \cap U_\alpha )\) is open by (1). By (2), applied to the pair \((\alpha , \beta )\) and to the pair \((\beta , \alpha )\), the transition map \(\tau = \varphi _\alpha \circ \varphi _\beta ^{-1} \colon \varphi _\beta (U_\alpha \cap U_\beta ) \to \varphi _\alpha (U_\alpha \cap U_\beta )\) is a smooth bijection with smooth inverse, in particular a homeomorphism between open subsets of \(\mathbb {R}^n\). Now let \(V \subseteq U_\beta \). For every \(\alpha \),

\[ \varphi _\alpha (V \cap U_\alpha ) = \tau \bigl(\varphi _\beta (V) \cap \varphi _\beta (U_\alpha \cap U_\beta )\bigr). \]

If \(\varphi _\beta (V)\) is open, the right-hand side is the image under the homeomorphism \(\tau \) of an open subset of \(\varphi _\beta (U_\alpha \cap U_\beta )\), so it is open in the open set \(\varphi _\alpha (U_\alpha \cap U_\beta )\), hence in \(\mathbb {R}^n\); so \(V\) is open in \(M\). Conversely, if \(V\) is open in \(M\), then taking \(\alpha = \beta \) in the definition shows that \(\varphi _\beta (V)\) is open. So \(\varphi _\beta \) maps the open subsets of \(U_\beta \) exactly onto the open subsets of \(\varphi _\beta (U_\beta )\): it is a homeomorphism. In particular \(M\) is locally Euclidean of dimension \(n\).

Hausdorff and second countable. Let \(p \neq q\). If both lie in some \(U_\alpha \), they have disjoint open neighbourhoods in \(U_\alpha \), which is homeomorphic to a subset of \(\mathbb {R}^n\) and hence Hausdorff; these are open in \(M\) because \(U_\alpha \) is. Otherwise (4) gives disjoint open sets \(U_\alpha \ni p\) and \(U_\beta \ni q\). Each \(U_\alpha \) is second countable, being homeomorphic to a subspace of \(\mathbb {R}^n\) (Proposition 17.3.10); if countably many \(U_{\alpha _1}, U_{\alpha _2}, \ldots \) cover \(M\) as in (3), the union of countable bases of the \(U_{\alpha _i}\) is a countable basis of \(M\).

The smooth structure. By (2) the \((U_\alpha , \varphi _\alpha )\) are pairwise compatible charts covering \(M\), so they form a smooth atlas, which generates a unique smooth structure (Lemma 32.2.3).

Uniqueness of the topology. In any topology for which the \(U_\alpha \) are open and the \(\varphi _\alpha \) are homeomorphisms onto open subsets of \(\mathbb {R}^n\), a set \(W\) is open if and only if every \(W \cap U_\alpha \) is open (the \(U_\alpha \) form an open cover), that is, if and only if every \(\varphi _\alpha (W \cap U_\alpha )\) is open. This is the topology defined above.

0276 Counterexample 32.2.8

Condition (4) is needed: the two charts of the line with two origins (Counterexample 19.5.2), each the identity on a copy of \(\mathbb {R}\), satisfy (1)–(3), with transition map the identity of \(\mathbb {R}\setminus \{ 0\} \), but the two origins lie in no common chart and in no disjoint charts, and the resulting space is not Hausdorff.

017O Example 32.2.9

  1. Euclidean spaces, vector spaces and open subsets. \(\mathbb {R}^n\) has its standard smooth structure (Example 32.2.5). A real vector space \(V\) of dimension \(n\) has a canonical smooth structure: every linear isomorphism \(V \to \mathbb {R}^n\) is a chart, and two of them differ by an invertible linear map of \(\mathbb {R}^n\), which is smooth with smooth inverse. If \(W\) is an open subset of a smooth manifold \(M\), the charts \((U \cap W, \varphi |_{U \cap W})\), for \((U, \varphi )\) a chart of \(M\), form a smooth atlas of \(W\): their transition maps are restrictions of transition maps of \(M\) to open sets. With this structure \(W\) is an open submanifold of \(M\). For example, the general linear group \(\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb {R}) = \{ A \in M_n(\mathbb {R}) : \det A \neq 0\} \) is open in \(M_n(\mathbb {R}) \cong \mathbb {R}^{n^2}\), since \(\det \) is a polynomial in the entries, hence continuous; so it is a smooth manifold of dimension \(n^2\).

  2. Spheres. Let \(S^n = \{ x \in \mathbb {R}^{n+1} : \| x\| = 1\} \), \(N = (0, \ldots , 0, 1)\) and \(S = -N\). The stereographic projections

    \[ \sigma _N(x) = \frac{(x_1, \ldots , x_n)}{1 - x_{n+1}} \quad (x \neq N), \qquad \sigma _S(x) = \frac{(x_1, \ldots , x_n)}{1 + x_{n+1}} \quad (x \neq S) \]

    are homeomorphisms \(S^n \setminus \{ N\} \to \mathbb {R}^n\) and \(S^n \setminus \{ S\} \to \mathbb {R}^n\) (Example 17.9.4, and its mirror image under \(x_{n+1} \mapsto -x_{n+1}\)). The inverse of \(\sigma _N\) is \(\tau (y) = (2y, \| y\| ^2 - 1)/(\| y\| ^2 + 1)\), and \(\sigma _N(S^n \setminus \{ N, S\} ) = \mathbb {R}^n \setminus \{ 0\} \) because \(\sigma _N(S) = 0\). For \(y \neq 0\), the last coordinate of \(\tau (y)\) gives \(1 + x_{n+1} = 2\| y\| ^2/(\| y\| ^2 + 1)\), so

    \[ \sigma _S \circ \sigma _N^{-1}(y) = \frac{2y/(\| y\| ^2 + 1)}{2\| y\| ^2/(\| y\| ^2 + 1)} = \frac{y}{\| y\| ^2}. \]

    This map is smooth on \(\mathbb {R}^n \setminus \{ 0\} \) and is its own inverse, so the two charts are compatible and form a smooth atlas of \(S^n\).

  3. Projective spaces. Let \(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^n = (\mathbb {R}^{n+1} \setminus \{ 0\} )/\mathbb {R}^\times \), with points written \([x] = [x_0 : \cdots : x_n]\). By Proposition 19.6.3 it is a topological \(n\)-manifold, the sets \(U_i = \{ [x] : x_i \neq 0\} \) (\(0 \leq i \leq n\)) are open, and the maps

    \[ \varphi _i([x]) = \Bigl(\frac{x_0}{x_i}, \ldots , \widehat{\frac{x_i}{x_i}}, \ldots , \frac{x_n}{x_i}\Bigr) \]

    (the hat means that the entry is omitted) are homeomorphisms \(U_i \to \mathbb {R}^n\). The inverse \(\varphi _i^{-1}(u)\) is the class of the vector obtained from \(u \in \mathbb {R}^n\) by inserting \(1\) in position \(i\). For \(i \neq j\), \(\varphi _i(U_i \cap U_j)\) is the set of \(u\) whose entry in the position of \(x_j\) is nonzero, which is open, and \(\varphi _j \circ \varphi _i^{-1}(u)\) is obtained by dividing this vector by that entry and omitting position \(j\). Its components are of the form \(u_k/u_l\) or \(1/u_l\) with \(u_l \neq 0\), so it is smooth, and the \((U_i, \varphi _i)\) form a smooth atlas. For \(n = 1\) there are two charts, \([x_0 : x_1] \mapsto x_1/x_0\) and \([x_0 : x_1] \mapsto x_0/x_1\), with transition map \(u \mapsto 1/u\) on \(\mathbb {R}\setminus \{ 0\} \). In the same way the complex projective space \(\mathbb {C}\mathbb {P}^n\) is a smooth \(2n\)-manifold, whose transition maps are even holomorphic (Chapter 42).

  4. Products. Let \(M\) and \(N\) be smooth manifolds of dimensions \(m\) and \(n\). Then \(M \times N\) is a topological \((m + n)\)-manifold (Proposition 19.6.1(2)). For charts \((U, \varphi )\) of \(M\) and \((V, \psi )\) of \(N\), the map \(\varphi \times \psi \colon U \times V \to \varphi (U) \times \psi (V) \subseteq \mathbb {R}^{m+n}\) is a homeomorphism onto an open set, these product charts cover \(M \times N\), and the transition map between two of them is \((\varphi ' \times \psi ') \circ (\varphi \times \psi )^{-1} = (\varphi ' \circ \varphi ^{-1}) \times (\psi ' \circ \psi ^{-1})\), which is smooth with smooth inverse. So the product charts form a smooth atlas. For example the torus \(T^n = S^1 \times \cdots \times S^1\) is a smooth \(n\)-manifold.

  5. Graphs. Let \(W \subseteq \mathbb {R}^n\) be open and \(f \colon W \to \mathbb {R}^k\) smooth. The graph \(\Gamma _f = \{ (x, f(x)) : x \in W\} \subseteq \mathbb {R}^{n+k}\) is Hausdorff and second countable as a subspace of \(\mathbb {R}^{n+k}\), and the projection \((x, y) \mapsto x\) is a homeomorphism \(\Gamma _f \to W\), with continuous inverse \(x \mapsto (x, f(x))\). So this projection is a chart, and by itself a smooth atlas. For instance the parabola \(\{ y = x^2\} \) and the paraboloid \(\{ z = x^2 + y^2\} \) are smooth manifolds.
