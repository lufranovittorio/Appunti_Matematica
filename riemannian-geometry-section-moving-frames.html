39.8 Moving frames02NN

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

The curvature of a metric given by an explicit formula can be computed from the Christoffel symbols, but this is laborious: there are \(n^2(n+1)/2\) symbols, each a combination of derivatives of the \(g_{ij}\), and the curvature involves their derivatives and their products. Élie Cartan’s method of moving frames replaces coordinates by orthonormal frames, and the Christoffel symbols by a skew-symmetric matrix of \(1\)-forms, which is found by solving a system of linear equations between differential forms, usually by inspection. The curvature then follows from the formula \(F = d\theta + \theta \wedge \theta \) of Proposition 37.4.3. In this section we develop the method and use it to compute the curvature of surfaces in orthogonal coordinates, of conformally flat metrics in every dimension (in particular hyperbolic space has constant curvature \(-1\)), and of hypersurfaces of Euclidean space, where Cartan’s lemma gives the symmetry of the second fundamental form and the Gauss equation in a few lines. Finally the Gauss–Bonnet–Chern theorem of Chapter 38 is written in terms of the curvature forms, and applied to spaces of constant curvature.

On a first reading this section can be skipped; later sections use only the curvature of hyperbolic space (Corollary 39.8.6).

Let \((M, g)\) be a Riemannian manifold of dimension \(n\) and \(U \subseteq M\) open. An orthonormal frame on \(U\) is a frame \((e_1, \ldots , e_n)\) of \(TM|_U\) with \(\langle e_i, e_j\rangle = \delta _{ij}\); one exists near every point, obtained by applying the Gram–Schmidt process to the coordinate fields of a chart. Its dual coframe is the \(n\)-tuple of \(1\)-forms \((\omega ^1, \ldots , \omega ^n)\) with \(\omega ^i(e_j) = \delta _{ij}\). Then \(\omega ^i(X) = \langle X, e_i\rangle \) and \(g = \sum _i\omega ^i \otimes \omega ^i\), usually written \(g = \sum _i(\omega ^i)^2\); conversely, if \(1\)-forms \(\omega ^1, \ldots , \omega ^n\) on \(U\), linearly independent at every point, satisfy \(g = \sum _i(\omega ^i)^2\), they are the dual coframe of the dual frame, which is orthonormal. So to apply the method to a metric written as a sum of squares, \(g = \sum _i(\omega ^i)^2\), there is nothing to compute: the coframe can be read off. Following Cartan we write the connection form of the Levi-Civita connection in the frame \(e\) (Proposition 37.3.4) as \((\omega ^i_j)\) and its curvature as \((\Omega ^i_j)\), so that

\[ \nabla _Xe_j = \sum _i\omega ^i_j(X)\, e_i, \qquad R(X, Y)e_j = \sum _i\Omega ^i_j(X, Y)\, e_i ; \]

equivalently \(\omega ^i_j(X) = \langle \nabla _Xe_j, e_i\rangle \) and \(\Omega ^i_j(X, Y) = R(X, Y, e_j, e_i)\) (Definition 39.7.1). For a surface with an oriented orthonormal frame, the \(1\)-form \(\omega \) of the proof of Theorem 39.7.9, defined by \(\nabla _Xe_1 = \omega (X)e_2\), is \(\omega ^2_1\).

We need one algebraic lemma about \(1\)-forms.

02NO Lemma 39.8.1 Cartan’s lemma

Let \(\omega ^1, \ldots , \omega ^n\) be \(1\)-forms on an \(n\)-manifold, linearly independent at every point, and let \(\alpha _1, \ldots , \alpha _p\), \(p \leq n\), be \(1\)-forms with \(\sum _{j=1}^p\alpha _j \wedge \omega ^j = 0\). Then \(\alpha _j = \sum _{k=1}^pA_{jk}\, \omega ^k\) with smooth functions \(A_{jk} = A_{kj}\).

Proof â–¼

Let \((e_k)\) be the dual frame and \(A_{jk} = \alpha _j(e_k)\), so that \(\alpha _j = \sum _{k=1}^nA_{jk}\omega ^k\), with smooth \(A_{jk}\). Then \(0 = \sum _{j \leq p}\sum _{k \leq n}A_{jk}\, \omega ^k \wedge \omega ^j\). The \(2\)-forms \(\omega ^a \wedge \omega ^b\) with \(a {\lt} b\) form a basis of the \(2\)-forms at each point (Proposition 11.3.3(3)). For \(a {\lt} b \leq p\) the coefficient of \(\omega ^a \wedge \omega ^b\) is \(A_{ba} - A_{ab}\), which therefore vanishes; for \(a \leq p {\lt} b\) it is \(-A_{ab}\), since the term with \(j = b\) does not occur, and therefore \(A_{ab} = 0\) for \(b {\gt} p\).

02NP Theorem 39.8.2 Cartan’s structure equations

Let \((\omega ^i)\) be the dual coframe of an orthonormal frame \((e_i)\) on \(U\), with connection forms \(\omega ^i_j\) and curvature forms \(\Omega ^i_j\).

  1. \(\omega ^j_i = -\omega ^i_j\).

  2. (First structure equation) \(d\omega ^i = -\sum _j\omega ^i_j \wedge \omega ^j\).

  3. (Second structure equation) \(\Omega ^i_j = d\omega ^i_j + \sum _k\omega ^i_k \wedge \omega ^k_j\), and \(\Omega ^j_i = -\Omega ^i_j\).

  4. \(\Omega ^i_j = \sum _{k {\lt} l}R(e_k, e_l, e_j, e_i)\, \omega ^k \wedge \omega ^l\). In particular the sectional curvature of the plane spanned by \(e_k\) and \(e_l\) is \(K(e_k, e_l) = \Omega ^k_l(e_k, e_l)\), and \(g\) has constant sectional curvature \(\kappa \) on \(U\) if and only if \(\Omega ^i_j = \kappa \, \omega ^i \wedge \omega ^j\) for all \(i\), \(j\).

  5. (Bianchi identities) \(\sum _j\Omega ^i_j \wedge \omega ^j = 0\) and \(d\Omega ^i_j = \sum _k(\Omega ^i_k \wedge \omega ^k_j - \omega ^i_k \wedge \Omega ^k_j)\).

Moreover (1) and (2) characterise the connection forms: if \((\eta ^i_j)\) is a matrix of \(1\)-forms on \(U\) with \(\eta ^j_i = -\eta ^i_j\) and \(d\omega ^i = -\sum _j\eta ^i_j \wedge \omega ^j\) for all \(i\), then \(\eta ^i_j = \omega ^i_j\).

Proof â–¼

(1) The connection is metric: \(0 = X\langle e_i, e_j\rangle = \langle \nabla _Xe_i, e_j\rangle + \langle e_i, \nabla _Xe_j\rangle = \omega ^j_i(X) + \omega ^i_j(X)\).

(2) This is the vanishing of the torsion. For vector fields \(X\) and \(Y = \sum _j\omega ^j(Y)e_j\), \(\nabla _XY = \sum _jX(\omega ^j(Y))e_j + \sum _{i,j}\omega ^j(Y)\omega ^i_j(X)e_i\), so \(\omega ^i(\nabla _XY) = X(\omega ^i(Y)) + \sum _j\omega ^i_j(X)\omega ^j(Y)\). Hence

\[ \omega ^i([X, Y]) = \omega ^i(\nabla _XY - \nabla _YX) = X(\omega ^i(Y)) - Y(\omega ^i(X)) + \sum _j\bigl(\omega ^i_j(X)\omega ^j(Y) - \omega ^i_j(Y)\omega ^j(X)\bigr). \]

The last sum is \(\sum _j(\omega ^i_j \wedge \omega ^j)(X, Y)\), and by the invariant formula \(d\omega ^i(X, Y) = X(\omega ^i(Y)) - Y(\omega ^i(X)) - \omega ^i([X, Y])\) (Theorem 35.2.5), the identity says \(0 = d\omega ^i(X, Y) + \sum _j(\omega ^i_j \wedge \omega ^j)(X, Y)\).

(3) The curvature of the Levi-Civita connection is \(R\) (Definition 39.7.1), and the formula is that of Proposition 37.4.3 in the frame \(e\). For a skew-symmetric matrix of \(1\)-forms \(\theta \), \(d\theta \) is skew-symmetric and so is \(\theta \wedge \theta \): \(\sum _k\theta ^j_k \wedge \theta ^k_i = \sum _k\theta ^k_j \wedge \theta ^i_k = -\sum _k\theta ^i_k \wedge \theta ^k_j\).

(4) A \(2\)-form \(\alpha \) equals \(\sum _{k{\lt}l}\alpha (e_k, e_l)\, \omega ^k \wedge \omega ^l\), as both sides agree on pairs of frame vectors, and \(\Omega ^i_j(e_k, e_l) = R(e_k, e_l, e_j, e_i)\). With \(i = k\), \(j = l\): \(\Omega ^k_l(e_k, e_l) = R(e_k, e_l, e_l, e_k) = K(e_k, e_l)\). By Example 39.7.3, constant curvature \(\kappa \) means \(R(X, Y)Z = \kappa (\langle Y, Z\rangle X - \langle X, Z\rangle Y)\); taking \(Z = e_j\) and pairing with \(e_i\) this says \(\Omega ^i_j(X, Y) = \kappa \bigl(\omega ^i(X)\omega ^j(Y) - \omega ^j(X)\omega ^i(Y)\bigr) = \kappa (\omega ^i \wedge \omega ^j)(X, Y)\), and conversely these identities for all \(i\), \(j\) give the formula for \(R(X, Y)Z\) by linearity in \(Z\).

(5) Differentiating (2), \(0 = dd\omega ^i = -\sum _jd\omega ^i_j \wedge \omega ^j + \sum _j\omega ^i_j \wedge d\omega ^j = -\sum _kd\omega ^i_k \wedge \omega ^k - \sum _{j,k}\omega ^i_j \wedge \omega ^j_k \wedge \omega ^k\), which is \(-\sum _k\Omega ^i_k \wedge \omega ^k\) by (3). The second identity is the Bianchi identity \(dF = F \wedge \theta - \theta \wedge F\) of Proposition 37.4.3.

Finally, let \(\alpha ^i_j = \eta ^i_j - \omega ^i_j\). Then \(\alpha ^j_i = -\alpha ^i_j\) and, subtracting (2), \(\sum _j\alpha ^i_j \wedge \omega ^j = 0\) for each \(i\). By Lemma 39.8.1, \(\alpha ^i_j = \sum _kA^i_{jk}\omega ^k\) with \(A^i_{jk} = A^i_{kj}\), while \(A^j_{ik} = -A^i_{jk}\). A triple of indices with a symmetry in the last two and an antisymmetry in the first two vanishes:

\[ A^i_{jk} = -A^j_{ik} = -A^j_{ki} = A^k_{ji} = A^k_{ij} = -A^i_{kj} = -A^i_{jk} . \]

So \(A = 0\) and \(\eta ^i_j = \omega ^i_j\).

The last statement is what makes the method work. Given a metric written as \(g = \sum _i(\omega ^i)^2\), one computes the \(d\omega ^i\), finds by inspection a skew-symmetric matrix of \(1\)-forms \(\omega ^i_j\) satisfying the first structure equation, and knows that it is the Levi-Civita connection; the second structure equation then gives the curvature forms, and (4) reads off the curvature. Equation (2) says in another language that the identity endomorphism \(\sum _ie_i \otimes \omega ^i \in \Omega ^1(U, TM)\) has \(d_\nabla (\mathrm{id}) = 0\), by the frame formula \(d_\nabla \omega = d\omega + \theta \wedge \omega \) of Proposition 37.4.1: the torsion is the exterior covariant derivative of the identity.

On a surface there is a single connection form \(\omega ^1_2 = -\omega ^2_1\), and everything becomes explicit.

02NQ Proposition 39.8.3

Let \((e_1, e_2)\) be an orthonormal frame on an open subset of a Riemannian surface, with dual coframe \((\omega ^1, \omega ^2)\).

  1. \(\omega ^1_2 = -d\omega ^1(e_1, e_2)\, \omega ^1 - d\omega ^2(e_1, e_2)\, \omega ^2\).

  2. \(d\omega ^1_2 = K\, \omega ^1 \wedge \omega ^2\), where \(K\) is the Gaussian curvature.

  3. (Orthogonal coordinates) If \(g = E\, du^2 + G\, dv^2\) with positive functions \(E\), \(G\), then

    \[ K = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{EG}}\left[\frac{\partial }{\partial v}\left(\frac{(\sqrt E)_v}{\sqrt G}\right) + \frac{\partial }{\partial u}\left(\frac{(\sqrt G)_u}{\sqrt E}\right)\right]. \]

  4. (Geodesic polar coordinates) If \(g = dr^2 + f(r)^2d\varphi ^2\) with \(f {\gt} 0\), then \(K = -f^{\prime \prime }/f\).

Proof â–¼

(1) Write \(\omega ^1_2 = a\, \omega ^1 + b\, \omega ^2\). The first structure equation gives \(d\omega ^1 = -\omega ^1_2 \wedge \omega ^2 = -a\, \omega ^1 \wedge \omega ^2\) and \(d\omega ^2 = -\omega ^2_1 \wedge \omega ^1 = \omega ^1_2 \wedge \omega ^1 = -b\, \omega ^1 \wedge \omega ^2\); evaluate on \((e_1, e_2)\). (2) In the second structure equation for \(\Omega ^1_2\) the quadratic terms \(\omega ^1_1 \wedge \omega ^1_2\) and \(\omega ^1_2 \wedge \omega ^2_2\) vanish, as \(\omega ^1_1 = \omega ^2_2 = 0\); so \(\Omega ^1_2 = d\omega ^1_2\), and \(K = \Omega ^1_2(e_1, e_2)\) by Theorem 39.8.2(4). (3) Here \(\omega ^1 = \sqrt E\, du\) and \(\omega ^2 = \sqrt G\, dv\), so \(d\omega ^1 = (\sqrt E)_v\, dv \wedge du = -\frac{(\sqrt E)_v}{\sqrt{EG}}\, \omega ^1 \wedge \omega ^2\) and \(d\omega ^2 = (\sqrt G)_u\, du \wedge dv = \frac{(\sqrt G)_u}{\sqrt{EG}}\, \omega ^1 \wedge \omega ^2\). By (1),

\[ \omega ^1_2 = \frac{(\sqrt E)_v}{\sqrt{EG}}\, \omega ^1 - \frac{(\sqrt G)_u}{\sqrt{EG}}\, \omega ^2 = \frac{(\sqrt E)_v}{\sqrt G}\, du - \frac{(\sqrt G)_u}{\sqrt E}\, dv, \]

and \(d\omega ^1_2 = -\bigl[\bigl(\frac{(\sqrt E)_v}{\sqrt G}\bigr)_v + \bigl(\frac{(\sqrt G)_u}{\sqrt E}\bigr)_u\bigr]\, du \wedge dv\), while \(K\, \omega ^1 \wedge \omega ^2 = K\sqrt{EG}\, du \wedge dv\); compare with (2). (4) is (3) with \((u, v) = (r, \varphi )\), \(E = 1\) and \(G = f^2\): \(K = -\frac1f(f^{\prime })^{\prime }\).

02NR Example 39.8.4

  1. The round sphere. In spherical coordinates \(g = d\vartheta ^2 + \sin ^2\vartheta \, d\varphi ^2\), so \(K = -(\sin \vartheta )^{\prime \prime }/\sin \vartheta = 1\). Here \(\omega ^1 = d\vartheta \), \(\omega ^2 = \sin \vartheta \, d\varphi \), \(d\omega ^1 = 0\) and \(d\omega ^2 = \cos \vartheta \, d\vartheta \wedge d\varphi = \cot \vartheta \, \omega ^1 \wedge \omega ^2\), so (1) gives \(\omega ^1_2 = -\cos \vartheta \, d\varphi \), in accordance with Example 37.3.5, and \(d\omega ^1_2 = \sin \vartheta \, d\vartheta \wedge d\varphi = \omega ^1 \wedge \omega ^2\).

  2. Polar coordinates. \(dr^2 + r^2d\varphi ^2\) (the plane) has \(K = 0\), and \(dr^2 + \sinh ^2r\, d\varphi ^2\) has \(K = -\sinh r/\sinh r = -1\).

  3. Surfaces of revolution. Rotating a curve \(u \mapsto (f(u), h(u))\) in the half-plane \(\{ (\rho , z) : \rho {\gt} 0\} \), parametrised by arc length (\(f^{\prime 2} + h^{\prime 2} = 1\)), about the \(z\)-axis gives the surface \(x(u, v) = (f(u)\cos v, f(u)\sin v, h(u))\) with metric \(du^2 + f(u)^2dv^2\), so \(K = -f^{\prime \prime }/f\) and \(dA = f\, du \wedge dv\). For the torus with radii \(R {\gt} r {\gt} 0\), \(f(u) = R + r\cos (u/r)\) and \(h(u) = r\sin (u/r)\), so

    \[ K = \frac{\cos (u/r)}{r(R + r\cos (u/r))}, \qquad \iint _TK\, dA = \int _0^{2\pi }\! \! \int _0^{2\pi r}\frac{\cos (u/r)}{r}\, du\, dv = 0 = 2\pi \chi (T), \]

    with \(K {\gt} 0\) on the outer half of the torus and \(K {\lt} 0\) on the inner half, in accordance with the Gauss–Bonnet theorem.

In higher dimensions the method is just as efficient for metrics that are a function times the Euclidean metric.

02NS Proposition 39.8.5 Conformally flat metrics

Let \(U \subseteq \mathbb {R}^n\) be open, \(u \colon U \to \mathbb {R}\) smooth, and \(g = e^{2u}(dx_1^2 + \cdots + dx_n^2)\), with the orthonormal coframe \(\omega ^i = e^u\, dx_i\). Write \(u_i = \partial u/\partial x_i\), \(u_{ik} = \partial ^2u/\partial x_i\partial x_k\), \(|\nabla u|^2 = \sum _ku_k^2\), and \(\alpha _i = du_i - u_i\, du = \sum _k(u_{ik} - u_iu_k)\, dx_k\). Then

\[ \omega ^i_j = u_j\, dx_i - u_i\, dx_j, \qquad \Omega ^i_j = dx_j \wedge \alpha _i - dx_i \wedge \alpha _j - |\nabla u|^2\, dx_i \wedge dx_j . \]

In particular, if \(u_{ik} - u_iu_k = \lambda \, \delta _{ik}\) for a function \(\lambda \), then \(\Omega ^i_j = -e^{-2u}(2\lambda + |\nabla u|^2)\, \omega ^i \wedge \omega ^j\), so at each point all planes have the same sectional curvature \(-e^{-2u}(2\lambda + |\nabla u|^2)\). For \(n = 2\), \(\Omega ^1_2 = -e^{-2u}\Delta u\, \omega ^1 \wedge \omega ^2\), where \(\Delta u = u_{11} + u_{22}\).

Proof â–¼

\(d\omega ^i = e^u\, du \wedge dx_i = \sum _ju_je^u\, dx_j \wedge dx_i\). The proposed forms are skew-symmetric, and

\[ -\sum _j\omega ^i_j \wedge \omega ^j = -e^u\sum _j(u_j\, dx_i - u_i\, dx_j) \wedge dx_j = -e^u\sum _ju_j\, dx_i \wedge dx_j = d\omega ^i, \]

so they are the connection forms by Theorem 39.8.2. For the curvature, \(d\omega ^i_j = du_j \wedge dx_i - du_i \wedge dx_j\), and

\[ \sum _k\omega ^i_k \wedge \omega ^k_j = \sum _k(u_k\, dx_i - u_i\, dx_k) \wedge (u_j\, dx_k - u_k\, dx_j) = u_j\, dx_i \wedge du - |\nabla u|^2\, dx_i \wedge dx_j + u_i\, du \wedge dx_j, \]

the term \(-u_iu_j\sum _kdx_k \wedge dx_k\) being zero. Adding, \(\Omega ^i_j = dx_i \wedge (u_j\, du - du_j) + (u_i\, du - du_i) \wedge dx_j - |\nabla u|^2\, dx_i \wedge dx_j\), which is the formula. If \(\alpha _i = \lambda \, dx_i\) for all \(i\), then \(\Omega ^i_j = \lambda \, dx_j \wedge dx_i - \lambda \, dx_i \wedge dx_j - |\nabla u|^2dx_i \wedge dx_j = -(2\lambda + |\nabla u|^2)e^{-2u}\omega ^i \wedge \omega ^j\). For \(n = 2\), the terms \(u_{12} - u_1u_2\) of \(\alpha _1\) and \(\alpha _2\) contribute \(dx_2 \wedge dx_2 = 0\) and \(dx_1 \wedge dx_1 = 0\), so \(\Omega ^1_2 = -\bigl((u_{11} - u_1^2) + (u_{22} - u_2^2) + |\nabla u|^2\bigr)dx_1 \wedge dx_2 = -\Delta u\, dx_1 \wedge dx_2\).

For \(n = 2\) this is the formula \(K = -e^{-2u}\Delta u\) proved with Christoffel symbols in Proposition 39.11.7 below.

02NT Corollary 39.8.6

  1. The upper half-space \(H^n = \{ x \in \mathbb {R}^n : x_n {\gt} 0\} \) with the metric \((dx_1^2 + \cdots + dx_n^2)/x_n^2\) has constant sectional curvature \(-1\).

  2. The ball \(B^n = \{ |x| {\lt} 1\} \) with the metric \(4(dx_1^2 + \cdots + dx_n^2)/(1 - |x|^2)^2\) has constant sectional curvature \(-1\).

  3. \(\mathbb {R}^n\) with the metric \(4(dx_1^2 + \cdots + dx_n^2)/(1 + |x|^2)^2\) has constant sectional curvature \(1\). It is the pullback of the round metric of \(S^n\) by the inverse of the stereographic projection (Exercise 39.15.13).

Multiplying these metrics by a constant \(c {\gt} 0\) multiplies the curvatures by \(1/c\).

Proof â–¼

We apply Proposition 39.8.5. (1) Here \(u = -\log x_n\), \(u_i = -\delta _{in}/x_n\) and \(u_{ik} = \delta _{in}\delta _{kn}/x_n^2 = u_iu_k\), so \(\lambda = 0\), \(|\nabla u|^2 = 1/x_n^2\) and the curvature is \(-x_n^2 \cdot x_n^{-2} = -1\). (2) Let \(s = 1 - |x|^2\), so \(u = \log 2 - \log s\), \(u_i = 2x_i/s\) and \(u_{ik} = 2\delta _{ik}/s + 4x_ix_k/s^2\). So \(u_{ik} - u_iu_k = (2/s)\delta _{ik}\), \(|\nabla u|^2 = 4|x|^2/s^2\), and \(2\lambda + |\nabla u|^2 = (4s + 4|x|^2)/s^2 = 4/s^2\); with \(e^{-2u} = s^2/4\) the curvature is \(-1\). (3) Now \(s = 1 + |x|^2\), \(u = \log 2 - \log s\), \(u_i = -2x_i/s\), \(u_{ik} = -2\delta _{ik}/s + 4x_ix_k/s^2\), so \(\lambda = -2/s\) and \(2\lambda + |\nabla u|^2 = (-4s + 4|x|^2)/s^2 = -4/s^2\); the curvature is \(1\). Finally, if \((\omega ^i)\) is an orthonormal coframe for \(g\), then \((\sqrt c\, \omega ^i)\) is one for \(cg\); the same forms \(\omega ^i_j\) satisfy the first structure equation for it, as that equation is linear in the coframe, so the connection and curvature forms are unchanged, and \(\Omega ^i_j = \kappa \, \omega ^i \wedge \omega ^j = \frac{\kappa }{c}(\sqrt c\, \omega ^i) \wedge (\sqrt c\, \omega ^j)\).

Next, hypersurfaces. For \(M \subseteq \mathbb {R}^{n+1}\) the frame is completed by a unit normal, and the structure equations of \(\mathbb {R}^{n+1}\) restricted to \(M\) contain the second fundamental form.

02NU Proposition 39.8.7

Let \(M \subseteq \mathbb {R}^{n+1}\) be a hypersurface with the induced metric, \(U \subseteq M\) open with an orthonormal frame \((e_1, \ldots , e_n)\) and a unit normal field \(e_{n+1} = \nu \). Regard the \(e_A\), \(A = 1, \ldots , n + 1\), and the inclusion \(x \colon U \to \mathbb {R}^{n+1}\) as vector-valued functions, and define \(1\)-forms on \(U\) by

\[ dx = \sum _{i=1}^n\omega ^i\, e_i, \qquad de_B = \sum _{A=1}^{n+1}\omega ^A_B\, e_A . \]

  1. \((\omega ^i)\) is the dual coframe of \((e_i)\), \(\omega ^B_A = -\omega ^A_B\), and for \(i, j \leq n\) the \(\omega ^i_j\) are the Levi-Civita connection forms of \(M\).

  2. \(\omega ^{n+1}_i = \sum _jh_{ij}\, \omega ^j\) with \(h_{ij} = h(e_i, e_j)\), the scalar second fundamental form (Definition 39.10.7), and \(h_{ij} = h_{ji}\).

  3. (Gauss equation) \(\Omega ^i_j = \omega ^{n+1}_i \wedge \omega ^{n+1}_j = \sum _{k,l}h_{ik}h_{jl}\, \omega ^k \wedge \omega ^l\), so \(K(e_i, e_j) = h_{ii}h_{jj} - h_{ij}^2\). For a surface in \(\mathbb {R}^3\), \(K = \det (h_{ij})\).

Proof â–¼

(1) For \(X\) tangent to \(M\), \(dx(X) = X\), so \(\omega ^i(X) = \langle X, e_i\rangle \); the normal component of \(dx\) vanishes. Since \(\langle e_A, e_B\rangle \) is constant, \(0 = \langle de_A, e_B\rangle + \langle e_A, de_B\rangle = \omega ^B_A + \omega ^A_B\). For a \(1\)-form \(\beta \) and a vector-valued function \(e\), \(d(\beta \, e) = d\beta \, e - \beta \wedge de\) (apply the Leibniz rule to each component). So

\[ 0 = d(dx) = \sum _i\bigl(d\omega ^i\, e_i - \omega ^i \wedge de_i\bigr) = \sum _A\Bigl(d\omega ^A - \sum _i\omega ^i \wedge \omega ^A_i\Bigr)e_A, \]

where \(\omega ^{n+1} = 0\). For \(A = j \leq n\) this is \(d\omega ^j = -\sum _i\omega ^j_i \wedge \omega ^i\), the first structure equation for the skew-symmetric matrix \((\omega ^j_i)_{i,j \leq n}\), so by Theorem 39.8.2 it is the Levi-Civita connection form of \(M\). (2) For \(A = n + 1\) the same identity gives \(\sum _i\omega ^{n+1}_i \wedge \omega ^i = 0\), so by Lemma 39.8.1 \(\omega ^{n+1}_i = \sum _jh_{ij}\omega ^j\) with \(h_{ij} = h_{ji}\). And \(h_{ij} = \omega ^{n+1}_i(e_j) = \langle de_i(e_j), \nu \rangle = \langle \overline\nabla _{e_j}e_i, \nu \rangle = h(e_j, e_i)\), where \(\overline\nabla \) is the derivative in \(\mathbb {R}^{n+1}\): this is a second proof of the symmetry of the second fundamental form. (3) Similarly \(0 = d(de_B) = \sum _A\bigl(d\omega ^A_B\, e_A - \omega ^A_B \wedge de_A\bigr) = \sum _C\bigl(d\omega ^C_B - \sum _A\omega ^A_B \wedge \omega ^C_A\bigr)e_C\), that is \(d\omega ^C_B + \sum _A\omega ^C_A \wedge \omega ^A_B = 0\): the curvature of \(\mathbb {R}^{n+1}\) vanishes. For \(C = i\), \(B = j \leq n\), separating the term \(A = n + 1\) and using (1) and the second structure equation on \(M\), \(\Omega ^i_j = -\omega ^i_{n+1} \wedge \omega ^{n+1}_j = \omega ^{n+1}_i \wedge \omega ^{n+1}_j\). Evaluating on \((e_i, e_j)\) gives \(K(e_i, e_j) = h_{ii}h_{jj} - h_{ij}h_{ji}\).

This recovers the Theorema Egregium (Corollary 39.10.8) with almost no computation.

02NV Example 39.8.8

For the sphere \(S^n(r) = \{ |x| = r\} \subseteq \mathbb {R}^{n+1}\) take \(\nu = x/r\). Then \(de_{n+1} = dx/r = \sum _i(\omega ^i/r)e_i\), so \(\omega ^i_{n+1} = \omega ^i/r\) and \(\omega ^{n+1}_i = -\omega ^i/r\), that is \(h_{ij} = -\delta _{ij}/r\). By the Gauss equation \(\Omega ^i_j = \frac{1}{r^2}\omega ^i \wedge \omega ^j\): the sphere of radius \(r\) has constant sectional curvature \(1/r^2\), in every dimension, and without computing a single Christoffel symbol.

Finally we return to the Gauss–Bonnet–Chern theorem. Let \(M\) be oriented of dimension \(n = 2m\). In an oriented orthonormal frame the Levi-Civita curvature forms \(\Omega ^i_j\) form a skew-symmetric matrix of \(2\)-forms, and its Pfaffian (Definition 11.4.7) divided by \((2\pi )^m\) is the Euler form \(e(TM, \nabla )\) of Definition 38.6.2. In dimension \(2\) it is \(\frac{1}{2\pi }\Omega ^1_2 = \frac{1}{2\pi }K\, dA\), by Proposition 39.8.3(2) and Proposition 39.3.3; in dimension \(4\) it is \(\frac{1}{4\pi ^2}(\Omega ^1_2 \wedge \Omega ^3_4 - \Omega ^1_3 \wedge \Omega ^2_4 + \Omega ^1_4 \wedge \Omega ^2_3)\).

02NW Corollary 39.8.9 Gauss–Bonnet–Chern

Let \(M\) be a compact oriented Riemannian manifold of dimension \(2m\).

  1. \(\displaystyle \chi (M) = \frac{1}{(2\pi )^m}\int _M\mathrm{Pf}(\Omega )\), and this equals the sum of the indices of any vector field on \(M\) with isolated zeros.

  2. If \(M\) has constant sectional curvature \(\kappa \), then \(\displaystyle \chi (M) = \frac{(2m-1)!!\, \kappa ^m}{(2\pi )^m}\operatorname {vol}(M)\), where \((2m-1)!! = 1 \cdot 3 \cdots (2m-1)\).

  3. \(\operatorname {vol}(S^{2m}) = 2(2\pi )^m/(2m-1)!!\) for the round unit sphere; for instance \(\operatorname {vol}(S^2) = 4\pi \) and \(\operatorname {vol}(S^4) = 8\pi ^2/3\).

  4. A compact oriented \(2m\)-manifold with a metric of constant curvature \(-1\) has \((-1)^m\chi (M) {\gt} 0\) and volume \((2\pi )^m|\chi (M)|/(2m-1)!!\); for surfaces of genus \(g \geq 2\) the area is \(4\pi (g - 1)\).

Proof â–¼

(1) is Corollary 38.6.13 for the Levi-Civita connection. (2) By Theorem 39.8.2(4), \(\Omega ^i_j = \kappa \, \omega ^i \wedge \omega ^j\), so by Proposition 11.4.8(1)

\[ \mathrm{Pf}(\Omega ) = \frac{\kappa ^m}{2^mm!}\sum _{\sigma \in S_{2m}}\operatorname {sgn}(\sigma )\, \omega ^{\sigma (1)} \wedge \cdots \wedge \omega ^{\sigma (2m)} = \frac{(2m)!}{2^mm!}\kappa ^m\, \omega ^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \omega ^{2m}, \]

and \(\omega ^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \omega ^{2m}\) is the volume form (Proposition 39.3.3), while \((2m)!/(2^mm!) = (2m-1)!!\). (3) follows from (2) with \(\kappa = 1\) (Example 39.8.8) and \(\chi (S^{2m}) = 2\) (Theorem 20.6.1). (4) follows from (2) with \(\kappa = -1\), since the volume is positive; for a surface of genus \(g\), \(\chi = 2 - 2g\).

So the volume of a compact manifold of even dimension with constant curvature \(-1\) is determined by its Euler characteristic; for surfaces this will reappear in Chapter 42, through the uniformisation theorem. In the opposite direction, (2) shows that a compact oriented manifold of even dimension with \(\chi (M) = 0\), such as a torus or \(S^1 \times S^3\), carries no metric of constant curvature \(\kappa \neq 0\), and that the sign of \(\chi \) restricts the possible signs of \(\kappa \).
