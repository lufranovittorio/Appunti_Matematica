66.3 The projective case: classes of divisors01X2

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

01X3 Theorem 66.3.1 Lefschetz \((1,1)\)

Let \(X\) be a nonsingular complex projective variety. Every class in \(\mathrm{Hdg}^1(X; \mathbb {Z})\) is the class of a divisor: the cycle class map \(\operatorname {CH}^1(X) \to \mathrm{Hdg}^1(X; \mathbb {Z})\) is surjective. In particular \(\mathrm{HC}(X, 1)\) holds, even integrally.

Proof â–¼

By Theorem 66.2.1, a class \(a \in \mathrm{Hdg}^1(X; \mathbb {Z})\) is \(c_1(L)\) for a holomorphic line bundle \(L\) on \(X^{\mathrm{an}}\). By GAGA (Corollary 55.4.3(2)), \(L\) is the analytification of an algebraic line bundle, still denoted \(L\). Let \(H\) be a hyperplane section. By Serre’s theorem (Theorem 52.5.3), \(L(m) = L \otimes \mathcal{O}(mH)\) is globally generated for \(m \gg 0\), so it has a nonzero section, whose divisor \(D_1\) is effective with \(\mathcal{O}(D_1) \cong L(m)\) (Proposition 54.3.2). Likewise \(\mathcal{O}(mH)\) has the divisor \(D_2 = mH\). So \(L \cong \mathcal{O}(D_1 - D_2)\), and \(a = c_1(L) = \mathrm{cl}(D_1) - \mathrm{cl}(D_2)\) by Example 60.3.2(2).

01X4 Corollary 66.3.2

For a nonsingular projective variety \(X\) of dimension \(n\), \(\mathrm{HC}(X, n - 1)\) holds. Consequently the Hodge conjecture holds for all nonsingular projective varieties of dimension at most \(3\).

Proof â–¼

\(\mathrm{HC}(X, 1)\) holds by Theorem 66.3.1, and implies \(\mathrm{HC}(X, n - 1)\) for \(n \geq 2\) by Proposition 65.3.2. Together with the trivial cases \(p = 0\) and \(p = n\) (Proposition 65.3.1), this covers all \(p\) when \(n \leq 3\).

01X5 Remark 66.3.3

For curves on a threefold, the integral version fails in general: Proposition 65.3.2 only gives that some multiple of an integral Hodge class in \(H^4\) is algebraic, since \(L^{n-2}\) need not be an isomorphism over \(\mathbb {Z}\). Kollár’s counterexamples to the integral Hodge conjecture are integral classes in \(H^4\) of threefolds (Chapter 70). On the other hand, Voisin proved the integral Hodge conjecture for curve classes on uniruled threefolds and on Calabi–Yau threefolds [Voi06].

01X6 Example 66.3.4

  1. For \(X \subseteq \mathbb {P}^3\) a nonsingular quartic surface, \(\mathrm{NS}(X)\) is a sublattice of \(H^2(X; \mathbb {Z}) \cong \mathbb {Z}^{22}\) of rank \(\rho \in [1, 20]\), and \(\rho = 20\) is attained (for instance by the Fermat quartic).

  2. For \(E \times E'\), \(\mathrm{NS} \cong \mathbb {Z}^2 \oplus \operatorname {Hom}(E, E')\) (Exercise 58.7.1), which by Theorem 66.3.1 is also the lattice of integral classes of type \((1,1)\), a statement about Hodge structures (Example 65.3.6).

02BB Example 66.3.5

Let \(X = \mathbb {P}^1 \times \mathbb {P}^1\), which is isomorphic to a nonsingular quadric surface in \(\mathbb {P}^3\) (Example 43.5.2), with projections \(\mathrm{pr}_1\), \(\mathrm{pr}_2\), and let \(h\) be the positive generator of \(H^2(\mathbb {P}^1; \mathbb {Z})\). By the Künneth formula (Theorem 21.4.2), \(H^2(X; \mathbb {Z}) = \mathbb {Z}a \oplus \mathbb {Z}b\) with \(a = \mathrm{pr}_1^*h\) and \(b = \mathrm{pr}_2^*h\). By naturality of \(c_1\) (Theorem 34.4.2), \(a = c_1(\mathrm{pr}_1^*\mathcal{O}(1))\) and \(b = c_1(\mathrm{pr}_2^*\mathcal{O}(1))\); these are the classes of the divisors \(\{ p\} \times \mathbb {P}^1\) and \(\mathbb {P}^1 \times \{ p\} \), the two rulings of the quadric. So every integral class is algebraic and \(\rho (X) = b_2(X) = 2\), without any Hodge theory. Conversely this determines the Hodge numbers: \(\rho (X) \leq h^{1,1}(X)\) (Corollary 66.2.2) and \(h^{2,0} + h^{1,1} + h^{0,2} = b_2 = 2\) with \(h^{2,0} = h^{0,2}\) force \(h^{1,1} = 2\) and \(h^{2,0} = 0\). The intersection numbers are \(a^2 = b^2 = 0\) and \(ab = 1\): two lines of the same ruling are disjoint, and two lines of different rulings meet transversally in one point.

01X7 Theorem 66.3.6 Noether–Lefschetz

For \(d \geq 4\), a very general nonsingular surface of degree \(d\) in \(\mathbb {P}^3\) has \(\operatorname {Pic}(X) = \mathbb {Z}\cdot \mathcal{O}_X(1)\), so \(\rho (X) = 1\).

This is proved in [Voi03] and [GH85]. The proof shows, with the infinitesimal variation of Hodge structure (Chapter 67), that the locus of surfaces on which a given integral class stays of type \((1,1)\) is a proper subvariety of the parameter space, unless the class is a multiple of the hyperplane class; a very general surface lies outside all these countably many loci. For \(d \leq 3\) the statement is false: quadrics contain two families of lines, and cubic surfaces contain \(27\) lines.
