18.14 The Cantor set and space-filling curves03AB

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

The Cantor set is the basic example of a compact space that is as disconnected as possible, and, surprisingly, it maps onto every cube; from this one gets curves that fill a square. Throughout, \(\Sigma = \{ 0, 1\} ^{\mathbb {N}_{{\gt}0}}\), the set of sequences \(a = (a_1, a_2, \ldots )\) of zeros and ones, is the product of countably many discrete two-point spaces, compact by Tychonoff’s theorem. A basis of its topology is formed by the cylinders \([w] = \{ a : a_1 = w_1, \ldots , a_k = w_k\} \) for finite words \(w = w_1 \cdots w_k\) in \(0\) and \(1\).

03ACLean Definition 18.14.1

The Cantor set is \(C = c(\Sigma ) \subseteq [0, 1]\), where \(c(a) = \sum _{n \geq 1}2a_n3^{-n}\): the numbers with a ternary expansion using only the digits \(0\) and \(2\).

03AD Proposition 18.14.2

  1. The map \(c \colon \Sigma \to C\) is a homeomorphism, and \(C\) is compact.

  2. For a word \(w\) of length \(k\), \(c([w])\) lies in the closed interval \(J_w\) of length \(3^{-k}\) with left endpoint \(\sum _{n \leq k}2w_n3^{-n}\); the intervals \(J_{w0}\) and \(J_{w1}\) are the left and right thirds of \(J_w\), and \(C = \bigcap _k\bigcup _{|w| = k}J_w\). The complement \([0, 1] \setminus C\) is the union of the open middle thirds \(M_w\) of the intervals \(J_w\), which are pairwise disjoint and have their endpoints \(c(w0111\cdots )\) and \(c(w1000\cdots )\) in \(C\). So \(C\) is the classical middle-thirds Cantor set: from \([0, 1]\) remove the open middle third, from each remaining interval its open middle third, and so on.

  3. \(C\) is uncountable, has no isolated points and empty interior, and is totally disconnected: its only connected subsets are points and \(\emptyset \).

Proof â–¼

(1) If \(a, b\) agree in the first \(N\) coordinates, \(|c(a) - c(b)| \leq \sum _{n {\gt} N}2 \cdot 3^{-n} = 3^{-N}\); since the cylinders form a basis, \(c\) is continuous. If \(a \neq b\) and \(n\) is the first index with \(a_n \neq b_n\), say \(a_n = 0\), \(b_n = 1\), then \(c(b) - c(a) \geq 2 \cdot 3^{-n} - \sum _{m {\gt} n}2 \cdot 3^{-m} = 3^{-n} {\gt} 0\). So \(c\) is a continuous bijection from a compact space onto the Hausdorff space \(C\), hence a homeomorphism (Proposition 18.6.3(4)), and \(C\) is compact.

(2) For \(a \in [w]\), \(c(a)\) is the left endpoint plus \(\sum _{n {\gt} k}2a_n3^{-n} \in [0, 3^{-k}]\). The left endpoints of \(J_{w0}\) and \(J_{w1}\) are those of \(J_w\) plus \(0\) and \(2 \cdot 3^{-k-1}\), and their lengths are \(3^{-k-1}\), which gives the thirds. So \(C \subseteq \bigcap _k\bigcup _{|w| = k}J_w\). Conversely, the \(2^k\) intervals \(J_w\) with \(|w| = k\) are pairwise disjoint (by induction, as the two thirds are disjoint), so a point \(x\) of the intersection lies, for each \(k\), in exactly one \(J_{w^{(k)}}\), and these words extend each other. Let \(a\) be the infinite word they define; then \(x\) and \(c(a)\) both lie in \(J_{w^{(k)}}\) for all \(k\), so \(|x - c(a)| \leq 3^{-k}\) for all \(k\), and \(x = c(a) \in C\). If \(x \in [0, 1] \setminus C\), let \(k\) be the largest integer such that \(x\) lies in some \(J_w\) with \(|w| = k\) (level \(0\) is \(J_\emptyset = [0, 1]\)); then \(x \in J_w \setminus (J_{w0} \cup J_{w1}) = M_w\). The set \(M_w\) lies in \(J_w\) and misses \(J_{w0} \cup J_{w1}\), which contain all the deeper intervals inside \(J_w\), so it is disjoint from every \(M_{w^{\prime }}\) with \(w^{\prime } \neq w\). Finally the right endpoint of \(J_{w0}\) is \(\sum _{n \leq k}2w_n3^{-n} + 3^{-k-1} = c(w0111\cdots )\), and the left endpoint of \(J_{w1}\) is \(c(w1000\cdots )\).

(3) \(\Sigma \) is uncountable by Cantor’s theorem (Theorem 3.6.3), as it is in bijection with the set of subsets of \(\mathbb {N}_{{\gt}0}\). Changing the \(n\)-th coordinate of \(a\) for large \(n\) gives points \(b \neq a\) arbitrarily close to \(a\), so there are no isolated points. If \(a \neq b\), say \(a_n \neq b_n\), the cylinder \(\{ x : x_n = a_n\} \) (a union of finitely many basic cylinders) is open and closed, contains \(a\) and not \(b\); so no connected subset contains both. Finally, a nonempty open subset of \([0, 1]\) contains an interval with more than one point, which is connected (Theorem 18.5.3); so \(C\) has empty interior.

So \(C\) is a nonempty compact metric space that is totally disconnected and has no isolated points. Brouwer’s theorem says that these properties characterise it: every such space is homeomorphic to \(C\) [Kec95]. For instance \(C \times C\) and \(C^\mathbb {N}\) are homeomorphic to \(C\); for these one can see it directly (Exercise 18.14.5).

03AE Proposition 18.14.3

  1. The map \(\varphi \colon C \to [0, 1]\), \(\varphi (c(a)) = \sum _{n \geq 1}a_n2^{-n}\), is a continuous surjection.

  2. For every \(m \geq 1\) there is a continuous surjection \(C \to [0, 1]^m\), and there is a continuous surjection \(C \to [0, 1]^\mathbb {N}\) onto the Hilbert cube.

Proof â–¼

(1) \(\varphi \circ c\) is continuous, as in the proof of (1) above with \(2^{-N}\) in place of \(3^{-N}\), and \(c\) is a homeomorphism. Every \(t \in [0, 1]\) has a binary expansion: define \(a_n = 1\) if \(s_{n-1} + 2^{-n} \leq t\) and \(a_n = 0\) otherwise, where \(s_n = \sum _{m \leq n}a_m2^{-m}\), \(s_0 = 0\). By induction \(0 \leq t - s_n \leq 2^{-n}\) (if \(a_n = 0\) then \(t - s_{n-1} {\lt} 2^{-n}\), and if \(a_n = 1\) then \(0 \leq t - s_{n-1} - 2^{-n} \leq 2^{-n}\)), so \(s_n \to t\) and \(\varphi (c(a)) = t\).

(2) Choose a bijection \(\mathbb {N}_{{\gt}0} \to \mathbb {N}_{{\gt}0} \times \mathbb {N}_{{\gt}0}\), \(n \mapsto (i(n), j(n))\) (Corollary 3.6.13). The map \(\Sigma \to \Sigma ^{\mathbb {N}_{{\gt}0}}\) sending \(a\) to the sequence of sequences \((b^{(i)})_i\) with \(b^{(i)}_j = a_n\) for \((i, j) = (i(n), j(n))\) is a bijection, and it is continuous in both directions, because each coordinate of the image is a coordinate of \(a\) and conversely. So \(\Sigma \cong \Sigma ^{\mathbb {N}_{{\gt}0}}\), and composing with \(c\) and with the product of copies of \(\varphi \circ c\), which is continuous by Proposition 18.4.7(2) and surjective, gives a continuous surjection \(C \to [0, 1]^\mathbb {N}\). For \([0, 1]^m\) use instead a bijection \(\mathbb {N}_{{\gt}0} \to \{ 1, \ldots , m\} \times \mathbb {N}_{{\gt}0}\), such as \(m(j - 1) + i \mapsto (i, j)\) for \(1 \leq i \leq m\), \(j \geq 1\).

The map \(\varphi \) is not injective: \(c(0111\cdots )\) and \(c(1000\cdots )\), the two endpoints \(\frac13\) and \(\frac23\) of the first removed interval, both map to \(\frac12\). Extended to \([0, 1]\) by constancy on the removed intervals, \(\varphi \) becomes the Cantor function, continuous, nondecreasing and constant on each removed interval, which reappears in measure theory as a continuous function with derivative \(0\) almost everywhere. The same idea of filling the gaps gives a curve through every point of a square.

03AFLean Theorem 18.14.4 Space-filling curves

For every \(m \geq 1\) there is a continuous surjection \(\gamma \colon [0, 1] \to [0, 1]^m\).

Proof â–¼

Let \(\psi \colon C \to [0, 1]^m\) be a continuous surjection (Proposition 18.14.3). By Proposition 18.14.2(2), \([0, 1] \setminus C\) is the union of the removed open intervals \((u, v)\), which are pairwise disjoint and have endpoints \(u, v \in C\). Define \(\gamma = \psi \) on \(C\) and

\[ \gamma ((1 - \lambda )u + \lambda v) = (1 - \lambda )\psi (u) + \lambda \psi (v) \qquad (0 {\lt} \lambda {\lt} 1) \]

on each removed interval \((u, v)\). The values lie in the convex set \([0, 1]^m\), and \(\gamma \) is surjective since \(\psi \) is. On the closure \([u, v]\) of each removed interval \(\gamma \) is affine, hence continuous.

Let \(t \in C\); we show that \(\gamma \) is continuous at \(t\) from the right (if \(t {\lt} 1\)); the left side is symmetric. If \(t\) is the left endpoint of a removed interval, \(\gamma \) is affine on it, and continuity from the right is clear. Otherwise every interval \((t, t + \eta )\) with \(0 {\lt} \eta \leq 1 - t\) meets \(C\): if it did not, this connected set would lie in the union of the pairwise disjoint open removed intervals, hence in one of them, \((u, v)\) (Lemma 18.5.2); then \(u \leq t\), and \(t \in C\) is not in \((u, v)\), so \(t = u\), which we excluded. Let \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\). As \(C\) is compact, \(\psi \) is uniformly continuous (Corollary 18.7.5): there is \(\delta {\gt} 0\) with \(\| \psi (x) - \psi (y)\| {\lt} \varepsilon \) for \(x, y \in C\), \(|x - y| {\lt} \delta \). Choose \(s \in C \cap (t, t + \min (\delta , 1 - t))\). Let \(t {\lt} t^{\prime } {\lt} s\). If \(t^{\prime } \in C\), then \(\| \gamma (t^{\prime }) - \gamma (t)\| {\lt} \varepsilon \). Otherwise \(t^{\prime }\) lies in a removed interval \((u, v)\), and since \(t, s \in C\), \(t \leq u {\lt} v \leq s\); so \(\psi (u)\) and \(\psi (v)\) lie in the open ball of radius \(\varepsilon \) about \(\psi (t)\), which is convex, and so does \(\gamma (t^{\prime })\), a convex combination of them. Hence \(\| \gamma (t^{\prime }) - \gamma (t)\| {\lt} \varepsilon \) for \(t {\lt} t^{\prime } {\lt} s\).

Peano found the first such curve in 1890, and Hilbert a simpler geometric one in 1891, as a limit of polygonal paths through a grid of \(4^k\) squares; the construction above is Lebesgue’s. The curve cannot be injective: a continuous bijection \([0, 1] \to [0, 1]^2\) would be a homeomorphism (Proposition 18.6.3(4)), but removing an interior point disconnects \([0, 1]\), while the square minus a point is still path-connected, hence connected (Proposition 18.5.7(2)): two points can be joined by a segment, or by two segments through a third point if the segment between them passes through the removed point. So dimension is not preserved by continuous surjections, but it is preserved by homeomorphisms: \([0, 1]^m \cong [0, 1]^n\) only for \(m = n\), by the invariance of dimension proved with homology (Chapter 21). Every compact metric space is a continuous image of \(C\) (the Alexandroff–Hausdorff theorem [Kec95]), which is one reason for the importance of the Cantor set.

03AG Exercise 18.14.5

Show that \(\Sigma \times \Sigma \) and \(\Sigma ^{\mathbb {N}_{{\gt}0}}\) are homeomorphic to \(\Sigma \), so that \(C \times C \cong C^\mathbb {N}\cong C\). Show also that \(C\) is homeomorphic to the set of \(x \in [0, 1]\) whose decimal expansion uses only the digits \(3\) and \(7\) (with a suitable meaning of “expansion”).

Solution â–¼

The proof of Proposition 18.14.3(2) gives \(\Sigma \cong \Sigma ^{\mathbb {N}_{{\gt}0}}\), and with the bijection \(\mathbb {N}_{{\gt}0} \to \{ 1, 2\} \times \mathbb {N}_{{\gt}0}\), \(2n - 1 \mapsto (1, n)\), \(2n \mapsto (2, n)\), it gives \(\Sigma \cong \Sigma \times \Sigma \): a sequence is split into its odd and even terms. For the decimal set, let \(d(a) = \sum _n(3 + 4a_n)10^{-n}\). As in the proof of Proposition 18.14.2(1), \(d\) is continuous, and it is injective: if \(n\) is the first index with \(a_n = 0\), \(b_n = 1\), then \(d(b) - d(a) \geq 4 \cdot 10^{-n} - \sum _{m {\gt} n}4 \cdot 10^{-m} = 4 \cdot 10^{-n}(1 - \frac19) {\gt} 0\). So \(d\) is a homeomorphism from \(\Sigma \) onto its image, which is the set of numbers \(\sum _nd_n10^{-n}\) with \(d_n \in \{ 3, 7\} \), and \(c \circ d^{-1}\) is a homeomorphism of it with \(C\).
