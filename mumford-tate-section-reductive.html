64.3 Reductivity and Hodge classes01VQ

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

01VR Theorem 64.3.1

If \(H\) is polarisable, then \(\mathrm{MT}(H)\) and \(\mathrm{Hg}(H)\) are reductive.

Proof â–¼

By Theorem 64.2.3, the \(\mathrm{MT}(H)\)-stable subspaces of \(H_\mathbb {Q}\) are the sub-Hodge structures. Since \(H\) is polarisable, every sub-Hodge structure has a complement (Theorem 63.5.3), so \(H_\mathbb {Q}\) is a semisimple representation of \(\mathrm{MT}(H)\). It remains semisimple after extension of scalars to \(\bar{\mathbb {Q}}\), as we are in characteristic \(0\) [Mil17]. Let \(U = R_u(\mathrm{MT}(H)_{\bar{\mathbb {Q}}})\). On each irreducible summand \(W\) of \(H_{\bar{\mathbb {Q}}}\), the unipotent normal subgroup \(U\) has a nonzero fixed vector (Kolchin’s theorem [Bor91]), and the fixed space \(W^U\) is stable under \(\mathrm{MT}(H)\) because \(U\) is normal, so \(W^U = W\). Hence \(U\) acts trivially on the faithful representation \(H_{\bar{\mathbb {Q}}}\), so \(U = 1\) and \(\mathrm{MT}(H)\) is reductive. The same argument applies to \(\mathrm{Hg}(H)\), a normal subgroup of the reductive group \(\mathrm{MT}(H)\) (it is normalised by \(\mathrm{MT}(H)\) because \(h(\mathbb {S})\) is commutative), whose representation \(H_{\bar{\mathbb {Q}}}\) remains semisimple.

01VS Theorem 64.3.2

Let \(H\) be a \(\mathbb {Q}\)-Hodge structure, pure of weight \(k\).

  1. A rational tensor \(t \in T^{m,n}(H)_\mathbb {Q}\) is a Hodge class, that is, of type \((p, p)\) with \(2p = k(m - n)\), if and only if it is fixed by \(\mathrm{Hg}(H)\).

  2. If \(H\) is polarisable, \(\mathrm{Hg}(H)\) is the subgroup of \(\mathrm{GL}(H_\mathbb {Q})\) fixing all Hodge classes in all tensor spaces \(T^{m,n}(H)\).

Proof â–¼

(1) The circle \(z \in \mathbb {U}(1)(\mathbb {R})\), \(|z| = 1\), acts on \(T^{p,q}\) by \(z^{-p}\bar z^{-q} = z^{q-p}\). So a rational \(t\) is fixed by \(h(\mathbb {U}(1)(\mathbb {R}))\) if and only if all its components have \(p = q\), that is, \(t\) is a Hodge class. The stabiliser of \(t\) is an algebraic subgroup defined over \(\mathbb {Q}\) (Proposition 57.3.3), and it contains \(h(\mathbb {U}(1)(\mathbb {R}))\) if and only if it contains \(\mathrm{Hg}(H)\). (2) By (1), \(\mathrm{Hg}(H)\) is contained in the subgroup fixing all Hodge classes. By Theorem 64.3.1 \(\mathrm{Hg}(H)\) is reductive, so by Theorem 57.3.2(3) it is the subgroup fixing all tensors fixed by \(\mathrm{Hg}(H)\), which by (1) are the Hodge classes.

01VT Corollary 64.3.3

Let \(A\) be a complex abelian variety and \(H = H^1(A; \mathbb {Q})\). Then \(H^\bullet (A; \mathbb {Q}) \cong \Lambda ^\bullet H\) as Hodge structures, and the Hodge classes on \(A\) are \(\bigoplus _p\mathrm{Hdg}^p(A) = (\Lambda ^\bullet H)^{\mathrm{Hg}(H)}\). The same holds for all powers \(A^r\), with \(H^1(A^r) = H^{\oplus r}\) and the same Hodge group.

Proof â–¼

\(H^\bullet (A; \mathbb {Q}) = \Lambda ^\bullet H^1(A; \mathbb {Q})\) by Proposition 58.2.1, compatibly with Hodge decompositions (cup product is a morphism of Hodge structures), and \(\Lambda ^\bullet H\) is a direct summand of the tensor algebra. Apply Theorem 64.3.2(1). For \(A^r\), the Hodge group of \(H^{\oplus r}\) is the diagonal image of \(\mathrm{Hg}(H)\) by Proposition 64.2.2(3).

02CP Proposition 64.3.4

Let \(H\) be pure of weight \(k\) with a polarisation \(Q\). Then \(\mathrm{Hg}(H)\) is contained in the automorphism group \(\mathrm{Aut}(H_\mathbb {Q}, Q)\) of the form, which is the symplectic group \(\mathrm{Sp}(H_\mathbb {Q}, Q)\) for \(k\) odd and the orthogonal group \(\mathrm{O}(H_\mathbb {Q}, Q)\) for \(k\) even. Consequently \(\mathrm{MT}(H)\) is contained in the group of similitudes \(\{ g : Q(gx, gy) = \lambda (g)Q(x, y)\} \).

Proof â–¼

Viewed as an element of \(T^{0,2}(H) = (H^\vee )^{\otimes 2}\), a Hodge structure of weight \(-2k\), the form \(Q\) is rational and lies in \(\bigoplus _{p+q=k}(H^{p,q})^\vee \otimes (H^{q,p})^\vee \) by condition (2) of Definition 63.5.1. By Theorem 63.3.3 the dual \((H^{p,q})^\vee \) has type \((-p,-q)\), so \(Q\) has type \((-k, -k)\) and is a Hodge class. By Theorem 64.3.2(1) it is fixed by \(\mathrm{Hg}(H)\), that is, \(Q(gx, gy) = Q(x, y)\) for \(g \in \mathrm{Hg}(H)\). The form is alternating for \(k\) odd and symmetric for \(k\) even. The last statement follows from Proposition 64.2.2(2), since scalars are similitudes.

02CQ Proposition 64.3.5

For a \(\mathbb {Q}\)-Hodge structure \(H\) pure of weight \(k\), the following are equivalent: \(\mathrm{Hg}(H) = 1\); every element of \(H_\mathbb {Q}\) is a Hodge class; \(H\) is of Tate type, that is, \(k = 2p\) is even and \(H \cong \mathbb {Q}(-p)^{\oplus r}\). In that case \(\mathrm{MT}(H) = \mathbb {G}_m\) (scalars) if \(p \neq 0\), and \(\mathrm{MT}(H) = 1\) if \(p = 0\).

Proof â–¼

If \(\mathrm{Hg}(H) = 1\), every rational vector is fixed, hence a Hodge class by Theorem 64.3.2(1). If every rational vector is a Hodge class, then \(H_\mathbb {C}\), spanned by \(H_\mathbb {Q}\), equals \(H^{p,p}\), and a basis of \(H_\mathbb {Q}\) gives \(H \cong \mathbb {Q}(-p)^{\oplus r}\). If \(H\) is of Tate type, \(\mathbb {U}(1)(\mathbb {R})\) acts on \(H^{p,p}\) by \(z^{-p}\bar z^{-p} = |z|^{-2p} = 1\), so the smallest \(\mathbb {Q}\)-group containing its image is trivial. The last statement follows from Proposition 64.2.2(2): \(\mathrm{MT}(H) = \mathbb {G}_m \cdot \mathrm{Hg}(H) = \mathbb {G}_m\) if the weight \(2p\) is nonzero, and \(\mathrm{MT}(H) = \mathrm{Hg}(H) = 1\) if \(p = 0\).

02CR Example 64.3.6

Let \(S\) be a nonsingular projective surface and \(H = H^2(S; \mathbb {Q})\). By Proposition 64.3.5, \(\mathrm{Hg}(H)\) is trivial exactly when \(h^{2,0}(S) = 0\), and then every class in \(H^2(S; \mathbb {Q})\) is a Hodge class; by the Lefschetz \((1,1)\)-theorem (Chapter 66) all of them are then classes of divisors. When \(h^{2,0} \neq 0\), the Hodge group fixes every Hodge class, in particular the class of a hyperplane section and all of \(\mathrm{NS}(S) \otimes \mathbb {Q}\), and it preserves the polarisation of Theorem 63.5.2 (Proposition 64.3.4); so it acts on the orthogonal complement of \(\mathrm{NS}(S) \otimes \mathbb {Q}\) for the intersection form, the transcendental part, preserving the intersection form, and it is nontrivial there.
