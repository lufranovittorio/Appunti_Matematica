12.9 The root space decomposition02QQ

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

In \(\mathfrak {sl}_n\) the diagonal matrices \(\mathfrak {h}\) act on \(\mathfrak {sl}_n\) by \(\operatorname {ad}\) diagonalisably: \([h, E_{ij}] = (h_i - h_j)E_{ij}\) for \(h = \operatorname {diag}(h_1, \ldots , h_n)\). So \(\mathfrak {sl}_n\) is the sum of \(\mathfrak {h}\) and of the lines \(\mathbb {C}E_{ij}\), on which \(\mathfrak {h}\) acts through the linear forms \(h \mapsto h_i - h_j\), the roots. This section shows that every semisimple Lie algebra has such a decomposition, and that the roots satisfy strong integrality conditions, all coming from the representation theory of \(\mathfrak {sl}_2\). In this section \(k = \mathbb {C}\) (the proofs work over any algebraically closed field of characteristic \(0\)), and \(\mathfrak {g}\) is semisimple, with Killing form \(\kappa \).

02QR Definition 12.9.1

A subalgebra \(\mathfrak {t} \subseteq \mathfrak {g}\) is toral if all its elements are semisimple, that is, every \(\operatorname {ad}x\), \(x \in \mathfrak {t}\), is diagonalisable. A Cartan subalgebra of \(\mathfrak {g}\) is a maximal toral subalgebra (one not contained in a larger toral subalgebra).

For semisimple Lie algebras over \(\mathbb {C}\) this agrees with the general definition of a Cartan subalgebra as a nilpotent subalgebra equal to its own normaliser [Hum72, Section 15].

02QS Lemma 12.9.2

Toral subalgebras are abelian, and if \(\mathfrak {g} \neq 0\) it has nonzero toral subalgebras. Hence Cartan subalgebras exist, and they are abelian and nonzero.

Proof â–¼

Let \(\mathfrak {t}\) be toral and \(x \in \mathfrak {t}\). Since \(\operatorname {ad}_{\mathfrak {t}}x\) is the restriction of the diagonalisable \(\operatorname {ad}x\) to an invariant subspace, it is diagonalisable, and it suffices to show that it has no nonzero eigenvalue. Suppose \([x, y] = ay\) with \(y \in \mathfrak {t}\) and \(a \neq 0\). Then \(\operatorname {ad}_{\mathfrak {t}}y(x) = -ay\) is a nonzero eigenvector of \(\operatorname {ad}_{\mathfrak {t}}y\) with eigenvalue \(0\). But \(\operatorname {ad}_{\mathfrak {t}}y\) is diagonalisable: writing \(x = \sum _iv_i\) with \([y, v_i] = \lambda _iv_i\), the vector \([y, x] = \sum _{\lambda _i \neq 0}\lambda _iv_i\) lies in the sum of the eigenspaces with nonzero eigenvalues, which meets the kernel only in \(0\); contradiction.

If every \(x \in \mathfrak {g}\) satisfied \(x_s = 0\), every \(\operatorname {ad}x\) would be nilpotent, and \(\mathfrak {g}\) would be nilpotent by Engel’s theorem (Corollary 12.5.6), hence solvable, hence \(0\). So some \(x_s\) is nonzero, and \(\mathbb {C}x_s\) is toral. A toral subalgebra of maximal dimension is maximal.

Fix a Cartan subalgebra \(\mathfrak {h}\). Its elements commute, so the operators \(\operatorname {ad}h\), \(h \in \mathfrak {h}\), are commuting diagonalisable endomorphisms of \(\mathfrak {g}\), and they are simultaneously diagonalisable: apply Theorem 10.6.6 to \(\operatorname {ad}h_1\), then to the restrictions of \(\operatorname {ad}h_2\) to its eigenspaces, and so on, for a basis \(h_1, \ldots , h_l\) of \(\mathfrak {h}\). A common eigenvector \(x\) satisfies \([h, x] = \alpha (h)x\) for a linear form \(\alpha \in \mathfrak {h}^*\). Hence

\[ \mathfrak {g} = \bigoplus _{\alpha \in \mathfrak {h}^*}\mathfrak {g}_\alpha , \qquad \mathfrak {g}_\alpha = \{ x \in \mathfrak {g} : [h, x] = \alpha (h)x \text{ for all } h \in \mathfrak {h}\} . \]

Here \(\mathfrak {g}_0 = C_{\mathfrak {g}}(\mathfrak {h})\) is the centraliser of \(\mathfrak {h}\), which contains \(\mathfrak {h}\). The roots of \(\mathfrak {g}\) with respect to \(\mathfrak {h}\) are the nonzero \(\alpha \) with \(\mathfrak {g}_\alpha \neq 0\); they form a finite set \(\Phi \subseteq \mathfrak {h}^*\), and \(\mathfrak {g}_\alpha \) is the root space of \(\alpha \).

02QT Proposition 12.9.3

For \(\alpha , \beta \in \mathfrak {h}^*\):

  1. \([\mathfrak {g}_\alpha , \mathfrak {g}_\beta ] \subseteq \mathfrak {g}_{\alpha + \beta }\);

  2. if \(\alpha \neq 0\) and \(x \in \mathfrak {g}_\alpha \), then \(\operatorname {ad}x\) is nilpotent;

  3. if \(\alpha + \beta \neq 0\), then \(\kappa (\mathfrak {g}_\alpha , \mathfrak {g}_\beta ) = 0\);

  4. the restriction of \(\kappa \) to \(\mathfrak {g}_0\) is nondegenerate.

Proof â–¼

(1) For \(x \in \mathfrak {g}_\alpha \), \(y \in \mathfrak {g}_\beta \), the Jacobi identity gives \([h, [x, y]] = [[h, x], y] + [x, [h, y]] = (\alpha (h) + \beta (h))[x, y]\). (2) By (1), \(\operatorname {ad}x\) maps \(\mathfrak {g}_\gamma \) into \(\mathfrak {g}_{\gamma + \alpha }\), and only finitely many \(\mathfrak {g}_\gamma \) are nonzero. (3) Choose \(h\) with \(\alpha (h) + \beta (h) \neq 0\); by invariance, for \(x \in \mathfrak {g}_\alpha \) and \(y \in \mathfrak {g}_\beta \), \(\alpha (h)\kappa (x, y) = \kappa ([h, x], y) = -\kappa ([x, h], y) = -\kappa (x, [h, y]) = -\beta (h)\kappa (x, y)\), so \(\kappa (x, y) = 0\). (4) By (3), \(\mathfrak {g}_0\) is orthogonal to all \(\mathfrak {g}_\alpha \) with \(\alpha \neq 0\); so an element of \(\mathfrak {g}_0\) orthogonal to \(\mathfrak {g}_0\) is orthogonal to \(\mathfrak {g}\), hence zero (Theorem 12.7.8).

02QU Theorem 12.9.4

\(C_{\mathfrak {g}}(\mathfrak {h}) = \mathfrak {h}\). Hence \(\mathfrak {g} = \mathfrak {h} \oplus \bigoplus _{\alpha \in \Phi }\mathfrak {g}_\alpha \), and the restriction of \(\kappa \) to \(\mathfrak {h}\) is nondegenerate.

Proof â–¼

Write \(C = C_{\mathfrak {g}}(\mathfrak {h})\), a subalgebra by the Jacobi identity.

Step 1: \(C\) contains the semisimple and nilpotent parts of its elements. For \(x \in C\), \(\operatorname {ad}x\) maps \(\mathfrak {h}\) to \(0\), and \(\operatorname {ad}x_s\), \(\operatorname {ad}x_n\) are polynomials without constant term in \(\operatorname {ad}x\) (Proposition 12.7.12 and Lemma 12.7.4(1)); so they also kill \(\mathfrak {h}\).

Step 2: the semisimple elements of \(C\) lie in \(\mathfrak {h}\). If \(s \in C\) is semisimple, \(\mathfrak {h} + \mathbb {C}s\) is an abelian subalgebra, and its elements \(h + cs\) have \(\operatorname {ad}(h + cs) = \operatorname {ad}h + c\operatorname {ad}s\), a sum of commuting diagonalisable endomorphisms, which is diagonalisable (Theorem 10.6.6). So \(\mathfrak {h} + \mathbb {C}s\) is toral, and \(s \in \mathfrak {h}\) by maximality.

Step 3: \(\kappa |_{\mathfrak {h}}\) is nondegenerate. Let \(h \in \mathfrak {h}\) with \(\kappa (h, \mathfrak {h}) = 0\), and \(x \in C\). Then \(x_s \in \mathfrak {h}\) by Steps 1 and 2, so \(\kappa (h, x_s) = 0\). And \(x_n \in C\), so \(\operatorname {ad}x_n\) is nilpotent and commutes with \(\operatorname {ad}h\); then \(\operatorname {ad}h\, \operatorname {ad}x_n\) is nilpotent and \(\kappa (h, x_n) = 0\). So \(\kappa (h, C) = 0\), and \(h = 0\) by Proposition 12.9.3(4).

Step 4: \(C\) is nilpotent. For \(x \in C\), \(\operatorname {ad}_Cx_s = 0\) since \(x_s \in \mathfrak {h}\), and \(\operatorname {ad}_Cx_n\) is nilpotent; so every \(\operatorname {ad}_Cx = \operatorname {ad}_Cx_n\) is nilpotent, and \(C\) is nilpotent by Engel’s theorem.

Step 5: \(\mathfrak {h} \cap [C, C] = 0\). By invariance \(\kappa (\mathfrak {h}, [C, C]) = \kappa ([\mathfrak {h}, C], C) = 0\); apply Step 3.

Step 6: \(C\) is abelian. Otherwise \([C, C]\) is a nonzero ideal of the nilpotent Lie algebra \(C\). The operators \(\operatorname {ad}_Cx\) restricted to \([C, C]\) are nilpotent, so by Theorem 12.5.5 they have a common null vector: there is \(z \neq 0\) in \([C, C]\) with \([C, z] = 0\). By Steps 2 and 5, \(z\) is not semisimple, so \(z_n \neq 0\); and \(z_n \in C\) with \(\operatorname {ad}z_n\) a polynomial in \(\operatorname {ad}z\), which kills \(C\), so \([z_n, C] = 0\). For \(y \in C\), \(\operatorname {ad}z_n\) is nilpotent and commutes with \(\operatorname {ad}y\), so \(\kappa (z_n, y) = 0\). This contradicts Proposition 12.9.3(4).

Step 7: \(C = \mathfrak {h}\). Otherwise there is \(x \in C \setminus \mathfrak {h}\), and \(x_n = x - x_s \neq 0\) lies in \(C\) by Steps 1 and 2. Since \(C\) is abelian, \(\operatorname {ad}x_n\) commutes with every \(\operatorname {ad}y\), \(y \in C\), so \(\kappa (x_n, C) = 0\) as in Step 6; again a contradiction.

Since \(\kappa |_{\mathfrak {h}}\) is nondegenerate, every \(\lambda \in \mathfrak {h}^*\) is \(\kappa (t_\lambda , -)\) for a unique \(t_\lambda \in \mathfrak {h}\), and \(\lambda \mapsto t_\lambda \) is a linear isomorphism \(\mathfrak {h}^* \to \mathfrak {h}\). We transport \(\kappa \) to the symmetric bilinear form \((\lambda , \mu ) = \kappa (t_\lambda , t_\mu )\) on \(\mathfrak {h}^*\), which is nondegenerate.

02QV Proposition 12.9.5

  1. \(\Phi \) spans \(\mathfrak {h}^*\).

  2. If \(\alpha \in \Phi \), then \(-\alpha \in \Phi \).

  3. If \(\alpha \in \Phi \), \(x \in \mathfrak {g}_\alpha \) and \(y \in \mathfrak {g}_{-\alpha }\), then \([x, y] = \kappa (x, y)t_\alpha \).

  4. If \(\alpha \in \Phi \), then \((\alpha , \alpha ) = \alpha (t_\alpha ) \neq 0\).

  5. If \(\alpha \in \Phi \) and \(0 \neq x_\alpha \in \mathfrak {g}_\alpha \), there is \(y_\alpha \in \mathfrak {g}_{-\alpha }\) such that \(x_\alpha \), \(y_\alpha \) and \(h_\alpha = [x_\alpha , y_\alpha ]\) span a subalgebra \(S_\alpha \) isomorphic to \(\mathfrak {sl}_2\), by \(x_\alpha \mapsto e\), \(y_\alpha \mapsto f\), \(h_\alpha \mapsto h\). The element \(h_\alpha = 2t_\alpha /(\alpha , \alpha )\), the coroot of \(\alpha \), does not depend on the choices, \(\alpha (h_\alpha ) = 2\), and \(h_{-\alpha } = -h_\alpha \).

Proof â–¼

(1) Otherwise some \(h \neq 0\) in \(\mathfrak {h}\) satisfies \(\alpha (h) = 0\) for all \(\alpha \in \Phi \). Then \([h, \mathfrak {g}_\alpha ] = 0\) for all roots and \([h, \mathfrak {h}] = 0\), so \(h\) is central (Theorem 12.9.4), contradicting semisimplicity.

(2) If \(-\alpha \notin \Phi \), then \(\kappa (\mathfrak {g}_\alpha , \mathfrak {g}_\beta ) = 0\) for all \(\beta \) with \(\mathfrak {g}_\beta \neq 0\) (Proposition 12.9.3(3)), so \(\mathfrak {g}_\alpha \perp \mathfrak {g}\) and \(\mathfrak {g}_\alpha = 0\), which is absurd.

(3) \([x, y] \in \mathfrak {g}_0 = \mathfrak {h}\), and for \(h \in \mathfrak {h}\), \(\kappa (h, [x, y]) = \kappa ([h, x], y) = \alpha (h)\kappa (x, y) = \kappa (h, \kappa (x, y)t_\alpha )\). As \(\kappa |_{\mathfrak {h}}\) is nondegenerate, \([x, y] = \kappa (x, y)t_\alpha \).

(4) Suppose \(\alpha (t_\alpha ) = 0\). For \(x \neq 0\) in \(\mathfrak {g}_\alpha \), \(\kappa (x, \mathfrak {g}_{-\alpha }) \neq 0\), since \(x\) is orthogonal to all other \(\mathfrak {g}_\beta \) and \(\kappa \) is nondegenerate; choose \(y \in \mathfrak {g}_{-\alpha }\) with \(\kappa (x, y) = 1\), so that \([x, y] = t_\alpha \) by (3). Then \([t_\alpha , x] = \alpha (t_\alpha )x = 0\) and \([t_\alpha , y] = 0\), so \(S = \operatorname {span}(x, y, t_\alpha )\) is a solvable subalgebra, with \([S, S] = \mathbb {C}t_\alpha \). By Lie’s theorem applied to \(\operatorname {ad}S \subseteq \mathfrak {gl}(\mathfrak {g})\), the element \(\operatorname {ad}t_\alpha \in [\operatorname {ad}S, \operatorname {ad}S]\) is strictly upper triangular in a suitable basis, hence nilpotent. It is also diagonalisable, as \(t_\alpha \in \mathfrak {h}\); so \(\operatorname {ad}t_\alpha = 0\) and \(t_\alpha \) is central, hence \(0\), contradicting \(\alpha \neq 0\).

(5) Choose \(y_\alpha \in \mathfrak {g}_{-\alpha }\) with \(\kappa (x_\alpha , y_\alpha ) = 2/(\alpha , \alpha )\), which is possible as in (4). By (3), \(h_\alpha = [x_\alpha , y_\alpha ] = 2t_\alpha /(\alpha , \alpha )\), so \(\alpha (h_\alpha ) = 2\), \([h_\alpha , x_\alpha ] = 2x_\alpha \) and \([h_\alpha , y_\alpha ] = -2y_\alpha \). The three elements are linearly independent (they lie in \(\mathfrak {g}_\alpha \), \(\mathfrak {g}_{-\alpha }\), \(\mathfrak {h}\) and are nonzero), and their brackets are those of \(e\), \(f\), \(h\). Since \(t_{-\alpha } = -t_\alpha \), \(h_{-\alpha } = -h_\alpha \).

So \(\mathfrak {g}\) is a representation of each \(S_\alpha \cong \mathfrak {sl}_2\), by \(\operatorname {ad}\), and Section 12.6 applies. In such a representation \(M\) the eigenvalues of \(h_\alpha \), the weights, are integers, \(M\) is a direct sum of irreducible representations \(V(m)\), and since \(V(m)\) has the weights \(m, m - 2, \ldots , -m\), each with multiplicity one, the number of summands is \(\dim M_0 + \dim M_1\), where \(M_j\) is the \(j\)-eigenspace of \(h_\alpha \) (Theorems 12.6.2 and 12.6.3).

02QW Theorem 12.9.6

Let \(\alpha , \beta \in \Phi \).

  1. \(\dim \mathfrak {g}_\alpha = 1\), and the only multiples \(c\alpha \), \(c \in \mathbb {C}\), of \(\alpha \) that are roots are \(\alpha \) and \(-\alpha \).

  2. \(\beta (h_\alpha ) \in \mathbb {Z}\), and \(\beta - \beta (h_\alpha )\alpha \in \Phi \).

  3. Let \(\beta \neq \pm \alpha \), and let \(r\), \(q\) be the largest integers such that \(\beta - r\alpha \) and \(\beta + q\alpha \) are roots. Then \(\beta + i\alpha \in \Phi \) for all \(-r \leq i \leq q\) (the \(\alpha \)-string through \(\beta \) is unbroken), and \(\beta (h_\alpha ) = r - q\).

  4. If \(\alpha + \beta \in \Phi \), then \([\mathfrak {g}_\alpha , \mathfrak {g}_\beta ] = \mathfrak {g}_{\alpha + \beta }\).

  5. \(\mathfrak {h}\) is spanned by the coroots \(h_\alpha \), and \(\mathfrak {g}\) is generated as a Lie algebra by the root spaces.

Proof â–¼

(1) Let \(M = \mathfrak {h} \oplus \bigoplus _{c \neq 0}\mathfrak {g}_{c\alpha }\). By Proposition 12.9.3(1) it is stable under \(\operatorname {ad}S_\alpha \), and \(h_\alpha \) acts on \(\mathfrak {g}_{c\alpha }\) by \(c\alpha (h_\alpha ) = 2c\). So \(2c \in \mathbb {Z}\) whenever \(c\alpha \in \Phi \), and the \(0\)-weight space of \(M\) is \(\mathfrak {h}\). Now \(\mathfrak {h} = \ker \alpha \oplus \mathbb {C}h_\alpha \), and \(S_\alpha \) acts by \(0\) on \(\ker \alpha \): \([x_\alpha , h] = -\alpha (h)x_\alpha = 0\) and \([y_\alpha , h] = 0\) for \(h \in \ker \alpha \). So \(\ker \alpha \oplus S_\alpha \) is a subrepresentation of \(M\), and by Theorem 12.6.3 it has a complementary subrepresentation \(M^{\prime }\). The \(0\)-weight space of \(M\) is contained in \(\ker \alpha \oplus S_\alpha \), so \(M^{\prime }\) has no weight \(0\), hence no summand \(V(m)\) with \(m\) even. Thus the even weights of \(M\) are those of \(\ker \alpha \oplus S_\alpha \), namely \(0\) and \(\pm 2\); as \(\mathfrak {g}_{2\alpha }\) would have weight \(4\), \(2\alpha \notin \Phi \). This holds for every root, so \(\frac12\alpha \) is not a root either (\(\alpha \) would be twice a root). Hence \(M\) has no weight \(1\), \(M^{\prime }\) has no summand \(V(m)\) with \(m\) odd, and \(M^{\prime } = 0\). So \(M = \ker \alpha \oplus S_\alpha \): \(\mathfrak {g}_\alpha = \mathbb {C}x_\alpha \), and \(\mathfrak {g}_{c\alpha } = 0\) for \(c \neq 0, \pm 1\).

(2) and (3) Let \(\beta \neq \pm \alpha \) and \(K = \bigoplus _{i \in \mathbb {Z}}\mathfrak {g}_{\beta + i\alpha }\), a subrepresentation of \(S_\alpha \). No \(\beta + i\alpha \) is \(0\), by (1), so each summand is a root space, of dimension \(1\), or zero. The weight of \(\mathfrak {g}_{\beta + i\alpha }\) is \(\beta (h_\alpha ) + 2i\); these are integers, so \(\beta (h_\alpha ) \in \mathbb {Z}\), they are distinct, and they all have the same parity. So \(\dim K_0 + \dim K_1 = 1\), and \(K\) is irreducible, \(K \cong V(m)\). The weights of \(V(m)\) form the unbroken string \(m, m - 2, \ldots , -m\), so the \(i\) with \(\beta + i\alpha \in \Phi \) form an interval \([-r, q]\), and \(m = \beta (h_\alpha ) + 2q\), \(-m = \beta (h_\alpha ) - 2r\). Hence \(\beta (h_\alpha ) = r - q\), and \(\beta - \beta (h_\alpha )\alpha = \beta + (q - r)\alpha \) is a root, as \(-r \leq q - r \leq q\). For \(\beta = \pm \alpha \), (2) holds since \(\pm \alpha (h_\alpha ) = \pm 2\) and \(-\beta \in \Phi \).

(4) Since \(2\alpha \) and \(0\) are not roots, \(\beta \neq \pm \alpha \), and \(K \cong V(m)\) as above, with \(\mathfrak {g}_\beta \) and \(\mathfrak {g}_{\alpha + \beta }\) the weight spaces of weights \(j = \beta (h_\alpha )\) and \(j + 2 \leq m\). In \(V(m)\), \(e\) maps the weight space \(m - 2i\) onto the weight space \(m - 2i + 2\) for \(1 \leq i \leq m\), since \(ev_i = (m - i + 1)v_{i-1}\) (Theorem 12.6.2). So \(\operatorname {ad}x_\alpha \colon \mathfrak {g}_\beta \to \mathfrak {g}_{\alpha + \beta }\) is nonzero, and onto, both spaces being one-dimensional.

(5) The coroots are nonzero multiples of the \(t_\alpha \), and the \(t_\alpha \) span \(\mathfrak {h}\) because \(\Phi \) spans \(\mathfrak {h}^*\). As \(h_\alpha = [x_\alpha , y_\alpha ]\), the subalgebra generated by the root spaces contains \(\mathfrak {h}\), hence everything.

02S1 Corollary 12.9.7 Dimension from the roots

\(\dim \mathfrak {g} = \dim \mathfrak {h} + |\Phi |\): the dimension of a semisimple Lie algebra is its rank plus the number of its roots. The number of roots is even, as \(\alpha \mapsto -\alpha \) is a fixed point free involution of \(\Phi \).

Proof â–¼

\(\mathfrak {g} = \mathfrak {h} \oplus \bigoplus _{\alpha \in \Phi }\mathfrak {g}_\alpha \) by Theorem 12.9.4, each \(\mathfrak {g}_\alpha \) is a line by Theorem 12.9.6(1), and \(-\Phi = \Phi \) by Proposition 12.9.5(2).

For example \(\mathfrak {sl}_n\) has rank \(n - 1\) and \(n(n - 1)\) roots, and \((n - 1) + n(n - 1) = n^2 - 1\) (Example 12.9.9).

02QX Theorem 12.9.8

  1. For \(h, h^{\prime } \in \mathfrak {h}\), \(\kappa (h, h^{\prime }) = \sum _{\gamma \in \Phi }\gamma (h)\gamma (h^{\prime })\). For \(\lambda \in \mathfrak {h}^*\) and \(\alpha \in \Phi \), \(\lambda (h_\alpha ) = 2(\lambda , \alpha )/(\alpha , \alpha )\).

  2. \((\alpha , \beta ) \in \mathbb {Q}\) for all \(\alpha , \beta \in \Phi \).

  3. Let \(E_\mathbb {Q}\) be the \(\mathbb {Q}\)-span of \(\Phi \) in \(\mathfrak {h}^*\). Every basis of \(\mathfrak {h}^*\) consisting of roots is a basis of \(E_\mathbb {Q}\) over \(\mathbb {Q}\); so \(\dim _\mathbb {Q}E_\mathbb {Q}= \dim \mathfrak {h}\), the rank of \(\mathfrak {g}\).

  4. The form \((\ ,\ )\) is positive definite on \(E_\mathbb {Q}\), and extends to an inner product on the real vector space \(E = \mathbb {R}\otimes _\mathbb {Q}E_\mathbb {Q}\).

In \(E\), with \(\langle \beta , \alpha \rangle = 2(\beta , \alpha )/(\alpha , \alpha )\):

  1. \(\Phi \) is finite, spans \(E\), and does not contain \(0\);

  2. the only multiples of \(\alpha \in \Phi \) in \(\Phi \) are \(\pm \alpha \);

  3. for \(\alpha , \beta \in \Phi \), \(\sigma _\alpha (\beta ) = \beta - \langle \beta , \alpha \rangle \alpha \in \Phi \);

  4. for \(\alpha , \beta \in \Phi \), \(\langle \beta , \alpha \rangle \in \mathbb {Z}\).

Proof â–¼

(1) \(\operatorname {ad}h\, \operatorname {ad}h^{\prime }\) is \(0\) on \(\mathfrak {h}\) and \(\gamma (h)\gamma (h^{\prime })\) on the line \(\mathfrak {g}_\gamma \). And \(\lambda (h_\alpha ) = \kappa (t_\lambda , h_\alpha ) = 2\kappa (t_\lambda , t_\alpha )/(\alpha , \alpha )\).

(2) By (1), \((\lambda , \mu ) = \sum _\gamma \gamma (t_\lambda )\gamma (t_\mu ) = \sum _\gamma (\gamma , \lambda )(\gamma , \mu )\). For \(\beta \in \Phi \), dividing \((\beta , \beta ) = \sum _\gamma (\gamma , \beta )^2\) by \((\beta , \beta )^2 \neq 0\) gives \(1/(\beta , \beta ) = \frac14\sum _\gamma \gamma (h_\beta )^2\), which is rational by Theorem 12.9.6(2). So \((\beta , \beta ) \in \mathbb {Q}\), and \((\alpha , \beta ) = \frac12\alpha (h_\beta )(\beta , \beta ) \in \mathbb {Q}\).

(3) Let \(\alpha _1, \ldots , \alpha _l \in \Phi \) be a basis of \(\mathfrak {h}^*\), and \(\beta = \sum _ic_i\alpha _i \in \Phi \) with \(c_i \in \mathbb {C}\). Then \((\beta , \alpha _j) = \sum _ic_i(\alpha _i, \alpha _j)\) for all \(j\). The matrix \(((\alpha _i, \alpha _j))\) has rational entries by (2) and is invertible, as \((\ ,\ )\) is nondegenerate; so by Cramer’s rule the \(c_i\) are rational. Hence the \(\alpha _i\) span \(E_\mathbb {Q}\) over \(\mathbb {Q}\), and they are linearly independent over \(\mathbb {Q}\), even over \(\mathbb {C}\).

(4) For \(\lambda \in E_\mathbb {Q}\), the numbers \((\gamma , \lambda )\) are rational, and \((\lambda , \lambda ) = \sum _\gamma (\gamma , \lambda )^2 \geq 0\), with equality only if \((\gamma , \lambda ) = 0\) for all roots \(\gamma \), that is, \(\lambda = 0\), since \(\Phi \) spans \(\mathfrak {h}^*\). In the basis \((\alpha _i)\) the Gram matrix \(G\) is a rational symmetric matrix with \(x^TGx {\gt} 0\) for \(x \in \mathbb {Q}^l \setminus 0\); by density of \(\mathbb {Q}^l\) in \(\mathbb {R}^l\) and continuity, \(x^TGx \geq 0\) for all \(x \in \mathbb {R}^l\). A real eigenvector \(x\) of \(G\) with eigenvalue \(\mu \) (Theorem 10.9.6) gives \(\mu |x|^2 = x^TGx \geq 0\), and \(\mu \neq 0\) since \(\det G \neq 0\); so all eigenvalues are positive, and \(G\) defines an inner product on \(E = \mathbb {R}\otimes _\mathbb {Q}E_\mathbb {Q}= \mathbb {R}^l\).

(R1) is clear, (R2) is Theorem 12.9.6(1) (a real multiple of \(\alpha \) lying in \(E_\mathbb {Q}\) is a rational multiple), and (R3), (R4) are Theorem 12.9.6(2), since \(\langle \beta , \alpha \rangle = \beta (h_\alpha )\) by (1).

02QY Example 12.9.9

The roots of \(\mathfrak {sl}_n\). Let \(\mathfrak {h} \subseteq \mathfrak {sl}_n(\mathbb {C})\) be the diagonal matrices of trace \(0\), and \(\varepsilon _i \in \mathfrak {h}^*\) the \(i\)-th diagonal entry. Since \([h, E_{ij}] = (\varepsilon _i(h) - \varepsilon _j(h))E_{ij}\), \(\mathfrak {h}\) is toral. Its centraliser is \(\mathfrak {h}\): choosing \(h\) with distinct diagonal entries, \([h, x] = \sum _{i \neq j}(h_i - h_j)x_{ij}E_{ij}\) vanishes only if \(x\) is diagonal. A toral subalgebra containing \(\mathfrak {h}\) is abelian (Lemma 12.9.2), hence contained in \(C(\mathfrak {h}) = \mathfrak {h}\); so \(\mathfrak {h}\) is a Cartan subalgebra. The roots are \(\varepsilon _i - \varepsilon _j\), \(i \neq j\), with root spaces \(\mathbb {C}E_{ij}\); there are \(n(n - 1)\) of them, and the rank is \(n - 1\). With \(\kappa (x, y) = 2n\operatorname {tr}(xy)\) (Example 12.7.9), for \(\alpha = \varepsilon _i - \varepsilon _j\) one finds \(t_\alpha = (E_{ii} - E_{jj})/2n\), \((\alpha , \alpha ) = 1/n\) and \(h_\alpha = E_{ii} - E_{jj}\), and \(S_\alpha \) is the copy of \(\mathfrak {sl}_2\) spanned by \(E_{ij}\), \(E_{ji}\) and \(E_{ii} - E_{jj}\). For distinct \(i\), \(j\), \(l\), \((\varepsilon _i - \varepsilon _j, \varepsilon _j - \varepsilon _l) = -1/2n\). So all roots have the same length, and the angle between \(\varepsilon _i - \varepsilon _j\) and \(\varepsilon _j - \varepsilon _l\) is \(2\pi /3\). Concretely, \(E\) is the hyperplane \(x_1 + \cdots + x_n = 0\) of \(\mathbb {R}^n\) with \(\frac1{2n}\) times the standard inner product, and \(\Phi = \{ e_i - e_j : i \neq j\} \); this root system is called \(A_{n-1}\). For \(n = 2\) there are two roots \(\pm \alpha \), with \(\alpha (h) = 2\).

02QZ Counterexample 12.9.10

A maximal abelian subalgebra need not be a Cartan subalgebra. In \(\mathfrak {sl}_2\), \([e, ae + bh + cf] = -2be + ch\) vanishes only for \(b = c = 0\), so \(\mathbb {C}e\) is a maximal abelian subalgebra; but it consists of nilpotent elements, and it is not toral. By contrast \(\mathbb {C}h\) is a Cartan subalgebra. And over \(\mathbb {R}\) the theory has to be modified: in \(\mathfrak {so}(3)\), for the infinitesimal rotation \(L_3 = E_{21} - E_{12}\) the operator \(\operatorname {ad}L_3\) has eigenvalues \(0, \pm i\), and in fact no nonzero element has \(\operatorname {ad}x\) diagonalisable over \(\mathbb {R}\); over \(\mathbb {C}\), \(\mathbb {C}L_3\) is a Cartan subalgebra of \(\mathfrak {so}_3(\mathbb {C})\).

02R0 Remark 12.9.11

All Cartan subalgebras of a semisimple Lie algebra over \(\mathbb {C}\) are conjugate under the group of automorphisms generated by the \(\exp (\operatorname {ad}x)\), \(x\) nilpotent [Hum72, Section 16]. Hence the root system does not depend, up to isomorphism, on the choice of \(\mathfrak {h}\), and the rank is an invariant of \(\mathfrak {g}\).
