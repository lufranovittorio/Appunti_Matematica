28.15 Differentiation of measures030K

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

The Lebesgue differentiation theorem (Theorem 28.8.4) says that for \(h \in L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb {R}^n)\) the averages \(\frac1{\lambda (B(x, r))}\int _{B(x, r)}h\) tend to \(h(x)\) for almost every \(x\). In terms of the measure \(\nu = h\lambda \) it says that \(\nu (B(x, r))/\lambda (B(x, r)) \to h(x)\): the density of \(\nu \) can be computed by shrinking balls. We now do this for an arbitrary Radon measure \(\nu \) on \(\mathbb {R}^n\). It has a Lebesgue decomposition \(\nu = h\lambda + \nu _s\) with respect to \(\lambda \) (Theorem 28.11.3, which applies since \(\nu \) is \(\sigma \)-finite by Proposition 28.13.2), and the shrinking balls recover \(h\) and do not see the singular part, except on a null set. In this section \(\lambda \) denotes Lebesgue measure on the Borel sets of \(\mathbb {R}^n\).

030L Definition 28.15.1

Let \(\nu \) be a Radon measure on \(\mathbb {R}^n\). The upper and lower derivatives of \(\nu \) with respect to \(\lambda \) at \(x\) are

\[ \overline{D}\nu (x) = \limsup _{r \to 0}\frac{\nu (B(x, r))}{\lambda (B(x, r))}, \qquad \underline{D}\nu (x) = \liminf _{r \to 0}\frac{\nu (B(x, r))}{\lambda (B(x, r))}, \]

in \([0, \infty ]\). If they agree, their common value is the derivative \(D\nu (x)\).

030M Example 28.15.2

For \(\nu = \delta _0\) on \(\mathbb {R}^n\), \(\nu (B(x, r)) = 0\) for \(r \leq \| x\| \), so \(D\nu (x) = 0\) for \(x \neq 0\), while \(D\nu (0) = \lim _r1/\lambda (B(0, r)) = \infty \). For the measure \(\sigma \) on the circle (Example 28.13.5(3)), \(D\sigma = 0\) outside \(S^1\), because the balls around a point at positive distance from \(S^1\) eventually miss \(S^1\); at a point of \(S^1\) the ball \(B(x, r)\) meets \(S^1\) in an arc of length about \(2r\), so \(\sigma (B(x, r))/\pi r^2\) behaves like \(2/(\pi r)\) and \(D\sigma = \infty \). In both cases the derivative is \(0\) almost everywhere, in accordance with the singularity of these measures.

030N Theorem 28.15.3 Differentiation of measures

Let \(\nu \) be a Radon measure on \(\mathbb {R}^n\), and \(\nu = h\lambda + \nu _s\) its Lebesgue decomposition, with \(h \geq 0\) Borel and \(\nu _s \perp \lambda \). Then \(h \in L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb {R}^n)\), and for \(\lambda \)-almost every \(x\)

\[ \lim _{r \to 0}\frac1{\lambda (B(x, r))}\Bigl(\int _{B(x, r)}|h(y) - h(x)|\, d\lambda (y) + \nu _s(B(x, r))\Bigr) = 0 . \]

In particular \(D\nu = h\) almost everywhere, and \(\nu \perp \lambda \) if and only if \(D\nu = 0\) almost everywhere.

Proof â–¼

For compact \(K\), \(\int _Kh\, d\lambda \leq \nu (K) {\lt} \infty \), so \(h \in L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}\), and by the Lebesgue differentiation theorem (Theorem 28.8.4) the first term tends to \(0\) almost everywhere. It remains to show that \(\overline{D}\nu _s = 0\) almost everywhere. Let \(S\) be a Borel set with \(\lambda (S) = 0\) and \(\nu _s(\mathbb {R}^n \setminus S) = 0\). The measure \(\nu _s\) is a Borel measure finite on compact sets (\(\nu _s \leq \nu \)), so it is a Radon measure (Proposition 28.13.2). Fix \(t {\gt} 0\) and \(R {\gt} 0\), and let

\[ A = \{ x \in B(0, R) \setminus S : \overline{D}\nu _s(x) {\gt} t\} . \]

We show \(\lambda ^*(A) = 0\). Let \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\). The Borel set \(B(0, R) \setminus S\) has \(\nu _s\)-measure \(0\), so by outer regularity it is contained in an open set \(U\) with \(\nu _s(U) {\lt} \varepsilon \). Let \(G\) be the union of all open balls \(B\) with \(B \subseteq U\) and \(\nu _s(B) {\gt} t\lambda (B)\). It is open and contains \(A\): for \(x \in A\) we have \(x \in U\), so \(B(x, r) \subseteq U\) for all small \(r\), and \(\nu _s(B(x, r)) {\gt} t\lambda (B(x, r))\) for some of these \(r\). Let \(K \subseteq G\) be compact. Finitely many of these balls cover \(K\), and by the Vitali covering lemma (Lemma 28.8.1) they contain pairwise disjoint balls \(B_1, \ldots , B_k\) with

\[ \lambda (K) \leq 3^n\sum _j\lambda (B_j) {\lt} \frac{3^n}{t}\sum _j\nu _s(B_j) \leq \frac{3^n}{t}\nu _s(U) {\lt} \frac{3^n\varepsilon }{t}. \]

By inner regularity of \(\lambda \), \(\lambda ^*(A) \leq \lambda (G) \leq 3^n\varepsilon /t\), and as \(\varepsilon \) is arbitrary, \(A\) is a null set. Taking the union over \(t = 1/j\) and \(R = j\), \(j \geq 1\), and adding the null set \(S\), we find that \(\overline{D}\nu _s = 0\) outside a null set.

Finally, \(\nu (B(x, r))/\lambda (B(x, r))\) differs from the average of \(h\) over \(B(x, r)\) by \(\nu _s(B(x, r))/\lambda (B(x, r))\), and the average differs from \(h(x)\) by at most the average of \(|h - h(x)|\); so \(D\nu = h\) almost everywhere. If \(\nu \perp \lambda \), then \(h = 0\) almost everywhere by uniqueness of the decomposition, so \(D\nu = 0\) almost everywhere; conversely, if \(D\nu = 0\) almost everywhere, then \(h = 0\) almost everywhere and \(\nu = \nu _s\).

So the Radon–Nikodym derivative of the absolutely continuous part, an object obtained in Theorem 28.11.2 by an abstract Hilbert space argument, can be computed as a limit of ratios. Balls are not essential: any sets that shrink to \(x\) and occupy a fixed proportion of a ball around \(x\) will do.

030O Corollary 28.15.4

Let \(\nu = h\lambda + \nu _s\) be as in Theorem 28.15.3 and \(c {\gt} 0\). For almost every \(x\) the following holds: if \(E_k\) are Borel sets with \(E_k \subseteq B(x, r_k)\), \(r_k \to 0\) and \(\lambda (E_k) \geq c\lambda (B(x, r_k))\), then \(\nu (E_k)/\lambda (E_k) \to h(x)\).

Proof â–¼

Take \(x\) as in the theorem. Then

\[ \Bigl|\frac{\nu (E_k)}{\lambda (E_k)} - h(x)\Bigr| \leq \frac1{\lambda (E_k)}\Bigl(\int _{E_k}|h - h(x)|\, d\lambda + \nu _s(E_k)\Bigr) \leq \frac1{c\lambda (B(x, r_k))}\Bigl(\int _{B(x, r_k)}|h - h(x)|\, d\lambda + \nu _s(B(x, r_k))\Bigr), \]

which tends to \(0\).

030P Corollary 28.15.5 Lebesgue’s theorem on monotone functions

Let \(F \colon [a, b] \to \mathbb {R}\) be continuous and nondecreasing. Then \(F\) is differentiable almost everywhere, \(F^{\prime } \geq 0\) is integrable, and

\[ \int _a^bF^{\prime }\, d\lambda \leq F(b) - F(a), \]

with equality if and only if \(F\) is absolutely continuous.

Proof â–¼

Let \(\mu _F\) be the measure of Lemma 28.12.3, and \(\nu (E) = \mu _F(E \cap [a, b])\) for Borel \(E \subseteq \mathbb {R}\); it is a finite Borel measure on \(\mathbb {R}\), hence a Radon measure. Let \(\nu = h\lambda + \nu _s\) be its Lebesgue decomposition, and let \(x \in (a, b)\) be a point where Corollary 28.15.4 holds with \(c = 1/4\). For \(0 {\lt} r {\lt} \min (b - x, x - a)\), the intervals \((x, x + r]\) and \((x - r, x]\) have length \(r = \frac14\lambda (B(x, 2r))\) and lie in \(B(x, 2r)\), and \(\nu ((x, x + r]) = F(x + r) - F(x)\), \(\nu ((x - r, x]) = F(x) - F(x - r)\). So both one-sided difference quotients of \(F\) at \(x\) tend to \(h(x)\), and \(F^{\prime }(x) = h(x)\). Hence \(F^{\prime } = h\) almost everywhere on \([a, b]\), and

\[ \int _a^bF^{\prime }\, d\lambda = \int _{[a, b]}h\, d\lambda \leq \nu ([a, b]) = F(b) - F(a), \]

with equality if and only if \(\nu _s([a, b]) = 0\), that is, \(\nu _s = 0\). In that case \(F(x) - F(a) = \nu ([a, x]) = \int _a^xh\, d\lambda \) for all \(x\), so \(F\) is absolutely continuous by Theorem 28.12.4. Conversely, if \(F\) is absolutely continuous, the same theorem gives \(F(b) - F(a) = \int _a^bF^{\prime }\).

For the Cantor function (Counterexample 28.8.7) the inequality is strict: \(\mu _F\) is singular, so \(F^{\prime } = 0\) almost everywhere, as we saw directly, while \(F(1) - F(0) = 1\). The corollary holds for all nondecreasing functions, continuous or not: a nondecreasing function has at most countably many jumps, and the jumps contribute a further singular part to its measure [Rud87, Chapter 7]. Since a function of bounded variation is a difference of two nondecreasing functions, it is differentiable almost everywhere as well.

030Q Remark 28.15.6

The proof used Lebesgue measure through the Vitali covering lemma, that is, through the fact that \(\lambda (3B) = 3^n\lambda (B)\). To differentiate a Radon measure \(\nu \) with respect to an arbitrary Radon measure \(\mu \) on \(\mathbb {R}^n\), which may give very different masses to \(B\) and \(3B\), one uses instead the Besicovitch covering theorem: from every family of balls whose centres form a bounded set \(A\) one can extract a subfamily covering \(A\) that splits into a number of disjoint subfamilies depending only on \(n\). With it one shows that \(\nu (B(x, r))/\mu (B(x, r))\) converges \(\mu \)-almost everywhere to the density of the part of \(\nu \) absolutely continuous with respect to \(\mu \), and tends to \(\infty \) almost everywhere for the singular part [EG92, Chapter 1]. This is the setting of geometric measure theory, where \(\mu \) is often a Hausdorff measure restricted to a set.
