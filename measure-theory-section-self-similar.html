28.18 Self-similar sets034O

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

The Cantor set \(C\) is the union of two copies of itself, \(C/3\) and \(C/3 + 2/3\), each scaled by the factor \(\frac13\). If we only knew that \(0 {\lt} \mathcal{H}^s(C) {\lt} \infty \) for some \(s\), the scaling property of Proposition 28.17.3(3) would already give the value of \(s\): the two copies are disjoint Borel sets, so \(\mathcal{H}^s(C) = 2 \cdot 3^{-s}\mathcal{H}^s(C)\), and \(3^s = 2\). Many sets that come up in analysis and dynamics, usually called fractals, are built in the same way from finitely many scaled copies of themselves, and their dimension can be read off from the scaling factors. This section makes the heuristic rigorous: we construct such sets in general (Theorem 28.18.5), and compute their dimension when the copies are disjoint (Theorem 28.18.10). Along the way we meet a second notion of dimension, which is cruder than the Hausdorff dimension but easier to compute. References: [Fal90], [Hut81].

28.18.1 Box-counting dimension034P

Hausdorff measure allows covers by sets of very different sizes, and this is what makes it a measure. If instead we cover a set by sets of one fixed size \(\delta \) and only count them, we get a simpler quantity. A segment of length \(1\) needs about \(1/\delta \) sets of diameter \(\delta \), a square about \(\delta ^{-2}\), a cube about \(\delta ^{-3}\): the exponent is the dimension.

034Q Definition 28.18.1

For a nonempty bounded \(A \subseteq \mathbb {R}^n\) and \(\delta {\gt} 0\), let \(N_\delta (A)\) be the smallest number of sets of diameter at most \(\delta \) that cover \(A\). The lower and upper box-counting dimensions of \(A\) are

\[ \underline{\dim }_BA = \liminf _{\delta \to 0}\frac{\log N_\delta (A)}{-\log \delta }, \qquad \overline{\dim }_BA = \limsup _{\delta \to 0}\frac{\log N_\delta (A)}{-\log \delta }. \]

If they are equal, their common value is the box-counting dimension \(\dim _BA\).

The number \(N_\delta (A)\) is finite: \(A\) lies in a cube, which is a finite union of small cubes. The definition says that \(N_\delta (A)\) is roughly \(\delta ^{-\dim _BA}\); more precisely, \(\overline{\dim }_BA {\lt} t\) if and only if \(N_\delta (A) \leq \delta ^{-t^{\prime }}\) for some \(t^{\prime } {\lt} t\) and all small \(\delta \).

034R Proposition 28.18.2

Let \(A, A_1, A_2, \ldots \) be subsets of \(\mathbb {R}^n\).

  1. (Countable stability) \(\dim _{\mathcal{H}}\bigcup _kA_k = \sup _k\dim _{\mathcal{H}}A_k\). In particular countable sets have Hausdorff dimension \(0\).

  2. If \(A\) is nonempty and bounded, \(N_\delta (A) = N_\delta (\overline A)\), so \(A\) and its closure have the same box-counting dimensions.

  3. If \(A\) is nonempty and bounded, \(\dim _{\mathcal{H}}A \leq \underline{\dim }_BA \leq \overline{\dim }_BA \leq n\).

Proof â–¼

(1) Since \(\mathcal{H}^s\) is monotone, \(\dim _{\mathcal{H}}A_k \leq \dim _{\mathcal{H}}\bigcup _kA_k\) for every \(k\). Conversely, if \(s {\gt} \sup _k\dim _{\mathcal{H}}A_k\), then \(\mathcal{H}^s(A_k) = 0\) for every \(k\), and \(\mathcal{H}^s(\bigcup _kA_k) = 0\) by countable subadditivity; so \(\dim _{\mathcal{H}}\bigcup _kA_k \leq s\). A point has \(\mathcal{H}^s\)-measure \(0\) for every \(s {\gt} 0\), since it is covered by one set of diameter \(0\); so a point has dimension \(0\), and so has a countable set.

(2) A cover of \(A\) by sets of diameter at most \(\delta \) gives the cover of \(\overline A\) by their closures, which have the same diameters, since a finite union of closed sets is closed. So \(N_\delta (\overline A) \leq N_\delta (A)\), and the other inequality holds as \(A \subseteq \overline A\).

(3) The middle inequality is clear. For the last one, let \(A\) lie in a cube of side \(\ell \). Dividing it into \(m^n\) cubes of side \(\ell /m\), of diameter \(\ell \sqrt n/m\), with \(m\) the smallest integer \(\geq \ell \sqrt n/\delta \), gives \(N_\delta (A) \leq m^n \leq (\ell \sqrt n/\delta + 1)^n\), and \(\log N_\delta (A)/(-\log \delta ) \leq n\log (\ell \sqrt n + \delta )/(-\log \delta ) + n\), whose \(\limsup \) as \(\delta \to 0\) is \(n\). For the first inequality, let \(s {\gt} t {\gt} \underline{\dim }_BA\). There are \(\delta _j \to 0\) with \(\log N_{\delta _j}(A) {\lt} t(-\log \delta _j)\), that is, \(N_{\delta _j}(A) {\lt} \delta _j^{-t}\). Covering \(A\) by \(N_{\delta _j}(A)\) sets of diameter at most \(\delta _j\) gives

\[ \mathcal{H}^s_{\delta _j}(A) \leq \alpha (s)N_{\delta _j}(A)\delta _j^s \leq \alpha (s)\delta _j^{s - t} \to 0 . \]

As \(\mathcal{H}^s_\delta (A)\) increases when \(\delta \) decreases, \(\mathcal{H}^s(A) = \lim _j\mathcal{H}^s_{\delta _j}(A) = 0\), so \(\dim _{\mathcal{H}}A \leq s\). Since \(s {\gt} \underline{\dim }_BA\) was arbitrary, the inequality follows.

By (2), box-counting dimension cannot see the difference between a set and its closure: \(\mathbb {Q}\cap [0, 1]\) has box-counting dimension \(1\), like \([0, 1]\), while its Hausdorff dimension is \(0\) by (1). So box-counting dimension is not countably stable, and in general it is not attached to a measure. The inequality in (3) can be strict even for compact sets.

034S Example 28.18.3

A compact countable set of box-counting dimension \(\frac12\). Let \(E = \{ 0\} \cup \{ 1/m : m \geq 1\} \). It is countable, so \(\dim _{\mathcal{H}}E = 0\). We show that \(\dim _BE = \frac12\). Let \(0 {\lt} \delta {\lt} 1\).

  1. Lower bound. Let \(m = \lfloor \delta ^{-1/2}\rfloor \geq 1\). For \(2 \leq j \leq m\) the consecutive points \(1/(j-1)\) and \(1/j\) are at distance \(\frac1{j(j-1)} {\gt} \frac1{m^2} \geq \delta \). So the points \(1, \frac12, \ldots , \frac1m\) are pairwise more than \(\delta \) apart, a set of diameter at most \(\delta \) contains at most one of them, and \(N_\delta (E) \geq m {\gt} \delta ^{-1/2} - 1\).

  2. Upper bound. Let \(m = \lceil \delta ^{-1/2}\rceil \). The interval \([0, \frac1m]\), which contains all points \(1/j\) with \(j \geq m\), is covered by \(\lceil \frac1{m\delta }\rceil \) intervals of length \(\delta \), and the remaining \(m - 1\) points are covered by singletons. Since \(m - 1 {\lt} \delta ^{-1/2}\) and \(\frac1{m\delta } \leq \delta ^{-1/2}\), \(N_\delta (E) \leq 2\delta ^{-1/2} + 1 \leq 3\delta ^{-1/2}\).

Taking logarithms, \(\log (\delta ^{-1/2} - 1)/(-\log \delta )\) and \((\log 3 + \frac12(-\log \delta ))/(-\log \delta )\) both tend to \(\frac12\) as \(\delta \to 0\). The reason is that the points \(1/j\) are spread out for \(j\) small and crowded for \(j\) large: at scale \(\delta \) one sees about \(\delta ^{-1/2}\) separate points, and the rest look like an interval of length about \(\delta ^{1/2}\).

28.18.2 Iterated function systems034T

A map \(f \colon \mathbb {R}^n \to \mathbb {R}^n\) is a similarity with ratio \(r {\gt} 0\) if \(|f(x) - f(y)| = r|x - y|\) for all \(x, y\); for example \(f(x) = rOx + b\) with \(O\) orthogonal (every similarity has this form, but we do not need this). A similarity with ratio \(r\) multiplies diameters by \(r\), and by Proposition 28.17.3(3), applied to \(f\) and to its inverse, \(\mathcal{H}^s(f(A)) = r^s\mathcal{H}^s(A)\) for every \(A\) and \(s\).

034U Definition 28.18.4

An iterated function system (of similarities) is a finite family \(f_1, \ldots , f_N\) of similarities of \(\mathbb {R}^n\) with ratios \(r_1, \ldots , r_N \in (0, 1)\), where \(N \geq 2\). For a set \(A\) write \(F(A) = f_1(A) \cup \cdots \cup f_N(A)\). A nonempty compact set \(K\) with \(F(K) = K\) is called the attractor of the system, or a self-similar set.

It is convenient to use words in the alphabet \(\{ 1, \ldots , N\} \). For a word \(w = w_1 \cdots w_k\) of length \(|w| = k\) let

\[ f_w = f_{w_1} \circ f_{w_2} \circ \cdots \circ f_{w_k}, \qquad r_w = r_{w_1}r_{w_2} \cdots r_{w_k}, \]

so that \(f_w\) is a similarity with ratio \(r_w\); for the empty word \(\varnothing \), \(f_\varnothing \) is the identity and \(r_\varnothing = 1\). Write \(wi\) for the word \(w\) followed by the letter \(i\), and \(r = \max _ir_i {\lt} 1\), so that \(r_w \leq r^{|w|}\). For an infinite sequence \(\omega = \omega _1\omega _2\cdots \) of letters, \(\omega |k = \omega _1 \cdots \omega _k\) is its beginning of length \(k\). Since \(F(A) = \bigcup _if_i(A)\), induction on \(k\) gives \(F^k(A) = \bigcup _{|w| = k}f_w(A)\).

034V Theorem 28.18.5 Hutchinson

Every iterated function system has a unique attractor \(K\). Moreover:

  1. If \(B\) is a nonempty compact set with \(F(B) \subseteq B\), then \(K = \bigcap _kF^k(B)\); in particular \(K \subseteq B\).

  2. Let \(K_w = f_w(K)\). Then \(K = \bigcup _{|w| = k}K_w\) for every \(k\), and \(K_w = \bigcup _iK_{wi}\), with \(\operatorname {diam}K_w = r_w\operatorname {diam}K\).

  3. Every \(x \in K\) has an address: an infinite sequence \(\omega \) with \(x \in K_{\omega |k}\) for all \(k\). For every infinite sequence \(\omega \), \(\bigcap _kK_{\omega |k}\) is a single point.

Proof â–¼

Existence, and (1). First, a compact set \(B\) with \(F(B) \subseteq B\) exists: let \(R = \max _i|f_i(0)|/(1 - r)\) and let \(B\) be the closed ball of radius \(R\) about \(0\). For \(|x| \leq R\), \(|f_i(x)| \leq |f_i(x) - f_i(0)| + |f_i(0)| \leq rR + (1 - r)R = R\). Now let \(B\) be any nonempty compact set with \(F(B) \subseteq B\). Applying \(F\), which preserves inclusions, gives \(B \supseteq F(B) \supseteq F^2(B) \supseteq \cdots \), a decreasing sequence of nonempty compact sets (finite unions of continuous images of compact sets). Their intersection \(K\) is nonempty and compact. To see \(F(K) = K\), note that \(f_i(K) = \bigcap _kf_i(F^k(B))\) since \(f_i\) is injective, so

\[ F(K) = \bigcup _i\bigcap _kf_i(F^k(B)) \qquad \text{and}\qquad K = \bigcap _kF^{k+1}(B) = \bigcap _k\bigcup _if_i(F^k(B)). \]

The first set is contained in the second. Conversely, if \(x\) lies in \(\bigcup _if_i(F^k(B))\) for every \(k\), then there is one \(i\) with \(x \in f_i(F^k(B))\) for infinitely many \(k\), and so for all \(k\), as these sets decrease with \(k\). So the two sets are equal.

Uniqueness. Let \(A\) and \(K\) be nonempty compact sets with \(F(A) = A\) and \(F(K) = K\), and \(D = \max \{ |a - y| : a \in A, y \in K\} \). Fix \(k\). Since \(A = F^k(A)\), every \(a \in A\) is \(f_w(a^{\prime })\) for a word \(w\) of length \(k\) and some \(a^{\prime } \in A\). For any \(y \in K\), the point \(f_w(y)\) lies in \(F^k(K) = K\), and \(|a - f_w(y)| = r_w|a^{\prime } - y| \leq r^kD\). So every point of \(A\) is within \(r^kD\) of \(K\), for every \(k\); as \(K\) is closed, \(A \subseteq K\). By symmetry \(K \subseteq A\).

(2) The first statement is \(K = F^k(K)\), the second is \(f_w(K) = f_w(F(K)) = \bigcup _if_w(f_i(K))\), and the third holds because \(f_w\) multiplies distances by \(r_w\).

(3) Since \(K = \bigcup _if_i(K)\), a point \(x \in K\) can be written \(x = f_{\omega _1}(x_1)\) with \(x_1 \in K\); then \(x_1 = f_{\omega _2}(x_2)\) with \(x_2 \in K\), and so on. Then \(x = f_{\omega |k}(x_k) \in K_{\omega |k}\) for all \(k\). For any \(\omega \), the sets \(K_{\omega |k}\) are nonempty, compact, decreasing by (2), and of diameter at most \(r^k\operatorname {diam}K \to 0\); so their intersection is one point.

034W Remark 28.18.6

Hutchinson’s original proof [Hut81] is an application of the Banach fixed point theorem. The nonempty compact subsets of \(\mathbb {R}^n\) form a complete metric space for the Hausdorff distance \(d_H(A, B) = \inf \{ \varepsilon {\gt} 0 : A \subseteq B_\varepsilon , B \subseteq A_\varepsilon \} \), where \(A_\varepsilon \) is the set of points at distance less than \(\varepsilon \) from \(A\), and \(F\) is a contraction with constant \(r\) for this distance; the attractor is its fixed point, and \(F^k(B) \to K\) for every nonempty compact \(B\). This also explains the name: starting from any picture \(B\), repeatedly replacing it by \(N\) smaller copies of itself converges to \(K\). The uniqueness proof above is the contraction argument in disguise.

034X Example 28.18.7

  1. The Cantor set. \(f_1(x) = x/3\), \(f_2(x) = x/3 + 2/3\) on \(\mathbb {R}\). The Cantor set \(C\), the set of \(\sum _ka_k3^{-k}\) with all \(a_k \in \{ 0, 2\} \), satisfies \(f_1(C) = \{ a_1 = 0\} \) and \(f_2(C) = \{ a_1 = 2\} \), so \(F(C) = C\) and \(C\) is the attractor. The sets \(C_w\) are the parts of \(C\) in the intervals \(I_w\) of Example 28.17.6, and the address of a point is its sequence of ternary digits, with \(1\) and \(2\) standing for the digits \(0\) and \(2\).

  2. An interval. \(f_1(x) = x/2\), \(f_2(x) = x/2 + 1/2\). Here \(F([0, 1]) = [0, 1]\), so the attractor is \([0, 1]\); addresses are binary expansions, and the dyadic rationals have two addresses.

  3. The Sierpiński gasket. Let \(p_1, p_2, p_3\) be the vertices of a triangle \(T\) in \(\mathbb {R}^2\) and \(f_i(x) = (x + p_i)/2\). Then \(f_i(T)\) is the triangle with vertex \(p_i\) and the midpoints of the two sides through \(p_i\), so \(F(T) \subseteq T\) and the attractor is \(S = \bigcap _kF^k(T)\) by (1): at each step every triangle is replaced by its three corner triangles, and the open middle one is removed.

  4. The Koch curve. In \(\mathbb {R}^2 = \mathbb {C}\) let \(\zeta = e^{i\pi /3}\) and

    \[ f_1(z) = \tfrac z3, \quad f_2(z) = \tfrac {\zeta z + 1}{3}, \quad f_3(z) = \tfrac {\bar{\zeta }z}{3} + \tfrac 12 + \tfrac {i\sqrt3}{6}, \quad f_4(z) = \tfrac {z + 2}{3}. \]

    They map the segment \([0, 1]\) to the four sides of the polygon \(0, \frac13, \frac12 + \frac{i\sqrt3}{6}, \frac23, 1\), whose middle part is the tip of an equilateral triangle erected on the middle third of \([0, 1]\): for instance \(f_2(1) = \frac{1 + \zeta }{3} = \frac12 + \frac{i\sqrt3}{6} = f_3(0)\) and \(f_3(1) = \frac{\bar{\zeta }}{3} + \frac12 + \frac{i\sqrt3}{6} = \frac23 = f_4(0)\). The attractor is the limit of the polygons \(F^k([0, 1])\), in the sense of Remark 28.18.6.

  5. The four-corner Cantor set. For \(a \in \{ 0, \frac34\} ^2\) let \(f_a(x) = \frac x4 + a\) on \(\mathbb {R}^2\). The four squares \(f_a([0, 1]^2)\) are the corner squares of side \(\frac14\) of the unit square, so the attractor \(K_4 \subseteq [0, 1]^2\) is obtained by repeatedly replacing every square by its four corner squares of a quarter of the size. It contains the fixed points \((0, 0)\) and \((1, 1)\) of \(f_{(0, 0)}\) and \(f_{(3/4, 3/4)}\), so \(\operatorname {diam}K_4 = \sqrt2\).

28.18.3 The similarity dimension034Y

The function \(s \mapsto \sum _ir_i^s\) is continuous and strictly decreasing on \([0, \infty )\), equal to \(N \geq 2\) at \(s = 0\) and tending to \(0\) as \(s \to \infty \). So there is a unique \(s {\gt} 0\) with

\begin{equation} r_1^s + r_2^s + \cdots + r_N^s = 1, \end{equation}
28.2

the similarity dimension of the system. If all ratios equal \(\rho \), then \(N\rho ^s = 1\) and \(s = \log N/\log (1/\rho )\): for the Cantor set \(s = \log 2/\log 3\), for the interval of Example 28.18.7(2) \(s = 1\), for the Sierpiński gasket \(s = \log 3/\log 2 \approx 1.585\), for the Koch curve \(s = \log 4/\log 3 \approx 1.262\), and for the four-corner set \(s = \log 4/\log 4 = 1\). To cover \(K\) efficiently by the pieces \(K_w\) one should use pieces of comparable sizes, not of equal word length; the following lemma organises this.

0350 Lemma 28.18.8

For \(0 {\lt} \rho {\lt} 1\) let \(Q_\rho \) be the set of nonempty words \(w\) with \(r_w \leq \rho {\lt} r_{w^-}\), where \(w^-\) is \(w\) with its last letter removed. Then every infinite sequence \(\omega \) has exactly one beginning \(\omega |k\) in \(Q_\rho \), the set \(Q_\rho \) is finite, and

\[ \sum _{w \in Q_\rho }r_w^s = 1, \qquad \# Q_\rho \leq (\rho \, r_{\min })^{-s}, \qquad K = \bigcup _{w \in Q_\rho }K_w, \]

where \(s\) is the similarity dimension and \(r_{\min } = \min _ir_i\).

Proof â–¼

Along \(\omega \) the numbers \(r_{\omega |k}\) decrease strictly from \(r_{\omega |0} = 1 {\gt} \rho \) to \(0\), so there is exactly one \(k \geq 1\) with \(r_{\omega |k} \leq \rho {\lt} r_{\omega |(k - 1)}\). Let \(k_0\) be such that \(r^{k_0} \leq \rho \). Every word \(w\) of length at least \(k_0 + 1\) has \(r_{w^-} \leq r^{k_0} \leq \rho \), so it is not in \(Q_\rho \); hence \(Q_\rho \) is finite. Every word \(v\) of length \(k_0\) has \(r_v \leq \rho \), so, applying the first statement to any infinite sequence beginning with \(v\), \(v\) has exactly one beginning in \(Q_\rho \). Grouping the words of length \(k_0\) according to this beginning, and using that the words of length \(k_0\) extending a given \(w\) are the \(wu\) with \(|u| = k_0 - |w|\),

\[ 1 = \Bigl(\sum _ir_i^s\Bigr)^{k_0} = \sum _{|v| = k_0}r_v^s = \sum _{w \in Q_\rho }\ \sum _{|u| = k_0 - |w|}r_w^sr_u^s = \sum _{w \in Q_\rho }r_w^s\Bigl(\sum _ir_i^s\Bigr)^{k_0 - |w|} = \sum _{w \in Q_\rho }r_w^s . \]

Each \(w \in Q_\rho \) has \(r_w = r_{w^-}r_{w_{|w|}} {\gt} \rho \, r_{\min }\), so \(1 \geq \# Q_\rho \, (\rho \, r_{\min })^s\). Finally, if \(x \in K\) has address \(\omega \) and \(\omega |k \in Q_\rho \), then \(x \in K_{\omega |k}\).

0351 Proposition 28.18.9

Let \(K\) be the attractor of an iterated function system with similarity dimension \(s\). Then \(\mathcal{H}^s(K) \leq \alpha (s)(\operatorname {diam}K)^s\), and \(\dim _{\mathcal{H}}K \leq \overline{\dim }_BK \leq s\).

Proof â–¼

We may assume \(d = \operatorname {diam}K {\gt} 0\). For \(0 {\lt} \rho {\lt} 1\) the sets \(K_w\), \(w \in Q_\rho \), cover \(K\) by Lemma 28.18.8 and have diameter \(r_wd \leq \rho d\), so

\[ \mathcal{H}^s_{\rho d}(K) \leq \sum _{w \in Q_\rho }\alpha (s)(r_wd)^s = \alpha (s)d^s, \]

and \(\rho \to 0\) gives the first claim. With \(\delta = \rho d\) they also show \(N_\delta (K) \leq \# Q_\rho \leq (r_{\min }\delta /d)^{-s}\) for \(0 {\lt} \delta {\lt} d\), so \(\log N_\delta (K)/(-\log \delta ) \leq s + s\log (d/r_{\min })/(-\log \delta )\), whose \(\limsup \) is \(s\). The remaining inequality is Proposition 28.18.2(3).

The upper bound can be far from the truth when the pieces overlap: for \(f_1(x) = f_2(x) = x/2\) the attractor is the point \(\{ 0\} \), while \(s = 1\). The lower bound needs a condition which prevents such overlaps. The simplest one is that the pieces are disjoint.

0352 Theorem 28.18.10

Let \(K\) be the attractor of an iterated function system with similarity dimension \(s\), and suppose that the sets \(f_1(K), \ldots , f_N(K)\) are pairwise disjoint (the strong separation condition). Then

\[ \alpha (s)(r_{\min }\eta )^s \leq \mathcal{H}^s(K) \leq \alpha (s)(\operatorname {diam}K)^s, \qquad \eta = \min _{i \neq j}d(f_i(K), f_j(K)) {\gt} 0, \]

and \(\dim _{\mathcal{H}}K = \dim _BK = s\).

Proof â–¼

Step 1: separation at all scales. \(\eta {\gt} 0\) because the \(f_i(K)\) are disjoint compact sets. For a word \(w\) and \(i \neq j\), \(f_w\) multiplies distances by \(r_w\), so \(d(K_{wi}, K_{wj}) = r_wd(f_i(K), f_j(K)) \geq r_w\eta \). Let \(x, y \in K\) with addresses \(\omega \) and \(\omega ^{\prime }\), and suppose these first differ at position \(j\); let \(u = \omega |(j - 1)\). Then \(x \in K_{u\omega _j}\) and \(y \in K_{u\omega ^{\prime }_j}\), so

\begin{equation} |x - y| \geq r_{\omega |(j - 1)}\, \eta . \end{equation}
28.3

In particular, for each \(k\) the \(N^k\) sets \(K_w\) with \(|w| = k\) are pairwise disjoint, and each of them is open in \(K\), its complement in \(K\) being the finite union of the others, which is compact.

Step 2: the natural measure. Put \(p_w = r_w^s\), so that \(\sum _ip_{wi} = p_w\sum _ir_i^s = p_w\), and by induction \(\sum _{|u| = l}p_{wu} = p_w\) for every \(l\). Fix \(x_0 \in K\), put \(x_w = f_w(x_0) \in K_w\), and for \(\varphi \in C(K)\) let

\[ S_k(\varphi ) = \sum _{|w| = k}p_w\, \varphi (x_w) . \]

Let \(\omega _\varphi (t) = \sup \{ |\varphi (x) - \varphi (y)| : |x - y| \leq t\} \), which tends to \(0\) as \(t \to 0\) since \(\varphi \) is uniformly continuous on the compact set \(K\). For \(l {\gt} k\), regrouping the words of length \(l\) by their beginnings of length \(k\),

\[ |S_l(\varphi ) - S_k(\varphi )| = \Bigl|\sum _{|w| = k}\sum _{|u| = l - k}p_{wu}\bigl(\varphi (x_{wu}) - \varphi (x_w)\bigr)\Bigr| \leq \omega _\varphi (r^k\operatorname {diam}K)\sum _{|w| = k}p_w = \omega _\varphi (r^k\operatorname {diam}K), \]

since \(x_{wu}\) and \(x_w\) both lie in \(K_w\). So \((S_k(\varphi ))_k\) is a Cauchy sequence, and \(\Lambda (\varphi ) = \lim _kS_k(\varphi )\) is a positive linear functional on \(C(K) = C_c(K)\) with \(\Lambda (1) = 1\). By the Riesz–Markov theorem (Theorem 28.14.6) there is a Borel probability measure \(\mu \) on \(K\) with \(\int \varphi \, d\mu = \Lambda (\varphi )\). By Step 1 the indicator function \(\mathbf{1}_{K_w}\) is continuous on \(K\), and for \(l \geq k = |w|\) the points \(x_v\) with \(|v| = l\) lying in \(K_w\) are those with \(v\) beginning with \(w\). Hence

\[ \mu (K_w) = \lim _lS_l(\mathbf{1}_{K_w}) = \sum _{|u| = l - k}p_{wu} = p_w = r_w^s . \]

Step 3: the mass of small sets. Let \(U\) be a closed set with \(d = \operatorname {diam}U {\lt} \eta \); we show \(\mu (U \cap K) \leq (d/(r_{\min }\eta ))^s\). This is clear if \(U \cap K = \emptyset \). Let \(x \in U \cap K\) have address \(\omega \). If \(d = 0\), then \(\mu (U \cap K) = \mu (\{ x\} ) \leq \mu (K_{\omega |k}) = r_{\omega |k}^s \to 0\). If \(d {\gt} 0\), the numbers \(r_{\omega |k}\eta \) decrease from \(\eta {\gt} d\) to \(0\), so there is a least \(k \geq 1\) with \(r_{\omega |k}\eta \leq d\), and \(r_{\omega |(k - 1)}\eta {\gt} d\). Every \(y \in U \cap K\) lies in \(K_{\omega |(k - 1)}\): otherwise an address of \(y\) first differs from \(\omega \) at a position \(j \leq k - 1\), and 28.3 gives \(|x - y| \geq r_{\omega |(j - 1)}\eta \geq r_{\omega |(k - 1)}\eta {\gt} d\), which is impossible. Therefore

\[ \mu (U \cap K) \leq \mu (K_{\omega |(k - 1)}) = r_{\omega |(k - 1)}^s \leq \Bigl(\frac{d}{r_{\min }\eta }\Bigr)^s, \]

the last step because \(d \geq r_{\omega |k}\eta = r_{\omega |(k - 1)}r_{\omega _k}\eta \geq r_{\omega |(k - 1)}r_{\min }\eta \).

Step 4: conclusion. Let \(\delta {\lt} \eta \) and let \((U_i)\) be a cover of \(K\) by closed sets of diameter at most \(\delta \); closed covers suffice to compute \(\mathcal{H}^s_\delta \). By Step 3,

\[ 1 = \mu (K) \leq \sum _i\mu (U_i \cap K) \leq (r_{\min }\eta )^{-s}\sum _i(\operatorname {diam}U_i)^s, \]

so \(\mathcal{H}^s_\delta (K) \geq \alpha (s)(r_{\min }\eta )^s\), and the lower bound follows. With Proposition 28.18.9, \(0 {\lt} \mathcal{H}^s(K) {\lt} \infty \), so \(\dim _{\mathcal{H}}K = s\), and \(s = \dim _{\mathcal{H}}K \leq \underline{\dim }_BK \leq \overline{\dim }_BK \leq s\) by Proposition 28.18.2(3).

Step 3 is the mass distribution principle of Example 28.17.6 again; the new ingredient is that the measure is built for us by the Riesz–Markov theorem, once we know what it should give to the pieces \(K_w\). The measure \(\mu \) is in fact the normalised restriction of \(\mathcal{H}^s\) to \(K\) (Exercise 28.23.20).

0354 Example 28.18.11

  1. The Cantor set. \(f_1(C) \subseteq [0, \frac13]\) and \(f_2(C) \subseteq [\frac23, 1]\) are disjoint, at distance \(\eta = \frac13\), the distance between the points \(\frac13\) and \(\frac23\) of \(C\). The theorem gives \(\dim _{\mathcal{H}}C = \dim _BC = \log 2/\log 3\) and \(\alpha (s)/4 \leq \mathcal{H}^s(C) \leq \alpha (s)\), as \((r_{\min }\eta )^s = 9^{-s} = \frac14\); the direct argument of Example 28.17.6 gave the better lower bound \(\alpha (s)/2\). In fact \(\mathcal{H}^s(C) = \alpha (s)\) [Fal90], but this requires a finer argument.

  2. A totally disconnected set of dimension \(1\). For the four-corner set \(K_4\) of Example 28.18.7(5) the four pieces lie in the four corner squares of side \(\frac14\), which are pairwise at distance at least \(\frac12\). So \(\dim _{\mathcal{H}}K_4 = 1\) and \(0 {\lt} \mathcal{H}^1(K_4) \leq \alpha (1)\sqrt2 = \sqrt2\) (note \(\alpha (1) = 1\)). Yet \(K_4\) contains no segment, indeed no connected set with more than one point: the sets \((K_4)_w\) are open and closed in \(K_4\) by Step 1, and every point has such neighbourhoods of arbitrarily small diameter. So \(K_4\) has the dimension and the finite positive length of a curve, but none of its geometry; Example 28.18.12 and Section 28.22 return to this.

  3. Products. \(C \times C \subseteq \mathbb {R}^2\) is the attractor of the four maps \((x, y) \mapsto (x/3 + a, y/3 + b)\) with \(a, b \in \{ 0, \frac23\} \) (by uniqueness, since \(C \times C\) is compact and invariant), whose images lie in the four corner squares of side \(\frac13\). So \(\dim _{\mathcal{H}}(C \times C) = \log 4/\log 3 = 2\dim _{\mathcal{H}}C\).

  4. Middle-\(\lambda \) Cantor sets. Removing open middle intervals of relative length \(\lambda \in (0, 1)\) instead of \(\frac13\) gives the attractor of \(x \mapsto \rho x\) and \(x \mapsto \rho x + 1 - \rho \) with \(\rho = (1 - \lambda )/2\), of dimension \(\log 2/\log (2/(1 - \lambda ))\). As \(\lambda \) runs through \((0, 1)\) this takes every value in \((0, 1)\).

0355 Example 28.18.12

Shadows of the four-corner set. Let \(K_4\) be as in Example 28.18.7(5). Its projection \(\pi _1(x, y) = x\) to the first axis satisfies \(\pi _1 \circ f_a = g_{a_1} \circ \pi _1\) with \(g_0(t) = t/4\) and \(g_{3/4}(t) = t/4 + \frac34\). So \(\pi _1(K_4)\) is a nonempty compact set with \(\pi _1(K_4) = g_0(\pi _1(K_4)) \cup g_{3/4}(\pi _1(K_4))\), the attractor of \(\{ g_0, g_{3/4}\} \), whose pieces lie in \([0, \frac14]\) and \([\frac34, 1]\). By Theorem 28.18.10 it has dimension \(\log 2/\log 4 = \frac12\), hence length \(0\). Now project instead by \(\pi (x, y) = x + y/2\). Then \(\pi \circ f_a = h_a \circ \pi \) with \(h_a(t) = t/4 + \pi (a)\), and \(\pi (a)\) runs through \(0, \frac38, \frac34, \frac98\) as \(a\) runs through \((0, 0), (0, \frac34), (\frac34, 0), (\frac34, \frac34)\). The interval \([0, \frac32]\) satisfies

\[ \bigcup _ah_a\bigl([0, \tfrac 32]\bigr) = [0, \tfrac 38] \cup [\tfrac 38, \tfrac 34] \cup [\tfrac 34, \tfrac 98] \cup [\tfrac 98, \tfrac 32] = [0, \tfrac 32], \]

so by uniqueness of attractors \(\pi (K_4) = [0, \frac32]\). The orthogonal projection onto the line spanned by \((2, 1)\) is \(\frac{2}{\sqrt5}\pi \) up to an isometry of the line, so the shadow of \(K_4\) in this direction has length \(\frac{3}{\sqrt5}\), while in the direction of the first axis it has length \(0\). As orthogonal projections are \(1\)-Lipschitz, this also gives

\[ \frac{3}{\sqrt5} \leq \mathcal{H}^1(K_4) \leq \sqrt2, \]

that is, \(1.341\ldots \leq \mathcal{H}^1(K_4) \leq 1.414\ldots \), a much better lower bound than Theorem 28.18.10 gives.

0356 Remark 28.18.13

Touching pieces. For the Sierpiński gasket and the Koch curve the pieces \(f_i(K)\) touch, so Theorem 28.18.10 does not apply, and Proposition 28.18.9 only gives the upper bounds \(\log 3/\log 2\) and \(\log 4/\log 3\) for their dimensions. Equality holds under the weaker open set condition: there is a nonempty bounded open set \(V\) with \(f_i(V) \subseteq V\) and the \(f_i(V)\) pairwise disjoint (for the gasket, the open triangle; for the Koch curve, the open isosceles triangle with base \([0, 1]\) and apex \(\frac12 + \frac{i\sqrt3}{6}\)). Under this condition \(0 {\lt} \mathcal{H}^s(K) {\lt} \infty \) and \(\dim _{\mathcal{H}}K = \dim _BK = s\) [Hut81], [Fal90, Chapter 9]. The proof follows the same plan: the natural measure exists without any condition, and the open set condition replaces 28.3 by a volume argument showing that a ball of radius \(\rho \) meets a bounded number of pieces \(K_w\), \(w \in Q_\rho \).
