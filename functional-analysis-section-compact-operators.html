29.5 Compact operators015Q

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

015R Definition 29.5.1

\(T \in B(X, Y)\) is compact if the closure of \(T(B(0,1))\) is compact; equivalently, every bounded sequence \((x_n)\) has a subsequence with \((Tx_{n_k})\) convergent.

015S Proposition 29.5.2

  1. Compact operators form a closed subspace of \(B(X, Y)\), and \(STR\) is compact if \(T\) is compact and \(S\), \(R\) are bounded.

  2. Operators of finite rank are compact; on Hilbert spaces, \(T\) is compact if and only if it is a norm limit of finite rank operators.

  3. If \(H\) is a Hilbert space, \(T \in B(H)\) is compact if and only if \(T^*\) is.

Proof â–¼

(1) Sums and scalar multiples are clear, and compositions with bounded operators map bounded sets to bounded sets. If \(T_n \to T\) with \(T_n\) compact, then \(T(B)\) is totally bounded: given \(\varepsilon \), choose \(n\) with \(\| T - T_n\| {\lt} \varepsilon \) and cover \(T_n(B)\) by finitely many \(\varepsilon \)-balls; so \(\overline{T(B)}\) is compact (Theorem 17.7.4). (2) A bounded set in a finite-dimensional space is relatively compact (Theorem 17.6.4). Conversely let \(T\) be compact and \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\); cover \(\overline{T(B)}\) by finitely many balls \(B(y_i, \varepsilon )\), let \(P\) be the orthogonal projection onto the span of the \(y_i\) (Theorem 29.3.3); then \(\| Tx - PTx\| \leq \| Tx - y_i\| {\lt} \varepsilon \) for a suitable \(i\), since \(P\) is a projection and \(\| Tx - PTx\| = \operatorname {dist}(Tx, \operatorname {im}P)\). So \(\| T - PT\| \leq \varepsilon \) with \(PT\) of finite rank. (3) follows from (2), since \((PT)^* = T^*P\) has finite rank and \(\| T^* - (PT)^*\| = \| T - PT\| \).

015T Theorem 29.5.3 Spectral theorem for compact self-adjoint operators

Let \(H \neq 0\) be a Hilbert space and \(T \in B(H)\) compact and self-adjoint (\(T = T^*\)).

  1. \(\| T\| = \sup _{\| x\| = 1}|\langle Tx, x \rangle |\), and \(\| T\| \) or \(-\| T\| \) is an eigenvalue of \(T\).

  2. \(H\) has an orthonormal basis consisting of eigenvectors of \(T\). The nonzero eigenvalues are real, form a finite or countable set with \(0\) as the only possible accumulation point, and each nonzero eigenvalue has finite-dimensional eigenspace.

Proof â–¼

(1) Let \(s = \sup _{\| x\| =1}|\langle Tx, x \rangle | \leq \| T\| \). For \(x, y\) with \(\| x\| , \| y\| \leq 1\), the identity \(\langle T(x + y), x + y \rangle - \langle T(x - y), x - y \rangle = 4\operatorname {Re}\langle Tx, y \rangle \) (using self-adjointness) and the parallelogram law give \(4\operatorname {Re}\langle Tx, y \rangle \leq s(\| x + y\| ^2 + \| x - y\| ^2) = 2s(\| x\| ^2 + \| y\| ^2) \leq 4s\), so \(\| T\| = \sup |\langle Tx, y \rangle | \leq s\). Choose unit vectors \(x_n\) with \(\langle Tx_n, x_n \rangle \to \lambda \) where \(|\lambda | = \| T\| \) (note \(\langle Tx, x \rangle \) is real). Then \(\| Tx_n - \lambda x_n\| ^2 = \| Tx_n\| ^2 - 2\lambda \langle Tx_n, x_n \rangle + \lambda ^2 \leq 2\lambda ^2 - 2\lambda \langle Tx_n, x_n \rangle \to 0\). By compactness, after passing to a subsequence \(Tx_n \to y\); if \(\lambda \neq 0\) then \(x_n \to y/\lambda \), so \(y/\lambda \) is a unit vector with \(Ty/\lambda = \lambda y/\lambda \). If \(\lambda = 0\) then \(T = 0\) and every vector is an eigenvector.

(2) Eigenvalues are real and eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal, as in finite dimensions. If \(|\lambda | \geq \varepsilon {\gt} 0\), an orthonormal family in the \(\lambda \)-eigenspace is mapped by \(T\) to a family of vectors at mutual distance \(|\lambda |\sqrt2\), so it must be finite by compactness; the same argument shows that only finitely many eigenvalues satisfy \(|\lambda | \geq \varepsilon \). Let \(M\) be the closed span of all eigenvectors. It is \(T\)-invariant, hence so is \(M^\perp \) (if \(x \in M^\perp \) and \(m \in M\), \(\langle Tx, m \rangle = \langle x, Tm \rangle = 0\)), and \(T|_{M^\perp }\) is compact and self-adjoint; by (1) it has an eigenvector unless \(M^\perp = 0\) or \(T|_{M^\perp } = 0\). An eigenvector in \(M^\perp \) is impossible, and if \(T|_{M^\perp } = 0\) then \(M^\perp \) consists of eigenvectors for the eigenvalue \(0\), so \(M^\perp \subseteq M\) and \(M^\perp = 0\). Hence \(M = H\), and an orthonormal basis of each eigenspace, together, is an orthonormal basis of \(H\) by Theorem 29.3.5.

015U Theorem 29.5.4 Fredholm alternative

Let \(H\) be a Hilbert space, \(K \in B(H)\) compact and \(T = I - K\).

  1. \(\ker T\) is finite-dimensional and \(\operatorname {im}T\) is closed, with \(\operatorname {im}T = (\ker T^*)^\perp \).

  2. \(T\) is injective if and only if it is surjective.

  3. \(\dim \ker T = \dim \ker T^*\); so the equation \(Tx = y\) is solvable if and only if \(y \perp \ker T^*\), and the solution is unique up to \(\ker T\), which has the same finite dimension as \(\ker T^*\).

Proof â–¼

(1) On \(\ker T\) we have \(K = I\), so the unit ball of \(\ker T\) is compact and \(\ker T\) is finite-dimensional (Proposition 29.2.2(3)). For closedness, let \(M = (\ker T)^\perp \); it suffices to show \(\| Tx\| \geq c\| x\| \) on \(M\) for some \(c {\gt} 0\), since then images of Cauchy sequences are Cauchy. If not, there are unit vectors \(x_n \in M\) with \(Tx_n \to 0\); passing to a subsequence, \(Kx_n \to y\), so \(x_n = Tx_n + Kx_n \to y\) with \(\| y\| = 1\), \(y \in M\) and \(Ty = 0\), a contradiction. Then \(\operatorname {im}T\) is closed and \(\operatorname {im}T = \overline{\operatorname {im}T} = (\ker T^*)^\perp \) by Theorem 29.3.4 applied to \(T^*\).

(2) Suppose \(T\) is injective but not surjective. Then \(H \supsetneq TH \supsetneq T^2H \supsetneq \cdots \) is a strictly decreasing chain of closed subspaces (each \(T^kH\) is closed by (1) applied to \(I - K_k\) with \(K_k = I - T^k\) compact, since \(T^k = (I - K)^k = I - K_k\) with \(K_k\) a sum of products involving \(K\)). Choose unit vectors \(u_k \in T^kH \cap (T^{k+1}H)^\perp \). For \(l {\gt} k\), \(Ku_k - Ku_l = u_k - (Tu_k + u_l - Tu_l)\) and \(Tu_k + u_l - Tu_l \in T^{k+1}H\), so \(\| Ku_k - Ku_l\| \geq 1\), contradicting compactness. Conversely, if \(T\) is surjective, then \(T^*\) is injective: indeed \(\ker T^* = (\operatorname {im}T)^\perp = 0\); applying the previous argument to \(T^* = I - K^*\) (with \(K^*\) compact) shows \(T^*\) is surjective, hence \(\ker T = (\operatorname {im}T^*)^\perp = 0\).

(3) This is the statement that the index of \(I - K\) is zero; see [Bre11, Theorem 6.6].

015V Remark 29.5.5

\(T \in B(X, Y)\) is Fredholm if \(\ker T\) and \(\operatorname {coker}T = Y/\operatorname {im}T\) are finite-dimensional; then \(\operatorname {im}T\) is closed, and the index \(\operatorname {ind}T = \dim \ker T - \dim \operatorname {coker}T\) is unchanged by compact perturbations and is locally constant in \(T\) [Bre11, Chapter 6]. Elliptic operators on compact manifolds are Fredholm (Chapter 40); the index is then computed by the Atiyah–Singer theorem, and its vanishing for the Laplacian is what produces harmonic representatives.

02AL Counterexample 29.5.6

Self-adjointness is needed in the spectral theorem.

  1. The Volterra operator \(Vf(x) = \int _0^xf(t)\, dt\) on \(L^2([0, 1])\) is compact but has no eigenvalues. It is compact: by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality \(|Vf(x) - Vf(y)| \leq |x - y|^{1/2}\| f\| _2\) and \(|Vf(x)| \leq \| f\| _2\), so \(V\) maps the unit ball to a bounded equicontinuous set of continuous functions, which is relatively compact in \(C([0, 1])\) by the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem (Theorem 26.13.4), hence in \(L^2\). If \(Vf = \lambda f\) with \(\lambda \neq 0\), then \(f = \lambda ^{-1}Vf\) is continuous, hence continuously differentiable, with \(f' = \lambda ^{-1}f\) and \(f(0) = 0\), so \(f = 0\); and \(Vf = 0\) forces \(\int _0^xf = 0\) for all \(x\), hence \(f = 0\) almost everywhere.

  2. The right shift \(S(x_1, x_2, \ldots ) = (0, x_1, x_2, \ldots )\) on \(\ell ^2\) is an isometry without eigenvalues (\(Sx = \lambda x\) gives \(\lambda x_1 = 0\), \(\lambda x_{k+1} = x_k\), hence \(x = 0\)), and it is not surjective. Its adjoint, the left shift, has every \(\lambda \) with \(|\lambda | {\lt} 1\) as an eigenvalue, with eigenvector \((1, \lambda , \lambda ^2, \ldots )\). So an injective bounded operator on an infinite-dimensional space need not be surjective, unlike in finite dimensions.
