74.3 The standard conjectures022T

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

By hard Lefschetz (an axiom of Weil cohomologies; for \(\ell \)-adic cohomology a theorem of Deligne, Theorem 62.4.2(6)), \(L^{n-i} \colon H^i(X) \to H^{2n-i}(X)\) is an isomorphism for \(i \leq n\). As in Chapter 47, this gives the Lefschetz decomposition \(H^i = \bigoplus _jL^jP^{i-2j}\), with \(P^j = \ker (L^{n-j+1} \colon H^j \to H^{2n-j+2})\) for \(j \leq n\), and a unique operator \(\Lambda \) with \([L, \Lambda ] = h_{\deg }\) and \([h_{\deg }, \Lambda ] = -2\Lambda \), where \(h_{\deg }\) acts on \(H^i\) by \(i - n\) (Proposition 47.2.4); explicitly \(\Lambda (L^rx) = r(n - j - r + 1)L^{r-1}x\) for \(x \in P^j\). So \(H^\bullet (X)\) is a representation of \(\mathfrak {sl}_2\) with \(e = L\), \(f = \Lambda \), \(h = h_{\deg }\), in which \(H^i\) is the weight space of weight \(i - n\) and \(P^j\) consists of lowest weight vectors of weight \(j - n\).

022U Conjecture 74.3.1 Lefschetz standard conjecture \(B(X)\)

The operator \(\Lambda \) is algebraic.

022V Conjecture 74.3.2 Künneth standard conjecture \(C(X)\)

The Künneth components \(\pi _i \in H^{2n-i}(X) \otimes H^i(X) \subseteq H^{2n}(X \times X)\) of the class of the diagonal are algebraic; equivalently, the projectors of \(H^\bullet (X)\) onto the summands \(H^i(X)\) are algebraic.

022W Conjecture 74.3.3 Standard conjecture \(D(X)\)

Homological and numerical equivalence coincide on \(Z^\bullet (X) \otimes \mathbb {Q}\): \(A^\bullet _{\mathrm{hom}}(X) = A^\bullet _{\mathrm{num}}(X)\).

022X Conjecture 74.3.4 Hodge standard conjecture \(\mathrm{Hdg}(X)\)

For \(p \leq n/2\), the symmetric bilinear form \((x, y) \mapsto (-1)^p\int _XL^{n-2p}(x \cup y)\) is positive definite on the \(\mathbb {Q}\)-space \(A^p_{\mathrm{hom}}(X) \cap P^{2p}(X)\) of primitive algebraic classes.

The equivalence in \(C(X)\) is Proposition 74.2.1(1): \((\pi _i)_*\) is the projector onto \(H^i(X)\).

022Y Lemma 74.3.5

Let \(V\) be a finite-dimensional representation of \(\mathfrak {sl}_2\) over a field of characteristic \(0\), with weight spaces \(V_w\) for \(h\).

  1. The projector of \(V\) onto \(V_w\), along the other weight spaces, is a polynomial in \(h\).

  2. The projector onto the isotypic component of \(V(m)\) is a polynomial in the Casimir element \(c = ef + fe + \frac12h^2\), which acts on \(V(m)\) by \(\frac12m(m + 2)\).

  3. For \(j \geq 0\), \(e^j \colon V_{-j} \to V_j\) is bijective, and its inverse is the restriction to \(V_j\) of \(P(f^je^j)f^j\) for a polynomial \(P\).

  4. If \(v\) is a lowest weight vector of weight \(-m\) and \(0 \leq j \leq m\), then \(f^je^jv = c_{m,j}v\) with \(c_{m,j}\) a nonzero integer.

Proof â–¼

We use the basis \(v_0, \ldots , v_m\) of \(V(m)\) from Theorem 12.6.2, with \(hv_i = (m - 2i)v_i\), \(fv_i = (i + 1)v_{i+1}\), \(ev_i = (m - i + 1)v_{i-1}\), and the decomposition of \(V\) into irreducibles. (1) \(h\) is diagonalisable with finitely many eigenvalues \(w_1, \ldots , w_s\), and the projector onto \(V_{w_a}\) is \(\prod _{b \neq a}(h - w_b)/(w_a - w_b)\). (2) On \(v_0\), \(ev_0 = 0\) gives \(efv_0 = [e, f]v_0 = hv_0 = mv_0\) and \(fev_0 = 0\), so \(cv_0 = (m + \frac12m^2)v_0\); as \(c\) commutes with \(e\), \(f\), \(h\), it acts on \(V(m)\) by this scalar. The scalars \(\frac12m(m + 2)\) are distinct for distinct \(m \geq 0\), so the projectors are Lagrange polynomials in \(c\). (4) The lowest weight vector of \(V(m)\) is \(v_m\). Applying \(e^j\) multiplies it by \(\prod _{s=0}^{j-1}(s + 1) = j!\) and gives \(v_{m-j}\); applying \(f^j\) multiplies by \(\prod _{t=0}^{j-1}(m - j + t + 1)\) and returns to \(v_m\). All factors are positive. (3) The weight \(-j\) vector of \(V(m)\), when it exists (\(m \geq j\), \(m \equiv j \bmod 2\)), is \(v_i\) with \(i = (m + j)/2\); applying \(e^j\) and then \(f^j\) multiplies it by \(\prod _{s=0}^{j-1}(m - i + s + 1)\prod _{t=0}^{j-1}(i - j + t + 1)\), a product of integers \(\geq (m - j)/2 + 1 \geq 1\). So \(f^je^j\) acts on \(V_{-j}\) diagonalisably with finitely many nonzero eigenvalues \(c_1, \ldots , c_r\), and \(P(f^je^j)\) inverts it for the Lagrange polynomial with \(P(c_a) = c_a^{-1}\). Then \(P(f^je^j)f^j \circ e^j\) is the identity of \(V_{-j}\), so \(e^j\) is injective there; as \(\dim V_{-j} = \dim V_j\), it is bijective, with inverse \(P(f^je^j)f^j|_{V_j}\).

022Z Proposition 74.3.6

Assume \(B(X)\). Then:

  1. \(C(X)\) holds;

  2. for \(p \leq n/2\), \(L^{n-2p} \colon A^p_{\mathrm{hom}}(X) \to A^{n-p}_{\mathrm{hom}}(X)\) is bijective (Grothendieck’s conjecture \(A(X, L)\));

  3. if \(x = \sum _jL^jx_j\) is the Lefschetz decomposition of an algebraic class, with \(x_j\) primitive, then every \(x_j\) is algebraic.

Proof â–¼

By \(B(X)\), \(L\) and \(\Lambda \) are algebraic, hence so is every noncommutative polynomial in them, in particular \(h_{\deg } = [L, \Lambda ]\) and the Casimir element. (1) By Lemma 74.3.5(1), the projector onto \(H^i\) is a polynomial in \(h_{\deg }\). (2) \(L^{n-2p}\) maps algebraic classes to algebraic classes and is injective on \(H^{2p}\) by hard Lefschetz. By Lemma 74.3.5(3), its inverse \(H^{2n-2p} \to H^{2p}\) is the restriction of an algebraic endomorphism, so it maps algebraic classes to algebraic classes. (3) The summand \(L^jP^{i-2j}\) of \(H^i\) is the intersection of \(H^i\) with the isotypic component of \(V(n - i + 2j)\), since a primitive class in \(P^{i-2j}\) is a lowest weight vector of weight \(i - 2j - n\). By Lemma 74.3.5(1),(2) the projector onto \(L^jP^{i-2j}\) is algebraic, so \(L^jx_j\) is algebraic. By Lemma 74.3.5(4), \(x_j = c^{-1}\Lambda ^jL^jx_j\) for a nonzero integer \(c\), so \(x_j\) is algebraic.

0230 Proposition 74.3.7

If \(k \subseteq \mathbb {C}\) and \(H^\bullet \) is Betti cohomology, then \(\mathrm{Hdg}(X)\) holds.

Proof â–¼

Algebraic classes in \(H^{2p}(X_\mathbb {C}; \mathbb {Q})\) are rational classes of type \((p,p)\) (Corollary 60.4.2). The class \(h\) is represented by a Kähler form \(\omega \), the restriction of a multiple of the Fubini–Study form. For a nonzero primitive rational class \(x\) of type \((p,p)\), the Hodge–Riemann relation (Theorem 47.4.2(4)) with \(k = 2p\) and \(\bar x = x\) reads \(Q(x, x) {\gt} 0\), where \(Q(x, x) = (-1)^{2p(2p-1)/2}\int x \cup x \cup [\omega ]^{n-2p} = (-1)^p\int L^{n-2p}(x \cup x)\), since \(p(2p - 1) \equiv p \pmod2\).

0231 Proposition 74.3.8

\(B(X)\) and \(\mathrm{Hdg}(X)\) together imply \(D(X)\).

Proof â–¼

First let \(p \leq n/2\), and put \(\langle x, y\rangle = \int L^{n-2p}(x \cup y)\) on \(H^{2p}\). The Lefschetz decomposition is orthogonal for \(\langle \ ,\ \rangle \): for \(a \in P^{2p-2j}\), \(b \in P^{2p-2j'}\) and \(j {\lt} j'\), \(\langle L^ja, L^{j'}b\rangle = \int b \cup L^{n-2p+j+j'}a\) vanishes because \(n - 2p + j + j' \geq n - (2p - 2j) + 1\), so \(L^{n-2p+j+j'}a = 0\) by primitivity. Let \(x \in A^p_{\mathrm{hom}}(X)\) with \(\langle x, y\rangle = 0\) for every \(y \in A^p_{\mathrm{hom}}(X)\), and write \(x = \sum _jL^jx_j\). By Proposition 74.3.6(3) the \(x_j\) are algebraic, so \(y = L^jx_j\) is allowed, and \(0 = \langle x, L^jx_j\rangle = \langle L^jx_j, L^jx_j\rangle = \int L^{n-2(p-j)}(x_j \cup x_j)\). By \(\mathrm{Hdg}(X)\) in codimension \(p - j\), \(x_j = 0\). Hence \(\langle \ ,\ \rangle \) is nondegenerate on \(A^p_{\mathrm{hom}}(X)\). Now if \(x \in A^p_{\mathrm{hom}}(X)\) is numerically trivial, then \(\langle x, y\rangle = \deg (x \cdot h^{n-2p} \cdot y) = 0\) for all algebraic \(y\), so \(x = 0\).

Now let \(p {\gt} n/2\) and let \(x \in A^p_{\mathrm{hom}}(X)\) be numerically trivial. By Proposition 74.3.6(2) applied to \(n - p {\lt} n/2\), \(L^{2p-n} \colon A^{n-p}_{\mathrm{hom}} \to A^p_{\mathrm{hom}}\) is bijective, so \(x = L^{2p-n}y\) with \(y\) algebraic, and every algebraic class of codimension \(p\) is \(L^{2p-n}u\) with \(u \in A^{n-p}_{\mathrm{hom}}\). Then \(\deg (y \cdot L^{2p-n}u) = \deg (x \cdot u) = 0\) for all such \(u\), so \(y\) is numerically trivial, hence \(y = 0\) by the first case, and \(x = 0\).

0232 Theorem 74.3.9

Let \(k \subseteq \mathbb {C}\) and let \(H^\bullet \) be Betti cohomology. If the Hodge conjecture holds for \(X \times X\), then \(B(X)\), \(C(X)\) and \(D(X)\) hold.

Proof â–¼

The operator \(\Lambda \) is defined over \(\mathbb {Q}\) and is a morphism of Hodge structures of type \((-1,-1)\) (see the proof of Corollary 72.4.3), so its class in \(H^{2n-2}(X \times X; \mathbb {Q})\) is a Hodge class (Proposition 63.6.2). By the Hodge conjecture it is algebraic, which is \(B(X)\). Then \(C(X)\) follows from Proposition 74.3.6, and \(D(X)\) from Propositions 74.3.7 and 74.3.8.

0233 Remark 74.3.10

Grothendieck showed that the standard conjectures for \(X\) and \(X \times X\) over a finite field imply the Riemann hypothesis part of the Weil conjectures, by an argument modelled on Serre’s Kähler analogue: the graph of Frobenius is an algebraic correspondence, and the positivity of \(\mathrm{Hdg}(X \times X)\) bounds its eigenvalues [Gro69b], [Kle68]. Deligne’s proof [Del74a] avoided the standard conjectures. Katz and Messing then deduced \(C(X)\) over finite fields from Deligne’s theorem [KM74]. In positive characteristic, \(\mathrm{Hdg}(X)\) is known in general only for surfaces, where it is the Hodge index theorem [Har77, Theorem V.1.9].

0234 Theorem 74.3.11 Known cases

  1. \(B(X)\) holds if \(\dim X \leq 2\), if \(X\) is an abelian variety [Lie68], for products of varieties for which it holds [Kle68], for varieties whose cohomology is spanned by algebraic classes (projective spaces, Grassmannians, flag varieties), for smooth hypersurfaces and complete intersections in projective space (Exercise 74.6.3), and for hyper-Kähler varieties deformation equivalent to Hilbert schemes of points on K3 surfaces [CM13].

  2. \(C(X)\) holds over finite fields [KM74], and whenever \(B(X)\) holds.

  3. \(D(X)\) holds for divisors in any characteristic, and in characteristic \(0\) for abelian varieties [Lie68].

  4. \(\mathrm{Hdg}(X)\) holds in characteristic \(0\) (Proposition 74.3.7) and for surfaces in any characteristic.

See [Kle94] for a survey and for the classical cases. For a surface, the components of \(\Lambda \) on \(H^2 \to H^0\) and \(H^4 \to H^2\) are expressed through \(h\) and the class of a point, and the component \(H^3 \to H^1\), the inverse of \(L \colon H^1 \to H^3\), is induced by a divisorial correspondence built from the Picard and Albanese varieties. Milne showed that \(\mathrm{Hdg}\) for abelian varieties in positive characteristic would follow from the Hodge conjecture for complex abelian varieties of CM type [Mil02].

0235 Remark 74.3.12

\(B(X)\) asks for the algebraicity of one specific Hodge class on \(X \times X\), while the Hodge conjecture asks for all of them, and still \(B(X)\) is open for most varieties of dimension \(\geq 3\). For a threefold, \(B(X)\) essentially amounts to the algebraicity of the inverse of \(L \colon H^2(X) \to H^4(X)\), a class in \(H^2(X) \otimes H^2(X)\) whose transcendental part is not accessible by divisors. The class of \(\Lambda \) is absolute Hodge (Corollary 72.4.3), but no algebraic cycle representing it is known in general.
