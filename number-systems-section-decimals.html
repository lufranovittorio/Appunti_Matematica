4.9 Irrational numbers and decimal expansions03FV

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

The real numbers were constructed abstractly, as classes of Cauchy sequences. In practice a real number is written as a decimal expansion such as \(3.14159\ldots \), and this section connects the two pictures. It also answers two natural questions. Which decimal expansions are rational? The answer is the eventually periodic ones (Theorem 4.9.5). And how large is \(\mathbb {R}\) compared with the plane \(\mathbb {R}^2\)? They have the same size (Proposition 4.9.7).

03FW Definition 4.9.1

A real number is irrational if it is not rational, that is, if it lies in \(\mathbb {R}\setminus \mathbb {Q}\).

The first example is \(\sqrt2\): it exists in \(\mathbb {R}\) by Proposition 4.7.4, and it is not rational by Proposition 4.6.4. More generally \(\sqrt[n]{m}\) is irrational unless the integer \(m \geq 0\) is an \(n\)-th power (Exercise 4.11.4). The irrational numbers are what the construction of \(\mathbb {R}\) adds to \(\mathbb {Q}\): they fill the gaps, such as the missing supremum of Counterexample 4.6.5.

03FX Proposition 4.9.2

  1. If \(q \in \mathbb {Q}\) and \(x \in \mathbb {R}\setminus \mathbb {Q}\), then \(q + x\) is irrational, and so is \(qx\) if \(q \neq 0\).

  2. Between any two real numbers \(a {\lt} b\) there are both a rational and an irrational number.

  3. \(\mathbb {R}\setminus \mathbb {Q}\) is uncountable; indeed \(|\mathbb {R}\setminus \mathbb {Q}| = |\mathbb {R}| = 2^{\aleph _0}\).

Proof â–¼

(1) If \(q + x = r \in \mathbb {Q}\), then \(x = r - q \in \mathbb {Q}\); if \(qx = r\) with \(q \neq 0\), then \(x = r/q \in \mathbb {Q}\). (2) A rational number exists by density (Proposition 4.7.3). For an irrational one, apply density to \(a/\sqrt2 {\lt} b/\sqrt2\) twice to find rationals \(a/\sqrt2 {\lt} q_1 {\lt} q_2 {\lt} b/\sqrt2\); at least one of them, call it \(q\), is nonzero. Then \(a {\lt} q\sqrt2 {\lt} b\), and \(q\sqrt2\) is irrational by (1). (3) \(\mathbb {Q}\) is countable (proof of Proposition 4.8.6). If \(\mathbb {R}\setminus \mathbb {Q}\) were countable, then so would be \(\mathbb {R}= \mathbb {Q}\cup (\mathbb {R}\setminus \mathbb {Q})\), by Corollary 3.7.6, contradicting Proposition 4.8.6. So \(\mathbb {R}\setminus \mathbb {Q}\) is infinite and uncountable, and by the same corollary \(|\mathbb {R}| \leq |\mathbb {Q}\sqcup (\mathbb {R}\setminus \mathbb {Q})| = \max (\aleph _0, |\mathbb {R}\setminus \mathbb {Q}|) = |\mathbb {R}\setminus \mathbb {Q}| \leq |\mathbb {R}|\).

So in the sense of cardinality almost every real number is irrational, although both kinds are everywhere. The sum of two irrationals may be rational, as \(\sqrt2 + (-\sqrt2) = 0\) shows.

Decimal expansions.

In an Archimedean ordered field every \(y\) has an integer part \(\lfloor y\rfloor \), the largest integer \(m\) with \(m \leq y\): the integers \(m {\gt} y\) form a nonempty set that is bounded below, which has a least element \(m_0\) by Theorem 4.4.2, and \(\lfloor y\rfloor = m_0 - 1\). Then \(\lfloor y\rfloor \leq y {\lt} \lfloor y\rfloor + 1\), and \(\lfloor y\rfloor \) is the only integer with this property. Since every real number is an integer plus an element of \([0, 1)\), it suffices to expand the numbers in \([0, 1)\). For a sequence of digits \(d_n \in \{ 0, 1, \ldots , 9\} \), \(n \geq 1\), we write

\[ 0.d_1d_2d_3\ldots = \sup _{N \geq 1}s_N, \qquad s_N = \sum _{n=1}^Nd_n10^{-n}; \]

the supremum exists because \(s_N \leq \sum _{n=1}^N9 \cdot 10^{-n} = 1 - 10^{-N} {\lt} 1\). (Limits of sequences of real numbers are studied in Chapter 27; here only suprema are needed, and \(s_N\) increases with \(N\).)

03FY Theorem 4.9.3

Every \(x \in [0, 1)\) has exactly one expansion \(x = 0.d_1d_2d_3\ldots \) whose digits are not all equal to \(9\) from some point on. Its digits are given by

\[ d_n = \lfloor 10^nx\rfloor - 10\lfloor 10^{n-1}x\rfloor , \qquad \text{and}\qquad s_N = \frac{\lfloor 10^Nx\rfloor }{10^N}. \]
Proof â–¼

Existence. Let \(m_N = \lfloor 10^Nx\rfloor \), so \(m_0 = 0\) and \(m_N \leq 10^Nx {\lt} m_N + 1\). Multiplying the inequalities for \(N - 1\) by \(10\) gives \(10m_{N-1} \leq 10^Nx {\lt} 10m_{N-1} + 10\), so \(10m_{N-1} \leq m_N \leq 10m_{N-1} + 9\), that is, \(d_N = m_N - 10m_{N-1} \in \{ 0, \ldots , 9\} \). Summing \(d_n10^{-n} = m_n10^{-n} - m_{n-1}10^{-(n-1)}\) over \(n \leq N\) gives \(s_N = m_N10^{-N}\), so

\[ s_N \leq x {\lt} s_N + 10^{-N} \qquad \text{for all } N. \]

Hence \(x\) is an upper bound of the \(s_N\), and no \(u {\lt} x\) is one: by the Archimedean property \(10^{-N} {\lt} x - u\) for some \(N\) (as \(10^N \geq N\)), and then \(s_N {\gt} x - 10^{-N} {\gt} u\). So \(x = 0.d_1d_2\ldots \). If \(d_n = 9\) for all \(n {\gt} N\), then for \(M {\gt} N\)

\[ s_M = s_N + 9\sum _{n=N+1}^M10^{-n} = s_N + 10^{-N} - 10^{-M}, \]

whose supremum over \(M\) is \(s_N + 10^{-N}\), by the Archimedean property again; but this supremum is \(x {\lt} s_N + 10^{-N}\). So the digits are not eventually \(9\).

Uniqueness. Let \(x = 0.e_1e_2\ldots \) with digits \(e_n\) not eventually \(9\), and partial sums \(t_N\). Fix \(N\) and choose \(m {\gt} N\) with \(e_m \leq 8\). For \(M \geq m\),

\[ t_M - t_N = \sum _{n=N+1}^Me_n10^{-n} \leq \sum _{n=N+1}^M9 \cdot 10^{-n} - 10^{-m} {\lt} 10^{-N} - 10^{-m}, \]

and the same bound holds for \(N {\lt} M {\lt} m\). So \(x = \sup _Mt_M \leq t_N + 10^{-N} - 10^{-m} {\lt} t_N + 10^{-N}\), while \(t_N \leq x\). Thus \(10^Nt_N\) is an integer with \(10^Nt_N \leq 10^Nx {\lt} 10^Nt_N + 1\), so \(10^Nt_N = \lfloor 10^Nx\rfloor = m_N\) for every \(N\), and \(e_N = m_N - 10m_{N-1} = d_N\).

03FZ Example 4.9.4

  1. \(0.999\ldots = 1\): here \(s_N = 1 - 10^{-N}\), and the supremum is \(1\). Similarly \(0.4999\ldots = 0.5000\ldots \). The proof shows that this is the only ambiguity: a number has two expansions if and only if it is of the form \(m/10^N\), one ending in zeros and one ending in nines (Exercise 4.11.8). This is why the theorem excludes the expansions ending in nines.

  2. Long division computes the digits of a fraction. For \(x = 1/7\): \(10 = 1 \cdot 7 + 3\), \(30 = 4 \cdot 7 + 2\), \(20 = 2 \cdot 7 + 6\), \(60 = 8 \cdot 7 + 4\), \(40 = 5 \cdot 7 + 5\), \(50 = 7 \cdot 7 + 1\), and the remainder \(1\) returns, so \(1/7 = 0.142857\, 142857\ldots \) repeats with period \(6\).

  3. \(\sqrt2 - 1 = 0.41421356\ldots \): the digits are computed from \(\lfloor 10^N\sqrt2\rfloor \), the largest integer whose square is at most \(2 \cdot 10^{2N}\); for instance \(141^2 = 19881 \leq 20000 {\lt} 142^2 = 20164\) gives the first two digits \(4, 1\).

A sequence of digits is eventually periodic if there are \(N \geq 0\) and \(p \geq 1\) with \(d_{n+p} = d_n\) for all \(n {\gt} N\); we write \(0.d_1 \ldots d_N\overline{d_{N+1} \ldots d_{N+p}}\). A finite expansion, ending in zeros, is eventually periodic with period \(1\).

03G0 Theorem 4.9.5

A number \(x \in [0, 1)\) is rational if and only if its decimal expansion is eventually periodic. More precisely:

  1. \(0.\overline{c_1 \ldots c_p} = \dfrac {c}{10^p - 1}\), where \(c = c_1c_2\ldots c_p\) is the integer with these decimal digits, \(c = \sum _{i=1}^pc_i10^{p-i}\);

  2. if \(x = a/b\) with integers \(0 \leq a {\lt} b\), the digits of \(x\) are eventually periodic, with a period \(p \leq b\) that begins after at most \(b\) digits.

Proof â–¼

For a digit sequence \((d_n)\) with partial sums \(s_N\), multiplication by \(10^k\) shifts the digits: since multiplying by a positive constant preserves suprema (Lemma 4.7.2),

\[ 10^k \cdot 0.d_1d_2\ldots = \sup _N10^ks_{N+k} = D_k + 0.d_{k+1}d_{k+2}\ldots , \qquad D_k = \sum _{n=1}^kd_n10^{k-n}, \]

because \(10^ks_{N+k} = D_k + \sum _{n=1}^Nd_{k+n}10^{-n}\), and adding a constant also preserves suprema.

(1) Let \(y = 0.\overline{c_1 \ldots c_p}\). Shifting by \(p\) gives the same digit sequence, so \(10^py = c + y\), and \(y = c/(10^p - 1)\).

Eventually periodic implies rational. If \(d_{n+p} = d_n\) for \(n {\gt} N\), then \(10^Nx = D_N + y\) with \(y = 0.d_{N+1}d_{N+2}\ldots \) periodic, which is rational by (1); so \(x = (D_N + y)/10^N \in \mathbb {Q}\).

(2) Long division: put \(r_0 = a\), and for \(n \geq 1\) let \(q_n = \lfloor 10r_{n-1}/b\rfloor \) and \(r_n = 10r_{n-1} - bq_n\), so that \(0 \leq r_n {\lt} b\). Since \(r_{n-1} {\lt} b\), \(q_n \leq 9\). By induction on \(N\), \(10^Na/b = Q_N + r_N/b\) with \(Q_N = \sum _{n=1}^Nq_n10^{N-n}\): this holds for \(N = 0\), and multiplying by \(10\) gives \(10^{N+1}a/b = 10Q_N + 10r_N/b = (10Q_N + q_{N+1}) + r_{N+1}/b\). As \(0 \leq r_N/b {\lt} 1\), \(Q_N = \lfloor 10^Nx\rfloor \), and Theorem 4.9.3 shows that the \(q_n\) are the decimal digits of \(x\). The remainders \(r_0, \ldots , r_b\) are \(b + 1\) elements of \(\{ 0, \ldots , b - 1\} \), so \(r_i = r_j\) for some \(i {\lt} j \leq b\) (Proposition 3.6.5). Each \(r_n\) and \(q_n\) is determined by \(r_{n-1}\), so by induction \(r_{n+p} = r_n\) for \(n \geq i\), with \(p = j - i\), and \(q_{n+p} = q_n\) for \(n {\gt} i\).

03G1 Example 4.9.6

  1. \(0.\overline{123} = 123/999 = 41/333\), \(0.\overline{9} = 9/9 = 1\), and \(0.1\overline{6} = (1 + 0.\overline{6})/10 = (1 + \frac23)/10 = \frac16\).

  2. The number \(x = 0.1010010001000010\ldots \), whose \(1\)’s are separated by blocks of \(1, 2, 3, \ldots \) zeros, is irrational. If its digits satisfied \(d_{n+p} = d_n\) for \(n {\gt} N\), take a digit \(d_m = 1\) with \(m {\gt} N\); then \(d_{m+kp} = 1\) for all \(k \geq 0\), so beyond \(m\) two consecutive \(1\)’s are never more than \(p\) places apart. But the blocks of zeros become longer than \(p\).

  3. The same argument shows that Champernowne’s number \(0.123456789101112131415\ldots \), obtained by writing the positive integers one after another, is irrational: it contains the blocks of zeros of \(10^k\), of every length \(k\), but it does not end in zeros.

The plane has as many points as the line.

One might expect \(\mathbb {R}^2\) to be “larger” than \(\mathbb {R}\), and Cantor himself expected so; in 1877 he proved the opposite, and wrote to Dedekind that he saw it but did not believe it. There are two proofs, one through subsets of \(\mathbb {N}\) and one through decimal digits.

03G2 Proposition 4.9.7

\(|\mathbb {R}^n| = |\mathbb {R}|\) for every \(n \geq 1\), and the set \(\mathbb {R}^{\mathbb {N}}\) of all sequences of real numbers also has the cardinality \(|\mathbb {R}| = 2^{\aleph _0}\). In particular the plane \(\mathbb {R}^2\), the square \([0, 1]^2\) and the segment \([0, 1]\) all have the same cardinality.

Proof â–¼

By Proposition 4.8.6 there is a bijection \(\mathbb {R}\to \mathcal{P}(\mathbb {N})\), so \(\mathbb {R}^2 \approx \mathcal{P}(\mathbb {N}) \times \mathcal{P}(\mathbb {N})\). The map

\[ \mathcal{P}(\mathbb {N}) \times \mathcal{P}(\mathbb {N}) \to \mathcal{P}(\mathbb {N}), \qquad (S, T) \mapsto \{ 2n : n \in S\} \cup \{ 2n + 1 : n \in T\} , \]

is a bijection: a subset \(U\) of \(\mathbb {N}\) comes from exactly one pair, namely \(S = \{ n : 2n \in U\} \) and \(T = \{ n : 2n + 1 \in U\} \), because every natural number is uniquely even or odd (Proposition 4.2.4). So \(\mathbb {R}^2 \approx \mathcal{P}(\mathbb {N}) \approx \mathbb {R}\), and by induction \(\mathbb {R}^{n+1} = \mathbb {R}^n \times \mathbb {R}\approx \mathbb {R}\times \mathbb {R}\approx \mathbb {R}\). For sequences, \(\mathbb {R}^{\mathbb {N}} \approx \mathcal{P}(\mathbb {N})^{\mathbb {N}}\), and \((S_k)_{k \in \mathbb {N}} \mapsto \{ (k, n) : n \in S_k\} \) is a bijection \(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb {N})^{\mathbb {N}} \to \mathcal{P}(\mathbb {N}\times \mathbb {N})\), with inverse \(U \mapsto (\{ n : (k, n) \in U\} )_k\). A bijection \(\mathbb {N}\times \mathbb {N}\to \mathbb {N}\) (Corollary 3.6.13) induces a bijection \(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb {N}\times \mathbb {N}) \to \mathcal{P}(\mathbb {N})\). Finally \([0, 1] \subseteq [0, 1]^2\) via \(x \mapsto (x, 0)\), the square lies in \(\mathbb {R}^2\), and \(x \mapsto \frac12\bigl(1 + \frac{x}{1 + |x|}\bigr)\) is an injection \(\mathbb {R}\to (0, 1) \subseteq [0, 1]\): it is strictly increasing, since \(\frac{x}{1 + x} = 1 - \frac1{1 + x}\) increases for \(x \geq 0\) and \(\frac{x}{1 + |x|}\) is odd. So \(|\mathbb {R}| \leq |[0, 1]| \leq |[0, 1]^2| \leq |\mathbb {R}^2| = |\mathbb {R}|\), and all are equal by Theorem 3.6.2.

03G3 Remark 4.9.8

Cantor’s interleaving of digits. For \(x = 0.a_1a_2a_3\ldots \) and \(y = 0.b_1b_2b_3\ldots \) in \([0, 1)\), written with the expansions of Theorem 4.9.3, put

\[ f(x, y) = 0.a_1b_1a_2b_2a_3b_3\ldots . \]

The interleaved digits are not eventually \(9\), since otherwise both the \(a_n\) and the \(b_n\) would be; so by the uniqueness in the theorem \(f(x, y)\) determines its digits, hence \(x\) and \(y\): \(f \colon [0, 1)^2 \to [0, 1)\) is injective. It is not surjective: \(0.191919\ldots \) would need \(y = 0.999\ldots \), which is not an allowed expansion. Together with the injection \(x \mapsto (x, 0)\) and Theorem 3.6.2 this proves \([0, 1)^2 \approx [0, 1)\) again. Such a bijection is necessarily wild: none of them is continuous. Removing one point disconnects the line but not the plane, and a continuous map sends connected sets to connected sets (Chapter 18); so a continuous bijection \(\mathbb {R}^2 \to \mathbb {R}\) would map the connected set \(\mathbb {R}^2 \setminus \{ p\} \) onto the disconnected set \(\mathbb {R}\setminus \{ f(p)\} \). The difference between the line and the plane, their dimension, is not a matter of how many points they have but of how the points are arranged, and it is detected by topology. Once \(\mathbb {C}\) is constructed as \(\mathbb {R}^2\) (Section 4.10), the proposition also gives \(|\mathbb {C}| = |\mathbb {R}|\).
