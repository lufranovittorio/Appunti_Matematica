42.5 Fredholm theory and the spectral decomposition01D5

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

01D6 Definition 42.5.1

The formal adjoint \(P^*\) of a differential operator \(P\) is the differential operator of the same order characterised by \(\int _M\langle Pu, v\rangle = \int _M\langle u, P^*v\rangle \) for smooth sections; it exists, is unique, and has symbol \(\sigma _m(P^*)(x, \xi ) = \sigma _m(P)(x, \xi )^*\) (Proposition 42.2.3), so \(P^*\) is elliptic if \(P\) is. The operator \(P\) is formally self-adjoint if \(P = P^*\); the Hodge Laplacian is (Proposition 40.15.3).

For instance on the circle, \(\int _0^{2\pi }u^{\prime }\bar v\, d\theta = -\int _0^{2\pi }u\overline{v^{\prime }}\, d\theta \) for periodic functions, so \((d/d\theta )^* = -d/d\theta \), with symbol \(-i\xi = \overline{i\xi }\), the adjoint of the symbol \(i\xi \) of \(d/d\theta \). This is the reason for the factor \(i\) in the definition of the symbol: integration by parts produces a sign \((-1)^m\) for \(m\) derivatives, and complex conjugation of \(i^m\) produces the same sign.

The following theorem is the infinite-dimensional analogue of the statement that, for a linear map \(A \colon V \to W\) between finite-dimensional inner product spaces, \(W = \operatorname {im}A \oplus \ker A^*\), so that \(Av = w\) is solvable exactly when \(w \perp \ker A^*\).

01D7 Theorem 42.5.2

Let \(P\) be elliptic of order \(m\) on a compact manifold. Then:

  1. \(\ker P = \{ u \in \Gamma (M, E) : Pu = 0\} \) is finite-dimensional and consists of smooth sections, and the same holds for \(\ker P^*\);

  2. \(P \colon H^{s+m}(E) \to H^s(F)\) is Fredholm for every \(s\), with kernel and cokernel independent of \(s\);

  3. there is an orthogonal decomposition \(L^2(F) = \operatorname {im}(P) \oplus \ker P^*\), and \(Pu = f\) is solvable with \(u\) smooth if and only if \(f\) is smooth and orthogonal to \(\ker P^*\).

Proof â–¼

(1) Elements of the kernel are smooth by elliptic regularity. On \(\ker P\) the elliptic estimate gives \(\| u\| _{s+m} \leq C_s\| u\| _s\), so the \(H^{s}\) and \(H^{s+m}\) norms are equivalent there; since the inclusion \(H^{s+m} \to H^s\) is compact (Proposition 42.3.2), the unit ball of \(\ker P\) is compact, and \(\ker P\) is finite-dimensional (Proposition 30.2.2(3)).

(2) Closed range: let \(K \subseteq H^{s+m}\) be the orthogonal complement of \(\ker P\). We claim \(\| u\| _{s+m} \leq C\| Pu\| _s\) on \(K\). If not, there are \(u_j \in K\) with \(\| u_j\| _{s+m} = 1\) and \(Pu_j \to 0\); by compactness of the inclusion, after passing to a subsequence \(u_j \to u\) in \(H^s\), and the elliptic estimate gives \(\| u_j - u_l\| _{s+m} \leq C(\| P(u_j - u_l)\| _s + \| u_j - u_l\| _s) \to 0\), so \(u_j \to u\) in \(H^{s+m}\) with \(\| u\| _{s+m} = 1\), \(Pu = 0\) and \(u \in K\), a contradiction. Hence \(P\) has closed range, and with (1) applied to \(P\) and \(P^*\) it is Fredholm; the kernel is independent of \(s\) by regularity, and so is the cokernel by (3).

(3) The operator \(P\) with domain \(H^m(E)\) and its formal adjoint satisfy: \(f \in L^2(F)\) is orthogonal to \(\operatorname {im}(P)\) if and only if \(\int \langle Pu, f\rangle = 0\) for all smooth \(u\), that is, \(P^*f = 0\) in the sense of distributions, which by elliptic regularity means \(f \in \ker P^*\) and \(f\) smooth. Since the range is closed, \(L^2 = \overline{\operatorname {im}P} \oplus \ker P^* = \operatorname {im}P \oplus \ker P^*\), where \(\operatorname {im}P\) is taken on \(H^m\); and if \(f\) is smooth and orthogonal to \(\ker P^*\), a solution \(u \in H^m\) exists and is smooth by regularity.

The index of \(P\) is \(\operatorname {ind}P = \dim \ker P - \dim \ker P^*\), the number of free parameters in the solutions minus the number of conditions on the right side. For square matrices it is \(0\); for elliptic operators it can be any integer, and it is a deep invariant (Remark 42.5.10).

02C9 Example 42.5.3

On the circle \(S^1 = \mathbb {R}/2\pi \mathbb {Z}\) let \(P = d/d\theta \) on complex functions. Its symbol \(i\xi \) is invertible for \(\xi \neq 0\), so \(P\) is elliptic of order \(1\), and integration by parts gives \(P^* = -d/d\theta \). Both kernels consist of the constants. Theorem 42.5.2 predicts that \(u' = f\) has a smooth periodic solution exactly when \(f\) is orthogonal to \(\ker P^*\), that is, when \(\int _0^{2\pi }f\, d\theta = 0\); this is visible directly, since \(u(\theta ) = \int _0^\theta f\) is periodic exactly in that case. The index is \(\dim \ker P - \dim \ker P^* = 0\). For the perturbation \(P_c = d/d\theta + c\) with a constant \(c \in \mathbb {C}\), the kernel is spanned by \(e^{-c\theta }\) when this function is \(2\pi \)-periodic, that is, when \(c \in i\mathbb {Z}\), and is \(0\) otherwise; so the dimension of the kernel jumps. The adjoint \(P_c^* = -d/d\theta + \bar c\) has kernel spanned by \(e^{\bar c\theta }\) for exactly the same values of \(c\), so the index stays \(0\), as predicted by Remark 42.5.10, since the principal symbol does not depend on \(c\).

02OO Example 42.5.4

On \(S^1 = \mathbb {R}/2\pi \mathbb {Z}\) consider \(P = -\frac{d^2}{d\theta ^2} - c\) with a real constant \(c\), which is elliptic and formally self-adjoint. On the Fourier coefficients of \(u = \sum _k\hat u(k)e^{ik\theta }\) it acts by \(\widehat{Pu}(k) = (k^2 - c)\hat u(k)\). If \(c\) is not the square of an integer, \(P\) is bijective on smooth functions, with inverse \(\hat f(k) \mapsto \hat f(k)/(k^2 - c)\), which gains two derivatives. If \(c = k_0^2\), the kernel is spanned by \(e^{\pm ik_0\theta }\) (by \(1\) if \(k_0 = 0\)), and \(-u^{\prime \prime } - k_0^2u = f\) is solvable exactly when \(\hat f(\pm k_0) = 0\), that is, when \(f\) is orthogonal to the kernel. This is the classical phenomenon of resonance: forcing a string at one of its own frequencies has no periodic response. Theorem 42.5.2 is the statement that this alternative, “either unique solvability or a finite number of conditions”, holds for every elliptic operator on every compact manifold.

034D Example 42.5.5

The Laplacian on the torus. On \(T^n = \mathbb {R}^n/\mathbb {Z}^n\) with the flat metric, \(\Delta = -\sum _j\partial _j^2\) acts on Fourier coefficients by \(\widehat{\Delta u}(k) = 4\pi ^2|k|^2\hat u(k)\), \(k \in \mathbb {Z}^n\). Every statement of Theorem 42.5.2 can be seen directly.

  1. The kernel consists of the \(u\) with \(\hat u(k) = 0\) for \(k \neq 0\): the constants. As \(\Delta \) is formally self-adjoint, \(\ker \Delta ^* = \ker \Delta \) is also the constants.

  2. The equation \(\Delta u = f\) forces \(\hat f(0) = 0\), that is, \(\int _{T^n}f = 0\), which is orthogonality to \(\ker \Delta ^*\). Conversely, if \(\hat f(0) = 0\), then \(\hat u(k) = \hat f(k)/(4\pi ^2|k|^2)\) for \(k \neq 0\) (and \(\hat u(0)\) arbitrary) defines a solution, unique up to the kernel.

  3. The solution gains two derivatives: for \(k \neq 0\) we have \(|k| \geq 1\), so \(2|k|^2 \geq 1 + |k|^2 = \langle k\rangle ^2\) and \(4\pi ^2|k|^2 \geq 2\pi ^2\langle k\rangle ^2\); hence \(\langle k\rangle ^{2(s+2)}|\hat u(k)|^2 \leq \frac{1}{4\pi ^4}\langle k\rangle ^{2s}|\hat f(k)|^2\), and \(f \in H^s\) gives \(u \in H^{s+2}\), and \(f\) smooth gives \(u\) smooth. This is elliptic regularity in its simplest form.

  4. The image of \(\Delta \colon H^{s+2} \to H^s\) is the closed hyperplane \(\{ \hat f(0) = 0\} \), of codimension \(1\), and the index is \(1 - 1 = 0\).

By contrast, the non-elliptic operator \(\partial _1\) on \(T^2\) multiplies \(\hat u(k)\) by \(2\pi ik_1\), which vanishes for all \(k = (0, k_2)\): its kernel, the functions of \(x_2\) alone, is infinite-dimensional, and a solution of \(\partial _1u = f\) gains a derivative only in the \(x_1\)-direction.

The operator \(P^*P\) is always formally self-adjoint and nonnegative, and it is elliptic when \(P\) is, since its symbol is \(\sigma _m(P)^*\sigma _m(P)\) (Proposition 42.2.3). It is the mechanism by which the Hodge Laplacian is built from \(d\) and its adjoint, and the following corollary is the abstract form of the statement that harmonic forms are closed and coclosed.

034E Corollary 42.5.6

Let \(P\) be elliptic. Then \(\ker (P^*P) = \ker P\), and the smooth sections decompose orthogonally as

\[ \Gamma (M, F) = P\bigl(\Gamma (M, E)\bigr) \oplus \ker P^*, \qquad \Gamma (M, E) = P^*\bigl(\Gamma (M, F)\bigr) \oplus \ker P. \]
Proof â–¼

If \(P^*Pu = 0\), then \(0 = \int \langle P^*Pu, u\rangle = \int |Pu|^2\), so \(Pu = 0\); the converse is clear. For the first decomposition, \(P(\Gamma (M, E)) \perp \ker P^*\) by the definition of the formal adjoint, and a smooth \(f\) is the sum of its orthogonal projection \(h\) to the finite-dimensional space \(\ker P^*\), which is smooth, and \(f - h\), which is smooth and orthogonal to \(\ker P^*\), hence of the form \(Pu\) with \(u\) smooth by Theorem 42.5.2(3). The second decomposition is the first one for the elliptic operator \(P^*\), whose adjoint is \(P\).

01D8 Theorem 42.5.7

Let \(P\) be a formally self-adjoint elliptic operator of order \(m {\gt} 0\) on a compact manifold, with \(P \geq 0\) in the sense that \(\int \langle Pu, u\rangle \geq 0\). Then \(L^2(E)\) has a complete orthonormal system of smooth eigensections of \(P\), the eigenvalues \(0 \leq \lambda _1 \leq \lambda _2 \leq \cdots \) are real, have finite multiplicity, and tend to \(+\infty \). Every \(u \in L^2(E)\) is the \(L^2\)-limit of its eigenfunction expansion, which converges in \(\Gamma (M, E)\) when \(u\) is smooth.

Proof â–¼

By Theorem 42.5.2 applied to \(P + I\), which is elliptic and injective on smooth sections (as \(\int \langle (P+I)u, u\rangle \geq \| u\| _{L^2}^2\)), the map \(P + I \colon H^m(E) \to L^2(E)\) is bijective, with bounded inverse \(G\) by the open mapping theorem (Theorem 30.4.2). Composing with the compact inclusion \(H^m \to L^2\) shows that \(G \colon L^2 \to L^2\) is compact, and it is self-adjoint and positive because \(P + I\) is. By the spectral theorem for compact self-adjoint operators (Theorem 30.6.3) there is an orthonormal basis of \(L^2(E)\) of eigenvectors of \(G\), with eigenvalues \(\mu _k {\gt} 0\) tending to \(0\) (they are nonzero since \(G\) is injective). Then \(Pu_k = (\mu _k^{-1} - 1)u_k\), so the \(u_k\) are eigensections of \(P\) with eigenvalues \(\lambda _k = \mu _k^{-1} - 1 \to \infty \), smooth by elliptic regularity, and \(\lambda _k \geq 0\) by positivity. Finite multiplicity is finite-dimensionality of the eigenspaces, which are kernels of the elliptic operators \(P - \lambda _k\). For smooth \(u\), the coefficients \(\langle u, u_k\rangle \) decay faster than any power of \(\lambda _k\), because \(\lambda _k^N\langle u, u_k\rangle = \langle P^Nu, u_k\rangle \) is bounded; with the Sobolev embedding this gives convergence in every \(H^s\), hence in \(\Gamma (M, E)\).

The proof reduces the spectral theory of the unbounded operator \(P\) to that of the compact operator \(G = (P + I)^{-1}\); the two have the same eigenvectors, and the eigenvalues correspond by \(\lambda = \mu ^{-1} - 1\). Eigenvalues of \(P\) tend to infinity because those of \(G\) tend to zero, which in turn reflects the compactness of \(H^m \to L^2\). For the circle the theorem gives back the Fourier basis \(e^{ik\theta }\) of \(-d^2/d\theta ^2\), with eigenvalues \(k^2\); for the round sphere \(S^2\) some eigenfunctions are computed in Exercise 42.6.6. The first nonzero eigenvalue of the Laplacian on functions is the best constant in the Poincaré inequality (Chapter 43), and the eigenvalue expansion solves the heat equation (Exercise 42.6.8).

The positivity assumption is a convenience: it makes \(P + I\) injective, so that its inverse exists. Without it the conclusion survives in a slightly weaker form, with eigenvalues that may go to \(-\infty \) as well.

034F Corollary 42.5.8

Let \(P\) be a formally self-adjoint elliptic operator of order \(m {\gt} 0\) on a compact manifold, not necessarily nonnegative. Then \(L^2(E)\) has a complete orthonormal system of smooth eigensections of \(P\); the eigenvalues are real, have finite multiplicity, and \(|\lambda | \to \infty \): for every \(c\) only finitely many of them satisfy \(|\lambda | \leq c\). If moreover \(P\) is bounded below, \(\int \langle Pu, u\rangle \geq -c_0\| u\| _{L^2}^2\) for some \(c_0\) and all smooth \(u\), then only finitely many eigenvalues are negative, and they can be ordered as \(\lambda _1 \leq \lambda _2 \leq \cdots \to +\infty \).

Proof â–¼

The operator \(P^2 = P^*P\) is elliptic of order \(2m\), with principal symbol \(\sigma _m(P)^2\) (Proposition 42.2.3), formally self-adjoint, and nonnegative: \(\int \langle P^2u, u\rangle = \int |Pu|^2 \geq 0\). By Theorem 42.5.7, \(L^2(E)\) is the Hilbert space direct sum of the eigenspaces \(E_\nu = \ker (P^2 - \nu )\), which are finite-dimensional, consist of smooth sections, and are nonzero only for a sequence of values \(\nu \geq 0\) tending to \(\infty \). Since \(P\) commutes with \(P^2\), it maps each \(E_\nu \) into itself, and on the finite-dimensional space \(E_\nu \) it is symmetric for the \(L^2\) inner product, by the definition of the formal adjoint. So \(E_\nu \) has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of \(P\) (Theorem 11.10.6), with real eigenvalues \(\lambda \) satisfying \(\lambda ^2 = \nu \), that is, \(\lambda = \pm \sqrt\nu \). Together these bases form a complete orthonormal system of smooth eigensections of \(P\). The \(\lambda \)-eigenspace of \(P\) lies in \(E_{\lambda ^2}\), hence is finite-dimensional, and \(|\lambda | = \sqrt\nu \to \infty \). If \(P\) is bounded below, apply Theorem 42.5.7 to the nonnegative operator \(P + c_0\), which has the same eigensections and eigenvalues shifted by \(c_0\); its eigenvalues tend to \(+\infty \), so those of \(P\) do too, and only finitely many are negative.

034G Example 42.5.9

  1. On \(S^1 = \mathbb {R}/2\pi \mathbb {Z}\), the operator \(P = -i\, d/d\theta \) is elliptic of order \(1\) and formally self-adjoint, since \((d/d\theta )^* = -d/d\theta \). Its eigenfunctions are \(e^{ik\theta }\) with eigenvalues \(k \in \mathbb {Z}\), which go to both \(+\infty \) and \(-\infty \): \(P\) is not bounded below, and its square is \(-d^2/d\theta ^2\). The same happens for every first-order operator of Dirac type, such as \(d + \delta \) on forms (Chapter 43), whose square is the Hodge Laplacian: the eigenvalues of \(d + \delta \) are \(\pm \sqrt\nu \) for the eigenvalues \(\nu \) of \(\Delta \).

  2. The operator \(-d^2/d\theta ^2 - c\) with \(c {\gt} 0\) is bounded below but not nonnegative. Its eigenvalues are \(k^2 - c\), of which finitely many, those with \(k^2 {\lt} c\), are negative. More generally, adding a smooth potential \(V\) to a nonnegative operator, \(P = \Delta + V\), gives an operator bounded below by \(-\sup |V|\).

  3. The order must be positive: for \(m = 0\), an operator of order \(0\) is multiplication by a section of \(\operatorname {End}(E)\), and multiplication by the function \(x\) on \(L^2([0, 1])\) has no eigenvectors at all (Example 30.7.4(2)). On a compact manifold the function \(\cos \theta \) on \(S^1\) gives the same phenomenon.

01D9 Remark 42.5.10

The index \(\operatorname {ind}P = \dim \ker P - \dim \ker P^*\) depends only on the homotopy class of the principal symbol, and the Atiyah–Singer index theorem computes it as a characteristic number of the symbol; for \(d + \delta \) this gives the Gauss–Bonnet theorem, and for the Dolbeault operator the Riemann–Roch–Hirzebruch theorem (Chapter 50). See [LM89].

42.5.1 Variational description of the eigenvalues034H

For a symmetric matrix the smallest eigenvalue is the minimum of \(\langle Ax, x\rangle \) on the unit sphere. The same is true for elliptic operators, and it gives a way to estimate eigenvalues without computing them. Let \(P\) be as in Theorem 42.5.7, with orthonormal eigensections \(u_1, u_2, \ldots \) and eigenvalues \(0 \leq \lambda _1 \leq \lambda _2 \leq \cdots \), listed with multiplicity. The Rayleigh quotient of a smooth section \(u \neq 0\) is

\[ \mathcal{R}(u) = \frac{\int _M\langle Pu, u\rangle }{\int _M|u|^2}. \]
034I Proposition 42.5.11 Min-max principle

  1. For smooth \(u\) with coefficients \(c_j = \langle u, u_j\rangle \), \(\int \langle Pu, u\rangle = \sum _j\lambda _j|c_j|^2\).

  2. \(\lambda _1 = \min _{u \neq 0}\mathcal{R}(u)\), and more generally \(\lambda _k = \min \{ \mathcal{R}(u) : u \neq 0, \ u \perp u_1, \ldots , u_{k-1}\} \); the minima are attained exactly at the eigensections for \(\lambda _1\), resp. at the combinations of eigensections with eigenvalue \(\lambda _k\) orthogonal to \(u_1, \ldots , u_{k-1}\).

  3. (Courant–Fischer) \(\lambda _k = \min _V\max _{0 \neq u \in V}\mathcal{R}(u)\), the minimum over all \(k\)-dimensional subspaces \(V\) of \(\Gamma (M, E)\).

Proof â–¼

(1) The coefficients of the smooth section \(Pu\) are \(\langle Pu, u_j\rangle = \langle u, Pu_j\rangle = \lambda _jc_j\), as \(P\) is formally self-adjoint and \(\lambda _j\) is real. By Parseval’s identity (Theorem 30.3.6), \(\langle Pu, u\rangle _{L^2} = \sum _j\lambda _jc_j\bar c_j\).

(2) If \(u \perp u_1, \ldots , u_{k-1}\), then \(c_j = 0\) for \(j {\lt} k\), and \(\int \langle Pu, u\rangle = \sum _{j \geq k}\lambda _j|c_j|^2 \geq \lambda _k\sum _j|c_j|^2 = \lambda _k\| u\| ^2\), with equality if and only if \(c_j = 0\) whenever \(\lambda _j {\gt} \lambda _k\). For \(u = u_k\) equality holds.

(3) A \(k\)-dimensional \(V\) contains a nonzero \(u\) orthogonal to the \(k - 1\) vectors \(u_1, \ldots , u_{k-1}\) (the map \(V \to \mathbb {C}^{k-1}\), \(u \mapsto (\langle u, u_j\rangle )_j\), has a nonzero kernel), and \(\mathcal{R}(u) \geq \lambda _k\) by (2); so \(\max _V\mathcal{R} \geq \lambda _k\). For \(V = \operatorname {span}(u_1, \ldots , u_k)\), (1) gives \(\mathcal{R}(u) = \sum _{j \leq k}\lambda _j|c_j|^2/\sum _{j \leq k}|c_j|^2 \leq \lambda _k\).

The min-max principle compares spectra. If \(P^{\prime } = P + V\) with \(V\) a smooth self-adjoint endomorphism with \(V \geq c\), then \(\mathcal{R}^{\prime } \geq \mathcal{R} + c\) for every \(u\), so \(\lambda _k(P^{\prime }) \geq \lambda _k(P) + c\) for every \(k\): raising the potential raises every eigenvalue. Characterisation (3) does not involve the eigensections, which is what makes such comparisons possible.

034J Example 42.5.12

Wirtinger’s inequality. On \(S^1 = \mathbb {R}/2\pi \mathbb {Z}\), \(P = -d^2/d\theta ^2\) has eigenvalues \(0, 1, 1, 4, 4, 9, 9, \ldots \), with eigenfunctions \(1\), \(e^{\pm i\theta }\), \(e^{\pm 2i\theta }\), …, and \(\int \langle Pu, u\rangle = \int _0^{2\pi }|u^{\prime }|^2\) by integration by parts. Proposition 42.5.11(2) with \(k = 2\) says: for a smooth \(2\pi \)-periodic \(u\) with \(\int _0^{2\pi }u = 0\),

\[ \int _0^{2\pi }|u^{\prime }|^2\, d\theta \geq \int _0^{2\pi }|u|^2\, d\theta , \]

with equality exactly for \(u = ae^{i\theta } + be^{-i\theta }\). This sharp Poincaré inequality on the circle gives Hurwitz’s proof of the isoperimetric inequality (Exercise 42.6.9).

42.5.2 Counting eigenvalues034K

How fast do the eigenvalues tend to infinity? Let \(N(\lambda )\) be the number of eigenvalues \(\leq \lambda \), counted with multiplicity. For the torus everything is explicit.

034L Proposition 42.5.13 Weyl’s law on the torus

Let \(\Delta \) be the Laplacian on functions on the flat torus \(T^n = \mathbb {R}^n/\mathbb {Z}^n\), and \(\omega _n\) the volume of the unit ball of \(\mathbb {R}^n\). Then

\[ N(\lambda ) = \# \{ k \in \mathbb {Z}^n : 4\pi ^2|k|^2 \leq \lambda \} , \qquad \lim _{\lambda \to \infty }\frac{N(\lambda )}{\lambda ^{n/2}} = \frac{\omega _n}{(2\pi )^n}. \]
Proof â–¼

The functions \(e^{2\pi i\langle k, x\rangle }\), \(k \in \mathbb {Z}^n\), form an orthonormal basis of \(L^2(T^n)\) (the \(n\)-dimensional version of Example 30.3.7) of eigenfunctions with eigenvalues \(4\pi ^2|k|^2\), which gives the formula for \(N(\lambda )\). Let \(L(R) = \# \{ k \in \mathbb {Z}^n : |k| \leq R\} \), so \(N(\lambda ) = L(\sqrt\lambda /2\pi )\). The unit cubes \(k + [0, 1)^n\) are disjoint, have volume \(1\) and diameter \(\sqrt n\). Those with \(|k| \leq R\) lie in the ball of radius \(R + \sqrt n\); and every point \(y\) of the ball of radius \(R - \sqrt n\) lies in the cube of \(k = \lfloor y\rfloor \) (componentwise), with \(|k| \leq |y| + \sqrt n \leq R\). Comparing volumes,

\[ \omega _n(R - \sqrt n)^n \leq L(R) \leq \omega _n(R + \sqrt n)^n \qquad (R \geq \sqrt n), \]

so \(L(R)/(\omega _nR^n) \to 1\), and \(N(\lambda )/\lambda ^{n/2} \to \omega _n/(2\pi )^n\).

Equivalently, the \(k\)-th eigenvalue grows like \(\lambda _k \sim 4\pi ^2(k/\omega _n)^{2/n}\). The same law holds on every compact Riemannian manifold of dimension \(n\), with the volume entering: \(N(\lambda ) \sim \omega _n\operatorname {vol}(M)\lambda ^{n/2}/(2\pi )^n\) (Weyl’s law, see [Kac66]). So the spectrum “hears” the dimension and the volume of \(M\). Whether it determines \(M\) itself is Kac’s question “can one hear the shape of a drum?” [Kac66]; for manifolds the answer is negative, as Milnor found two flat tori of dimension \(16\) with the same spectrum that are not isometric [Mil64].
