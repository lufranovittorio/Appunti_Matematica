18.11 Function spaces03AZ

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

For spaces \(X\) and \(Y\) let \(C(X, Y)\) be the set of continuous maps \(X \to Y\). Many constructions treat such maps as points of a space: a homotopy is a path of maps, the loops in a space form a space, a sequence of functions converges to a function. Which topology should \(C(X, Y)\) carry? Two requirements are natural.

  • Evaluation should be continuous: if \(f\) is close to \(g\) and \(x\) is close to \(y\), then \(f(x)\) should be close to \(g(y)\).

  • Currying: a continuous map \(F \colon Z \times X \to Y\) of two variables is the same as a family of maps \(F(z, -) \colon X \to Y\) depending on the parameter \(z\), and this family should be a continuous map \(Z \to C(X, Y)\), and conversely.

The product topology of \(Y^X\) (the topology of pointwise convergence) is too coarse for this, and the topology of uniform convergence needs a metric on \(Y\). The right topology is the compact-open topology. We show that it satisfies both requirements when \(X\) is locally compact Hausdorff, that for metric \(Y\) it is the topology of uniform convergence on compact sets, and we characterise its compact subsets for compact \(X\) (the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem).

03B0Lean Definition 18.11.1

For \(K \subseteq X\) compact and \(U \subseteq Y\) open let

\[ V(K, U) = \{ f \in C(X, Y) : f(K) \subseteq U\} . \]

The compact-open topology on \(C(X, Y)\) is the topology generated by the sets \(V(K, U)\), that is, the coarsest topology in which they are open (Definition 18.2.9). Unless stated otherwise, \(C(X, Y)\) carries this topology.

So a basic open set is a finite intersection \(V(K_1, U_1) \cap \cdots \cap V(K_m, U_m)\): it consists of the maps that send each of finitely many compact sets \(K_i\) into a prescribed open set \(U_i\).

03B1 Example 18.11.2

  1. If \(X\) is discrete, its compact subsets are its finite subsets, every map is continuous, and \(V(K, U) = \bigcap _{x \in K}V(\{ x\} , U)\). The sets \(V(\{ x\} , U)\) are the subbasic open sets of the product topology, so \(C(X, Y) = Y^X\) with the product topology. For \(X = \{ 1, \ldots , n\} \) this is \(Y^n\).

  2. For any \(X\), the sets \(V(\{ x\} , U)\) are among the subbasic open sets, so the compact-open topology is finer than the topology of pointwise convergence, the subspace topology of \(Y^X\). In general it is strictly finer: see Example 18.11.5.

  3. If \(X\) is a point, \(C(X, Y) = Y\).

The first and most useful fact is that, for a metric target, the compact-open topology is a familiar topology of analysis.

03B2 Proposition 18.11.3

Let \((Y, d)\) be a metric space. For \(f \in C(X, Y)\), \(K \subseteq X\) compact and \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\) let

\[ B_K(f, \varepsilon ) = \Bigl\{ g \in C(X, Y) : \sup _{x \in K}d(f(x), g(x)) {\lt} \varepsilon \Bigr\} . \]

These sets form a basis of the compact-open topology. In other words, the compact-open topology is the topology of uniform convergence on compact sets: a sequence \(f_n\) converges to \(f\) if and only if \(\sup _{x \in K}d(f_n(x), f(x)) \to 0\) for every compact \(K \subseteq X\).

Proof â–¼

Note first that \(x \mapsto d(f(x), g(x))\) is continuous, so its supremum over a nonempty compact \(K\) is attained (Proposition 18.6.3(3)) and finite; for \(K = \emptyset \) the supremum is taken to be \(0\), and \(B_\emptyset (f, \varepsilon ) = C(X, Y)\). We prove two things: every set \(V(K, U)\) contains a set \(B_K(f, \varepsilon )\) around each of its points, and every set \(B_K(f, \varepsilon )\) contains a basic open set of the compact-open topology around each of its points. Together they show that both families generate the same topology and that the sets \(B_K(f, \varepsilon )\) form a basis (the intersection of \(B_K(f, \varepsilon )\) and \(B_L(f^{\prime }, \varepsilon ^{\prime })\) contains, around each of its points \(g\), the set \(B_{K \cup L}(g, \delta )\) for small \(\delta \), by the triangle inequality).

(a) Let \(f \in V(K, U)\) with \(K \neq \emptyset \). If \(U = Y\), then \(V(K, U) = C(X, Y)\). Otherwise the function \(x \mapsto d(f(x), Y \setminus U) = \inf _{y \notin U}d(f(x), y)\) is continuous (it is \(1\)-Lipschitz in \(f(x)\)) and positive on \(K\), since \(f(x)\) lies in the open set \(U\) and so a ball around \(f(x)\) misses \(Y \setminus U\). Its minimum \(\varepsilon \) on \(K\) is positive. If \(g \in B_K(f, \varepsilon )\) and \(x \in K\), then \(d(g(x), f(x)) {\lt} \varepsilon \leq d(f(x), Y \setminus U)\), so \(g(x) \in U\). Hence \(B_K(f, \varepsilon ) \subseteq V(K, U)\).

(b) Let \(g \in B_K(f, \varepsilon )\) and put \(\eta = \varepsilon - \sup _{x \in K}d(f(x), g(x)) {\gt} 0\); then \(B_K(g, \eta ) \subseteq B_K(f, \varepsilon )\) by the triangle inequality, so it suffices to find a basic open set \(W\) with \(g \in W \subseteq B_K(g, \eta )\). For \(x \in K\) let \(K_x = K \cap g^{-1}(\bar{B}(g(x), \eta /4))\), a closed subset of \(K\), hence compact (Proposition 18.6.3(1)). The sets \(K \cap g^{-1}(B(g(x), \eta /4))\) are open in \(K\) and cover it, so finitely many of them, for points \(x_1, \ldots , x_m\), cover \(K\); a fortiori \(K = K_{x_1} \cup \cdots \cup K_{x_m}\). Let

\[ W = V\bigl(K_{x_1}, B(g(x_1), \eta /2)\bigr) \cap \cdots \cap V\bigl(K_{x_m}, B(g(x_m), \eta /2)\bigr). \]

Then \(g \in W\), since \(g(K_{x_i}) \subseteq \bar{B}(g(x_i), \eta /4)\). If \(h \in W\) and \(y \in K\), choose \(i\) with \(y \in K_{x_i}\); then \(d(h(y), g(y)) \leq d(h(y), g(x_i)) + d(g(x_i), g(y)) {\lt} \eta /2 + \eta /4\). So \(\sup _{y \in K}d(h(y), g(y)) \leq 3\eta /4 {\lt} \eta \), and \(h \in B_K(g, \eta )\).

The statement about sequences follows: \(f_n \to f\) if and only if every basic neighbourhood \(B_K(f, \varepsilon )\) eventually contains \(f_n\).

03B3 Corollary 18.11.4

If \(X\) is compact and \((Y, d)\) is a metric space, the compact-open topology on \(C(X, Y)\) is the topology of the uniform metric \(d_\infty (f, g) = \sup _{x \in X}d(f(x), g(x))\).

Proof â–¼

The supremum is finite, as in the proof of Proposition 18.11.3, and \(d_\infty \) is a metric. Its balls are the sets \(B_X(f, \varepsilon )\), and \(B_K(f, \varepsilon ) \supseteq B_X(f, \varepsilon )\) for every compact \(K \subseteq X\); so the sets \(B_X(f, \varepsilon )\) alone form a basis of the topology of Proposition 18.11.3.

03B4 Example 18.11.5

Let \(f_n \in C([0, 1], \mathbb {R})\) be the “moving tent”: \(f_n\) is \(0\) outside \([0, 2/n]\), \(f_n(1/n) = 1\), and \(f_n\) is linear on \([0, 1/n]\) and on \([1/n, 2/n]\). For every \(x\), \(f_n(x) = 0\) as soon as \(2/n {\lt} x\), and \(f_n(0) = 0\); so \(f_n \to 0\) pointwise. But \(d_\infty (f_n, 0) = 1\) for all \(n\), so \(f_n\) does not converge to \(0\) in the compact-open topology. On the non-compact space \(X = \mathbb {R}\), the functions \(g_n\) that are tents of height \(1\) over \([n, n + 2]\) converge to \(0\) uniformly on compact sets, hence in the compact-open topology, although \(\sup _{x \in \mathbb {R}}|g_n(x)| = 1\): for non-compact \(X\) the compact-open topology is coarser than the topology of uniform convergence on all of \(X\).

We now turn to the two requirements of the beginning. Both use local compactness.

03B5Lean Proposition 18.11.6

Let \(X\) be locally compact Hausdorff and \(Y\) any space. The evaluation map

\[ \mathrm{ev} \colon C(X, Y) \times X \to Y, \qquad (f, x) \mapsto f(x), \]

is continuous. For arbitrary \(X\), the evaluation \(\mathrm{ev}_x \colon C(X, Y) \to Y\) at a fixed point \(x\) is continuous.

Proof â–¼

Let \((f, x) \in C(X, Y) \times X\) and let \(U\) be an open neighbourhood of \(f(x)\). The set \(f^{-1}(U)\) is an open neighbourhood of \(x\), so by Lemma 18.9.5 (with \(K = \{ x\} \)) there is an open \(W \ni x\) with compact closure \(\overline{W} \subseteq f^{-1}(U)\). Then \(V(\overline{W}, U) \times W\) is an open neighbourhood of \((f, x)\), and for \((g, y)\) in it, \(g(y) \in g(\overline{W}) \subseteq U\). So \(\mathrm{ev}\) is continuous at \((f, x)\). For the second statement, \(\mathrm{ev}_x^{-1}(U) = V(\{ x\} , U)\) is open.

03B6Lean Theorem 18.11.7 Exponential law

Let \(X\), \(Y\), \(Z\) be spaces. For a map \(F \colon Z \times X \to Y\) let \(\hat{F} \colon Z \to Y^X\) be \(\hat{F}(z) = F(z, -)\).

  1. If \(F\) is continuous, then \(\hat{F}(z) \in C(X, Y)\) for every \(z\), and \(\hat{F} \colon Z \to C(X, Y)\) is continuous.

  2. If \(X\) is locally compact Hausdorff, then conversely, for every continuous \(G \colon Z \to C(X, Y)\) the map \(Z \times X \to Y\), \((z, x) \mapsto G(z)(x)\), is continuous.

Hence, for \(X\) locally compact Hausdorff, \(F \mapsto \hat{F}\) is a bijection \(C(Z \times X, Y) \to C(Z, C(X, Y))\).

Proof â–¼

(1) \(\hat{F}(z)\) is the composite of the continuous maps \(x \mapsto (z, x)\) and \(F\). To show that \(\hat{F}\) is continuous it suffices to show that the preimages of the subbasic sets \(V(K, U)\) are open (Proposition 18.2.13). Let \(z \in \hat{F}^{-1}(V(K, U))\), that is, \(F(\{ z\} \times K) \subseteq U\). The open set \(F^{-1}(U) \subseteq Z \times X\) contains \(\{ z\} \times K\). For each \(x \in K\) choose a basic open set \(A_x \times B_x \subseteq F^{-1}(U)\) containing \((z, x)\); finitely many \(B_{x_1}, \ldots , B_{x_m}\) cover \(K\), and \(A = A_{x_1} \cap \cdots \cap A_{x_m}\) is an open neighbourhood of \(z\) with \(A \times K \subseteq F^{-1}(U)\) (this is the tube lemma of the proof of Proposition 18.6.4). So \(\hat{F}(A) \subseteq V(K, U)\), and \(\hat{F}^{-1}(V(K, U))\) is open.

(2) The map is the composite of \(G \times \mathrm{id}_X \colon Z \times X \to C(X, Y) \times X\) and the evaluation map, which is continuous by Proposition 18.11.6.

The last statement: \(F \mapsto \hat{F}\) is injective, since \(F(z, x) = \hat{F}(z)(x)\), and by (2) every continuous \(G\) is \(\hat{F}\) for the continuous map \(F(z, x) = G(z)(x)\).

The name comes from writing \(Y^X\) for \(C(X, Y)\): the theorem says \(Y^{Z \times X} = (Y^X)^Z\), as for exponentials of numbers or of sets (where \(Y^{Z \times X}\) and \((Y^X)^Z\) are in bijection for all sets). In the language of Chapter 6, for \(X\) locally compact Hausdorff the functor \(- \times X\) on topological spaces is left adjoint to \(C(X, -)\).

03B7 Example 18.11.8

  1. Homotopies as paths. A homotopy \(H \colon X \times [0, 1] \to Y\) is the same as a continuous map \(X \to C([0, 1], Y)\), for every space \(X\), since \([0, 1]\) is compact Hausdorff (apply the theorem with \(Z = X\) and the roles of the factors exchanged). And if \(X\) is locally compact Hausdorff, a homotopy is also the same as a path \([0, 1] \to C(X, Y)\), \(t \mapsto H(-, t)\); so homotopy classes of maps \(X \to Y\) are the path components of \(C(X, Y)\). This is the starting point of the homotopy theory of Chapter 19, where the space \(C([0, 1], Y)\) of paths and its subspace of loops at a point play a central role.

  2. Continuity of composition. For any space \(X\) and a locally compact Hausdorff space \(Y\), composition \(C(Y, W) \times C(X, Y) \to C(X, W)\) is continuous (Exercise 18.11.14). Without local compactness this can fail, which is one reason why algebraic topology often restricts to locally compact spaces or modifies the topology of \(C(X, Y)\).

We come to the compact subsets of \(C(X, Y)\). Bounded sequences of real numbers have convergent subsequences, but bounded sequences of continuous functions need not: on \([0, 1]\) the functions \(x^n\) take values in \([0, 1]\), but converge pointwise to a function that is \(0\) on \([0, 1)\) and \(1\) at \(1\), which is not continuous, so no subsequence converges uniformly (Proposition 18.3.10). What goes wrong is that the graphs become steeper and steeper near \(1\). The following notion forbids this.

03B8 Definition 18.11.9

Let \(X\) be a space and \((Y, d)\) a metric space. A family \(\mathcal{F} \subseteq C(X, Y)\) is equicontinuous at \(x \in X\) if for every \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\) there is a neighbourhood \(W\) of \(x\) such that \(d(f(x^{\prime }), f(x)) {\lt} \varepsilon \) for all \(x^{\prime } \in W\) and all \(f \in \mathcal{F}\). It is equicontinuous if it is equicontinuous at every point.

Continuity of each \(f\) gives a neighbourhood \(W\) depending on \(f\); equicontinuity asks for one neighbourhood that works for all members of the family. For example, a family of maps between metric spaces that are Lipschitz with a common constant \(L\) is equicontinuous (take \(W = B(x, \varepsilon /L)\)), while the family \(\{ x^n : n \in \mathbb {N}\} \) on \([0, 1]\) is not equicontinuous at \(1\): for every \(\delta {\gt} 0\), \(1 - (1 - \delta /2)^n {\gt} 1/2\) for large \(n\).

03B9 Lemma 18.11.10

Let \(X\) be a space, \((Y, d)\) a metric space and \(\mathcal{F} \subseteq C(X, Y)\) equicontinuous.

  1. The closure \(\overline{\mathcal{F}}\) of \(\mathcal{F}\) in \(Y^X\), with the product topology, consists of continuous maps and is equicontinuous.

  2. If \(X\) is compact, the topology of pointwise convergence and the uniform topology coincide on \(\mathcal{F}\).

Proof â–¼

(1) Let \(x \in X\) and \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\), and let \(W\) be a neighbourhood of \(x\) with \(d(f(x^{\prime }), f(x)) {\lt} \varepsilon /3\) for \(x^{\prime } \in W\) and \(f \in \mathcal{F}\). Let \(g \in \overline{\mathcal{F}}\) and \(x^{\prime } \in W\). The set of \(h \in Y^X\) with \(d(h(x), g(x)) {\lt} \varepsilon /3\) and \(d(h(x^{\prime }), g(x^{\prime })) {\lt} \varepsilon /3\) is open in the product topology and contains \(g\), so it contains some \(f \in \mathcal{F}\). Then

\[ d(g(x^{\prime }), g(x)) \leq d(g(x^{\prime }), f(x^{\prime })) + d(f(x^{\prime }), f(x)) + d(f(x), g(x)) {\lt} \varepsilon . \]

So \(\overline{\mathcal{F}}\) is equicontinuous at \(x\) with the same \(W\); in particular each \(g \in \overline{\mathcal{F}}\) is continuous.

(2) The uniform topology is the compact-open one (Corollary 18.11.4), which is finer than the pointwise one (Example 18.11.2(2)). Conversely, let \(g \in \mathcal{F}\) and \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\). For each \(x \in X\) choose an open \(W_x \ni x\) with \(d(h(x^{\prime }), h(x)) {\lt} \varepsilon /4\) for all \(x^{\prime } \in W_x\) and \(h \in \mathcal{F}\). Finitely many \(W_{x_1}, \ldots , W_{x_m}\) cover \(X\). If \(h \in \mathcal{F}\) satisfies \(d(h(x_i), g(x_i)) {\lt} \varepsilon /4\) for \(i = 1, \ldots , m\), a condition defining a neighbourhood of \(g\) in the pointwise topology, then for \(x \in W_{x_i}\),

\[ d(h(x), g(x)) \leq d(h(x), h(x_i)) + d(h(x_i), g(x_i)) + d(g(x_i), g(x)) {\lt} 3\varepsilon /4, \]

so \(d_\infty (h, g) \leq 3\varepsilon /4 {\lt} \varepsilon \). Hence every uniform ball around \(g\) contains, within \(\mathcal{F}\), a pointwise neighbourhood of \(g\).

03BALean Theorem 18.11.11 Arzelà–Ascoli

Let \(X\) be a compact space and \((Y, d)\) a metric space. A subset \(\mathcal{F} \subseteq C(X, Y)\) has compact closure in \(C(X, Y)\), with the uniform metric, if and only if

  1. \(\mathcal{F}\) is equicontinuous, and

  2. for every \(x \in X\) the set \(\mathcal{F}(x) = \{ f(x) : f \in \mathcal{F}\} \) has compact closure in \(Y\).

Proof â–¼

Suppose (1) and (2). Let \(\mathcal{G}\) be the closure of \(\mathcal{F}\) in \(Y^X\) with the product topology. It is contained in the closed set \(\prod _{x \in X}\overline{\mathcal{F}(x)}\), which is compact by (2) and Tychonoff’s theorem (Theorem 18.6.8); so \(\mathcal{G}\), a closed subset of a compact space, is compact in the pointwise topology. By Lemma 18.11.10(1), \(\mathcal{G} \subseteq C(X, Y)\) and \(\mathcal{G}\) is equicontinuous, so by Lemma 18.11.10(2), applied to \(\mathcal{G}\), it is compact also in the uniform topology. A compact subset of the metric space \((C(X, Y), d_\infty )\) is closed (Proposition 18.6.3(2)); so the closure of \(\mathcal{F}\) in the uniform topology is contained in \(\mathcal{G}\), and it is compact, being a closed subset of a compact space.

Conversely, suppose that the closure \(\overline{\mathcal{F}}\) of \(\mathcal{F}\) in \((C(X, Y), d_\infty )\) is compact. The evaluation \(\mathrm{ev}_x\) is continuous (Proposition 18.11.6), so \(\mathrm{ev}_x(\overline{\mathcal{F}})\) is a compact subset of \(Y\) containing \(\mathcal{F}(x)\); it is closed, so it contains \(\overline{\mathcal{F}(x)}\), which is therefore compact: this is (2). For (1), let \(x \in X\) and \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\). The compact metric space \(\overline{\mathcal{F}}\) is totally bounded (Theorem 18.7.4), so there are \(f_1, \ldots , f_m \in \overline{\mathcal{F}}\) such that every \(f \in \mathcal{F}\) has \(d_\infty (f, f_i) {\lt} \varepsilon /3\) for some \(i\). Let \(W\) be a neighbourhood of \(x\) with \(d(f_i(x^{\prime }), f_i(x)) {\lt} \varepsilon /3\) for \(x^{\prime } \in W\) and \(i = 1, \ldots , m\). For \(f \in \mathcal{F}\) and \(x^{\prime } \in W\), choosing \(i\) as above, \(d(f(x^{\prime }), f(x)) \leq d(f(x^{\prime }), f_i(x^{\prime })) + d(f_i(x^{\prime }), f_i(x)) + d(f_i(x), f(x)) {\lt} \varepsilon \).

For \(Y = \mathbb {R}^m\), condition (2) says that \(\mathcal{F}\) is pointwise bounded, by the Heine–Borel theorem (Theorem 18.6.5); this is the classical form of the theorem, proved again by a different method in Chapter 27, where it is used for differential equations and in complex analysis. Since \((C(X, Y), d_\infty )\) is a metric space, compact closure means that every sequence in \(\mathcal{F}\) has a uniformly convergent subsequence (Theorem 18.7.4).

03BB Example 18.11.12

  1. Let \(X\) and \(Y\) be compact metric spaces and \(L {\gt} 0\). The set of maps \(X \to Y\) that are Lipschitz with constant \(L\) is compact in \(C(X, Y)\): it is equicontinuous, condition (2) holds because \(Y\) is compact, and it is closed, since a uniform (even pointwise) limit of \(L\)-Lipschitz maps is \(L\)-Lipschitz.

  2. The family \(\{ \sin (nx) : n \in \mathbb {N}\} \) in \(C([0, \pi ], \mathbb {R})\) is bounded but has no uniformly convergent subsequence. Indeed it is not equicontinuous at \(0\): \(\sin (n \cdot \pi /(2n)) = 1\) while \(\sin (0) = 0\), and \(\pi /(2n) \to 0\). Here the theorem is used in the direction “compact closure implies equicontinuous”.

03BC Exercise 18.11.13

Show that if \(Y\) is Hausdorff, then so is \(C(X, Y)\) with the compact-open topology.

Solution â–¼

Let \(f \neq g\), and choose \(x\) with \(f(x) \neq g(x)\) and disjoint open sets \(U \ni f(x)\) and \(U^{\prime } \ni g(x)\). Then \(V(\{ x\} , U)\) and \(V(\{ x\} , U^{\prime })\) are disjoint open neighbourhoods of \(f\) and \(g\): a map in both would send \(x\) into \(U \cap U^{\prime } = \emptyset \).

03BD Exercise 18.11.14

Let \(X\) be any space and \(Y\) a locally compact Hausdorff space. Show that composition \(C(Y, W) \times C(X, Y) \to C(X, W)\), \((g, f) \mapsto g \circ f\), is continuous.

Solution â–¼

Let \(g \circ f \in V(K, U)\), with \(K \subseteq X\) compact and \(U \subseteq W\) open. Then \(f(K)\) is compact and contained in the open set \(g^{-1}(U)\). By Lemma 18.9.5 there is an open \(A\) with compact closure such that \(f(K) \subseteq A \subseteq \overline{A} \subseteq g^{-1}(U)\). Then \(V(\overline{A}, U) \times V(K, A)\) is an open neighbourhood of \((g, f)\), and for \((g^{\prime }, f^{\prime })\) in it, \(g^{\prime }(f^{\prime }(K)) \subseteq g^{\prime }(A) \subseteq U\). So composition maps this neighbourhood into \(V(K, U)\), and the preimages of subbasic open sets are open.
