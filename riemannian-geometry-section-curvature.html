40.8 Curvature01CG

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

01CH Definition 40.8.1

The Riemann curvature tensor is the curvature of the Levi-Civita connection (Proposition 38.4.3), \(R(X, Y)Z = \nabla _X\nabla _YZ - \nabla _Y\nabla _XZ - \nabla _{[X,Y]}Z\), a tensor field of type \((1,3)\) (Definition 35.3.8). Lowering its index with the metric (Definition 12.4.10) gives the tensor field \(R(X, Y, Z, W) = g(R(X, Y)Z, W)\) of type \((0,4)\), also denoted \(R\). For a plane \(\sigma \subseteq T_pM\) with orthonormal basis \(u, v\), the sectional curvature is \(K(\sigma ) = R(u, v, v, u)\). The Ricci tensor is the contraction \(\mathrm{Ric}(X, Y) = \operatorname {tr}(Z \mapsto R(Z, X)Y)\) (Definition 12.4.7), a symmetric tensor field of type \((0,2)\) (Proposition 40.8.2); for an orthonormal frame \(\mathrm{Ric}(X, Y) = \sum _iR(e_i, X, Y, e_i)\). The scalar curvature is the trace \(s = \operatorname {tr}_g\mathrm{Ric}\) (Definition 12.4.10), which is \(\sum _i\mathrm{Ric}(e_i, e_i)\) for an orthonormal frame (Proposition 12.4.11).

01CI Proposition 40.8.2

\(R\) is tensorial and satisfies \(R(X, Y, Z, W) = -R(Y, X, Z, W) = -R(X, Y, W, Z) = R(Z, W, X, Y)\) and the first Bianchi identity \(R(X, Y)Z + R(Y, Z)X + R(Z, X)Y = 0\). The sectional curvatures determine \(R\).

Proof â–¼

Tensoriality and the first antisymmetry are general facts about curvature. The second follows by polarising \(R(X, Y, Z, Z) = 0\), which holds because the connection is metric: \(0 = XY g(Z,Z)/2 - \cdots \) expands to \(g(R(X,Y)Z, Z)\). The first Bianchi identity follows from torsion-freeness and the Jacobi identity, and the symmetry in the pairs is a formal consequence of the other three, obtained by adding the Bianchi identities for the four cyclic permutations. The last statement is the standard polarisation argument [dC92, Chapter 4].

01CJ Example 40.8.3

Euclidean space has \(R = 0\); the round sphere of radius \(1\) has constant sectional curvature \(1\) and \(\mathrm{Ric} = (n-1)g\); hyperbolic space has constant curvature \(-1\). A Lie group with a bi-invariant metric has \(R(X, Y)Z = \frac14[[X, Y], Z]\) for left-invariant fields, hence non-negative sectional curvature. A metric has constant sectional curvature \(\kappa \) if and only if \(R(X, Y)Z = \kappa (\langle Y, Z\rangle X - \langle X, Z\rangle Y)\), since the right-hand side has the symmetries of Proposition 40.8.2 and sectional curvature \(\kappa \).

02WQ Proposition 40.8.4

Let \((M, g)\) have dimension \(n\) and constant sectional curvature \(\kappa \). Then

\[ \mathrm{Ric} = (n - 1)\kappa \, g, \qquad s = n(n - 1)\kappa . \]

For instance the unit sphere has \(\mathrm{Ric} = (n - 1)g\) and \(s = n(n - 1)\), and hyperbolic space has \(\mathrm{Ric} = -(n - 1)g\). Every surface (\(n = 2\)), constant curvature or not, has \(\mathrm{Ric} = Kg\) and \(s = 2K\), where \(K\) is the Gaussian curvature.

Proof â–¼

Let \((e_i)\) be an orthonormal basis of \(T_pM\). By the formula of Example 40.8.3,

\[ \mathrm{Ric}(X, Y) = \sum _i\langle R(e_i, X)Y, e_i\rangle = \kappa \sum _i\bigl(\langle X, Y\rangle \langle e_i, e_i\rangle - \langle e_i, Y\rangle \langle X, e_i\rangle \bigr) = \kappa \bigl(n\langle X, Y\rangle - \langle X, Y\rangle \bigr), \]

using \(\sum _i\langle e_i, Y\rangle \langle X, e_i\rangle = \langle X, Y\rangle \) (expand \(X\) in the basis). Taking the trace once more gives \(s = \sum _i(n - 1)\kappa = n(n - 1)\kappa \). For a surface, the only plane in \(T_pM\) is \(T_pM\) itself, so the sectional curvature is constant on each tangent space, equal to \(K(p)\), and the computation applies pointwise with \(n = 2\).

02WR Remark 40.8.5

The sphere of radius \(r\) has constant curvature \(1/r^2\) (Section 40.9 below), so its Ricci tensor is \(\frac{n-1}{r^2}g\). In a space of constant curvature \(\kappa \), the Ricci curvature in the direction of a unit vector \(v\) is \(\mathrm{Ric}(v, v) = (n - 1)\kappa \): it is the sum of the \(n - 1\) sectional curvatures of the planes spanned by \(v\) and the vectors of an orthonormal basis of \(v^\perp \), each equal to \(\kappa \). A metric with \(\mathrm{Ric} = \lambda g\) for a constant \(\lambda \) is called Einstein; so constant curvature metrics are Einstein. The converse fails in general: for the product \(S^2 \times S^2\) of two unit spheres, the Koszul formula shows that the Levi-Civita connection is the product of the connections of the factors, so the curvature tensor is the sum of the curvature tensors of the factors. Hence a plane spanned by a vector tangent to the first factor and one tangent to the second has sectional curvature \(0\), while \(\mathrm{Ric} = g\), each factor contributing \(\mathrm{Ric} = g\) on its own tangent vectors. In dimension \(2\) the Ricci tensor carries exactly the same information as the Gaussian curvature.

0285 Lemma 40.8.6

For \(f\) as in Lemma 40.4.3 and a vector field \(V\) along \(f\), \(D_sD_tV - D_tD_sV = R(\partial _sf, \partial _tf)V\).

Proof â–¼

Write \(V = V^k(\partial _k \circ f)\). In \(D_sD_tV\) the terms containing derivatives of the \(V^k\) are symmetric in \(s\) and \(t\), so the left side is \(V^k(D_sD_t - D_tD_s)(\partial _k \circ f)\). Now \(D_t(\partial _k \circ f) = \partial _tf^i(\nabla _{\partial _i}\partial _k) \circ f\), and \(D_sD_t(\partial _k \circ f) = \partial _s\partial _tf^i(\nabla _{\partial _i}\partial _k) \circ f + \partial _tf^i\partial _sf^j(\nabla _{\partial _j}\nabla _{\partial _i}\partial _k) \circ f\). Antisymmetrising and using \([\partial _i, \partial _j] = 0\) gives \(\partial _sf^j\partial _tf^iR(\partial _j, \partial _i)\partial _k = R(\partial _sf, \partial _tf)\partial _k\).

0286 Definition 40.8.7

A Jacobi field along a geodesic \(\gamma \) is a vector field \(J\) along \(\gamma \) with \(D_t^2J + R(J, \dot\gamma )\dot\gamma = 0\). A point \(\gamma (b)\) is conjugate to \(\gamma (a)\) along \(\gamma \) if there is a nonzero Jacobi field vanishing at \(a\) and \(b\).

The Jacobi equation is a linear second order equation, so a Jacobi field is determined by \(J(a)\) and \(D_tJ(a)\), and the Jacobi fields along \(\gamma \) form a vector space of dimension \(2n\).

0287 Proposition 40.8.8

  1. If \(f(s, t)\) is a family of geodesics \(t \mapsto f(s, t)\), then \(J = \partial _sf(0, -)\) is a Jacobi field along \(\gamma = f(0, -)\).

  2. For \(v \in \mathcal{E}_p\) and \(w \in T_pM\), \(J(t) = d_{tv}\exp _p(tw)\) is the Jacobi field along \(\gamma (t) = \exp _p(tv)\) with \(J(0) = 0\) and \(D_tJ(0) = w\).

  3. \(d_v\exp _p\) is singular if and only if \(\exp _p(v)\) is conjugate to \(p\) along \(t \mapsto \exp _p(tv)\), \(t \in [0, 1]\).

Proof â–¼

(1) With \(T = \partial _tf\) and \(S = \partial _sf\), Lemmas 40.4.3 and 40.8.6 give \(D_tD_tS = D_tD_sT = D_sD_tT + R(T, S)T = R(T, S)T = -R(S, T)T\), since \(D_tT = 0\). (2) Apply (1) to \(f(s, t) = \exp _p(t(v + sw))\): \(\partial _sf(0, t) = d_{tv}\exp _p(tw)\). It vanishes at \(t = 0\), and \(D_tS(0, 0) = D_sT(0, 0) = \frac{d}{ds}(v + sw) = w\), the covariant derivative along the constant curve \(s \mapsto p\) being the ordinary derivative in \(T_pM\). (3) By (2) and uniqueness, the Jacobi fields vanishing at \(0\) are exactly the \(d_{tv}\exp _p(tw)\), and at \(t = 1\) this is \(d_v\exp _p(w)\).

0288 Example 40.8.9

If \(g\) has constant sectional curvature \(\kappa \) and \(\gamma \) has unit speed, then for a parallel unit field \(E \perp \dot\gamma \), \(R(E, \dot\gamma )\dot\gamma = \kappa E\) by Example 40.8.3, and the Jacobi fields normal to \(\gamma \) with \(J(0) = 0\) are \(J(t) = s_\kappa (t)E(t)\) with \(s_\kappa (t) = t\), \(\sin (\sqrt\kappa t)/\sqrt\kappa \) or \(\sinh (\sqrt{-\kappa }t)/\sqrt{-\kappa }\) for \(\kappa = 0\), \({\gt} 0\), \({\lt} 0\). On the unit sphere they vanish again at \(t = \pi \): antipodal points are conjugate, which explains Remark 40.6.13. For \(\kappa \leq 0\) there are no conjugate points.

0289 Theorem 40.8.10 Cartan–Hadamard

Let \((M, g)\) be complete and connected with sectional curvature \(K \leq 0\). Then for every \(p\), \(\exp _p \colon T_pM \to M\) is a covering map; if \(M\) is simply connected, it is a diffeomorphism, so \(M\) is diffeomorphic to \(\mathbb {R}^n\).

Sketch of proof â–¼

Let \(J\) be a Jacobi field along a geodesic \(\gamma \) with \(J(0) = 0\) and \(D_tJ(0) \neq 0\), and \(\phi = |J|^2\). Then \(\phi '' = 2|D_tJ|^2 - 2\langle R(J, \dot\gamma )\dot\gamma , J\rangle = 2|D_tJ|^2 - 2K(J, \dot\gamma )(|J|^2|\dot\gamma |^2 - \langle J, \dot\gamma \rangle ^2) \geq 0\), where \(K(J, \dot\gamma )\) is the sectional curvature of the plane they span (if they are independent). So \(\phi \) is convex with \(\phi (0) = \phi '(0) = 0\) and \(\phi ''(0) {\gt} 0\), hence positive for \(t {\gt} 0\): there are no conjugate points, and \(\exp _p\) is a local diffeomorphism on \(T_pM\) (defined everywhere by Theorem 40.6.8) by Proposition 40.8.8(3). The omitted verification is that a local diffeomorphism from a complete Riemannian manifold (here \(T_pM\) with the pulled back metric, whose radial lines are geodesics) that is a local isometry is a covering map [dC92, Chapter 7].

02DH Theorem 40.8.11 Gauss–Bonnet

Let \(M\) be a compact oriented Riemannian surface, with Gaussian curvature \(K\) (the sectional curvature of its tangent planes) and area form \(dA\) (Proposition 40.3.3). Then

\[ \int _MK\, dA = 2\pi \chi (M) . \]
Proof â–¼

Let \(J\) be the rotation by \(+\pi /2\) in each tangent plane, for the metric and the orientation: \(Je_1 = e_2\) for every oriented orthonormal frame \((e_1, e_2)\). It is a smooth endomorphism with \(J^2 = -1\), so \(TM\) becomes a complex line bundle, with \(i\) acting as \(J\), whose underlying oriented real bundle is \(TM\). Near a point choose an oriented orthonormal frame \((e_1, e_2)\) and define the \(1\)-form \(\omega \) by \(\nabla _Xe_1 = \omega (X)e_2\); since \(\nabla \) is metric, \(\langle \nabla _Xe_1, e_1\rangle = 0\) and \(\nabla _Xe_2 = -\omega (X)e_1\). Hence \(\nabla J = J\nabla \), so \(\nabla \) is a connection on the complex line bundle, and in the complex frame \(e_1\) it reads \(\nabla e_1 = \omega \otimes Je_1 = i\omega \otimes e_1\): its connection form is \(\theta = i\omega \) and its curvature is \(F = d\theta = i\, d\omega \) (Proposition 38.4.3).

We compare \(d\omega \) with \(K\). From the definition of the curvature tensor,

\[ R(X, Y)e_1 = \nabla _X(\omega (Y)e_2) - \nabla _Y(\omega (X)e_2) - \omega ([X, Y])e_2 = \big(X\omega (Y) - Y\omega (X) - \omega ([X, Y])\big)e_2 = d\omega (X, Y)\, e_2, \]

the terms \(\mp \omega (X)\omega (Y)e_1\) cancelling. By Definition 40.8.1 and Proposition 40.8.2, \(K = R(e_1, e_2, e_2, e_1) = -R(e_1, e_2, e_1, e_2) = -\langle R(e_1, e_2)e_1, e_2\rangle = -d\omega (e_1, e_2)\). So \(d\omega = -K\, e^1 \wedge e^2 = -K\, dA\) and \(F = -iK\, dA\).

By Chern–Weil theory (Theorem 39.2.4 and Definition 39.2.5), \(c_1(TM)\) is represented by \(\frac{i}{2\pi }F = \frac{1}{2\pi }K\, dA\) for this connection. For a complex line bundle the Euler class of the underlying oriented real bundle is \(c_1\) (Theorem 39.5.1(2)), and \(\int _Me(TM) = \chi (M)\) by Theorem 39.5.1(3). Hence \(\frac{1}{2\pi }\int _MK\, dA = \chi (M)\).

02DI Corollary 40.8.12

Let \(\Sigma _g\) be the closed orientable surface of genus \(g\), so that \(\chi (\Sigma _g) = 2 - 2g\) (Example 21.7.5).

  1. The integral of the Gaussian curvature does not depend on the metric: for the round unit sphere, \(K = 1\) and the area is \(4\pi = 2\pi \cdot 2\).

  2. If \(\Sigma _g\) carries a metric with \(K {\gt} 0\) everywhere, then \(g = 0\); if it carries a metric with \(K \geq 0\) everywhere, then \(g \leq 1\); if it carries one with \(K {\lt} 0\) everywhere, then \(g \geq 2\).

  3. The torus admits flat metrics, as \(\mathbb {R}^2/\mathbb {Z}^2\), but no metric with \(K {\gt} 0\) everywhere or \(K {\lt} 0\) everywhere.

Proof â–¼

(1) is Theorem 40.8.11. (2) The sign of \(\int _MK\, dA = 2\pi (2 - 2g)\) is positive, non-negative or negative accordingly, and \(2 - 2g {\gt} 0\) forces \(g = 0\), \(2 - 2g \geq 0\) forces \(g \leq 1\), and \(2 - 2g {\lt} 0\) forces \(g \geq 2\). (3) The torus has \(\chi = 0\), so \(K\) cannot have constant nonzero sign, while the flat metric induced from \(\mathbb {R}^2\) has \(K = 0\) (Example 40.8.3).
