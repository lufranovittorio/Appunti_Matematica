28.2 Measures012Z

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

Why \(\sigma \)-algebras. We want to assign a size to subsets of \(\mathbb {R}\) extending the length of intervals, additive on disjoint sets, and invariant under translation. Two features of the definition below need explanation. First, additivity is required for countable disjoint unions, not only finite ones: this is what makes limits work (the convergence theorems of Section 28.4), and it is natural, since for instance \([0, 1) = \bigsqcup _n[1 - 2^{-n}, 1 - 2^{-n-1})\) should have length \(\sum _n2^{-n-1} = 1\). Second, the size is defined only on a family of “measurable” sets, not on all subsets: by the Vitali construction (Counterexample 28.3.7) there is no countably additive, translation invariant length defined on all subsets of \(\mathbb {R}\). The family of measurable sets must be closed under the operations used in analysis, complements and countable unions; this is a \(\sigma \)-algebra.

0130 Definition 28.2.1

A \(\sigma \)-algebra on a set \(X\) is a set \(\mathcal{A}\) of subsets containing \(X\) and closed under complements and countable unions. The \(\sigma \)-algebra generated by a family of subsets is the intersection of all \(\sigma \)-algebras containing it; for a topological space, the Borel \(\sigma \)-algebra \(\mathcal{B}(X)\) is generated by the open sets. A measure on \((X, \mathcal{A})\) is a map \(\mu \colon \mathcal{A} \to [0, \infty ]\) with \(\mu (\emptyset ) = 0\) and \(\mu (\bigsqcup _k A_k) = \sum _k\mu (A_k)\) for countable disjoint families. It is finite if \(\mu (X) {\lt} \infty \), \(\sigma \)-finite if \(X\) is a countable union of sets of finite measure, and complete if subsets of measure zero sets are measurable. A property holds almost everywhere (a.e.) if it holds outside a set of measure zero.

02WD Example 28.2.2

  1. On any set \(X\), \(\{ \emptyset , X\} \) and the power set \(\mathcal{P}(X)\) are \(\sigma \)-algebras, the smallest and the largest. If \(X = A_1 \sqcup \cdots \sqcup A_k\) is a finite partition, the unions of some of the \(A_i\) form a \(\sigma \)-algebra with \(2^k\) elements; on a finite set every \(\sigma \)-algebra is of this form, generated by its minimal nonempty members.

  2. The sets that are countable or have countable complement form a \(\sigma \)-algebra on any set: complements exchange the two kinds, and a countable union is countable unless one of the sets is cocountable, in which case the union is cocountable. On \(\mathbb {R}\) it is strictly smaller than the Borel \(\sigma \)-algebra, since \([0, 1]\) is neither countable nor cocountable.

  3. The Borel \(\sigma \)-algebra of \(\mathbb {R}\) contains the open sets, the closed sets, the countable unions of closed sets (such as \(\mathbb {Q}\) or \([0, 1)\)) and the countable intersections of open sets (such as \(\mathbb {R}\setminus \mathbb {Q}\)), and much more; it is generated already by the intervals \((a, b)\), or by the rays \((a, \infty )\).

02WE Example 28.2.3

  1. Counting measure on \(\mathcal{P}(X)\): \(\mu (A)\) is the number of elements of \(A\), or \(\infty \). It is \(\sigma \)-finite if and only if \(X\) is countable.

  2. Dirac measure at \(x\): \(\delta _x(A) = 1\) if \(x \in A\) and \(0\) otherwise; more generally \(\sum _ic_i\delta _{x_i}\) with \(c_i \geq 0\), a discrete measure. A fair die is the probability measure \(\frac16\sum _{k=1}^6\delta _k\) on \(\{ 1, \ldots , 6\} \), and the probability of an even result is \(\mu (\{ 2, 4, 6\} ) = \frac12\).

  3. If \(\mu \) is a measure and \(E\) a measurable set, \(\mu _E(A) = \mu (A \cap E)\) is a measure, the restriction of \(\mu \) to \(E\); and if \(f \geq 0\) is measurable, \(A \mapsto \int _Af\, d\mu \) is a measure (Section 28.4), the measure with density \(f\).

  4. Lebesgue measure on \(\mathbb {R}^n\) (Section 28.3), which assigns to boxes their volume.

Countable additivity forces continuity properties that finite additivity does not give (Proposition 28.2.4 below). For instance, for counting measure on \(\mathbb {N}\) the sets \(A_n = \{ n, n + 1, \ldots \} \) decrease to \(\emptyset \) while \(\mu (A_n) = \infty \) for all \(n\): the hypothesis \(\mu (A_1) {\lt} \infty \) in continuity from above cannot be dropped.

0131 Proposition 28.2.4

Let \(\mu \) be a measure. If \(A \subseteq B\), then \(\mu (A) \leq \mu (B)\); \(\mu (\bigcup _kA_k) \leq \sum _k\mu (A_k)\); if \(A_1 \subseteq A_2 \subseteq \cdots \), then \(\mu (\bigcup _kA_k) = \lim _k\mu (A_k)\); and if \(A_1 \supseteq A_2 \supseteq \cdots \) with \(\mu (A_1) {\lt} \infty \), then \(\mu (\bigcap _kA_k) = \lim _k\mu (A_k)\).

Proof â–¼

\(\mu (B) = \mu (A) + \mu (B \setminus A)\). Replacing \(A_k\) by \(A_k \setminus \bigcup _{j{\lt}k}A_j\) gives disjoint sets with the same union and smaller measures. For increasing sets, \(\bigcup _kA_k = \bigsqcup _k(A_k \setminus A_{k-1})\), and the partial sums of the measures are \(\mu (A_k)\). For decreasing sets apply this to \(A_1 \setminus A_k\).

0132 Definition 28.2.5

A map \(f \colon (X, \mathcal{A}) \to (Y, \mathcal{B})\) is measurable if \(f^{-1}(B) \in \mathcal{A}\) for \(B \in \mathcal{B}\). Functions to \([-\infty , \infty ]\), \(\mathbb {R}\) or \(\mathbb {C}\) are measurable for the Borel \(\sigma \)-algebras. A simple function is a finite combination \(\sum _ia_i\mathbf{1}_{A_i}\) of indicator functions of measurable sets.

Measurability of functions is the condition that makes integrals of \(f\) meaningful: one needs to measure the sets \(\{ a {\lt} f \leq b\} \), on which \(f\) is approximately constant. Continuous functions on a topological space are Borel measurable, since preimages of open sets are open; monotone functions on \(\mathbb {R}\) are Borel measurable, since \(\{ f {\gt} a\} \) is an interval; and an indicator function \(\mathbf{1}_A\) is measurable if and only if \(A\) is measurable, since \(\{ \mathbf{1}_A {\gt} a\} \) is \(\emptyset \), \(A\) or \(X\).

0133 Proposition 28.2.6

  1. \(f \colon X \to [-\infty , \infty ]\) is measurable if and only if \(\{ f {\gt} a\} \) is measurable for all \(a \in \mathbb {R}\).

  2. If \(f_1, \ldots , f_k \colon X \to \mathbb {R}\) are measurable and \(\Phi \colon \mathbb {R}^k \to \mathbb {R}\) is continuous, \(\Phi (f_1, \ldots , f_k)\) is measurable; so sums and products of measurable functions are measurable, and \(f \colon X \to \mathbb {C}\) is measurable if and only if its real and imaginary parts are.

  3. Pointwise \(\sup \), \(\inf \), \(\limsup \) and \(\liminf \) of sequences of measurable functions are measurable.

  4. Every measurable \(f \colon X \to [0, \infty ]\) is the pointwise limit of an increasing sequence of simple functions \(0 \leq s_1 \leq s_2 \leq \cdots \).

Proof â–¼

(1) The sets \((a, \infty ]\) generate the Borel \(\sigma \)-algebra of \([-\infty , \infty ]\), and the sets \(B\) with \(f^{-1}(B)\) measurable form a \(\sigma \)-algebra. (2) Open subsets of \(\mathbb {R}^k\) are countable unions of products of open intervals, and \((f_1, \ldots , f_k)^{-1}\) of such a product is a finite intersection of measurable sets; so \((f_1, \ldots , f_k)\) is measurable for Borel sets, and so is its composite with \(\Phi \). (3) \(\{ \sup _nf_n {\gt} a\} = \bigcup _n\{ f_n {\gt} a\} \), \(\inf _nf_n = -\sup _n(-f_n)\), and \(\limsup f_n = \inf _n\sup _{k \geq n}f_k\). (4) Let \(s_n = \min (n, 2^{-n}\lfloor 2^nf \rfloor )\).
