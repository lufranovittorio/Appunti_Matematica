28.6 Conformal maps and the Riemann mapping theorem014Z

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

0150 Lemma 28.6.1 Schwarz

Let \(f \colon \mathbb {D} \to \mathbb {D}\) be holomorphic with \(f(0) = 0\). Then \(|f(z)| \leq |z|\) and \(|f'(0)| \leq 1\), with equality for some \(z \neq 0\) or in the second inequality only if \(f(z) = \lambda z\) with \(|\lambda | = 1\).

Proof â–¼

The function \(g(z) = f(z)/z\), extended by \(f'(0)\) at \(0\), is holomorphic on \(\mathbb {D}\) (Proposition 28.5.3(1)). On \(|z| = r\), \(|g| \leq 1/r\), so by the maximum principle \(|g| \leq 1/r\) on \(D(0, r)\); letting \(r \to 1\) gives \(|g| \leq 1\). Equality at an interior point forces \(g\) to be a constant of modulus \(1\).

0151 Proposition 28.6.2

For \(a \in \mathbb {D}\) the Möbius map \(\phi _a(z) = \frac{z - a}{1 - \bar az}\) is a biholomorphic self-map of \(\mathbb {D}\) with \(\phi _a(a) = 0\) and inverse \(\phi _{-a}\). Every biholomorphic self-map of \(\mathbb {D}\) is \(z \mapsto \lambda \phi _a(z)\) with \(|\lambda | = 1\).

Proof â–¼

For \(|z| {\lt} 1\) and \(|a| {\lt} 1\), \(|1 - \bar az|^2 - |z - a|^2 = (1 - |a|^2)(1 - |z|^2) {\gt} 0\), so \(\phi _a\) maps \(\mathbb {D}\) into \(\mathbb {D}\), and a computation gives \(\phi _{-a}\phi _a = \mathrm{id}\). If \(F\) is a biholomorphic self-map with \(F(a) = 0\), then \(g = F \circ \phi _{-a}\) fixes \(0\), and Lemma 28.6.1 applied to \(g\) and \(g^{-1}\) gives \(|g'(0)| = 1\), hence \(g(z) = \lambda z\).

0152 Theorem 28.6.3 Montel

Let \(\mathcal{F}\) be a family of holomorphic functions on an open \(U\) that is uniformly bounded on every compact subset. Then every sequence in \(\mathcal{F}\) has a subsequence converging uniformly on compact subsets to a holomorphic function.

Proof â–¼

Let \(K \subseteq U\) be compact and \(2\rho \) the distance from \(K\) to \(\mathbb {C}\setminus U\) (or \(1\) if \(U = \mathbb {C}\)), and let \(K'\) be the set of points at distance \(\leq \rho \) from \(K\), a compact subset of \(U\). By the Cauchy estimates, \(|f'| \leq \rho ^{-1}\sup _{K'}|f|\) on \(K\) for all \(f \in \mathcal{F}\), so \(\mathcal{F}\) is equicontinuous on \(K\) (mean value inequality). By the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem (Theorem 26.13.4) a sequence has a subsequence converging uniformly on \(K\). Exhausting \(U\) by compact sets \(K_1 \subseteq K_2 \subseteq \cdots \) and taking a diagonal subsequence gives uniform convergence on all compact subsets; the limit is holomorphic by Corollary 28.4.3(4).

0153 Theorem 28.6.4 Hurwitz

Let \(f_n \to f\) uniformly on compact subsets of a domain \(U\), with \(f_n\) holomorphic and \(f\) nonconstant. If every \(f_n\) is nowhere zero, so is \(f\); if every \(f_n\) is injective, so is \(f\).

Proof â–¼

Suppose \(f(z_0) = 0\). Since \(f \not\equiv 0\), there is \(r\) with \(\overline{D}(z_0, r) \subseteq U\) and \(f \neq 0\) on the circle \(|z - z_0| = r\), so \(|f| \geq \delta {\gt} 0\) there. Then \(f_n \to f\) and \(f_n' \to f'\) uniformly on the circle, so \(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int f_n'/f_n \to \frac{1}{2\pi i}\int f'/f \geq 1\), and by Theorem 28.5.10 \(f_n\) has a zero inside for large \(n\). For injectivity, if \(f(z_1) = f(z_2)\) with \(z_1 \neq z_2\), apply the first part on a punctured neighbourhood of \(z_2\) to the functions \(f_n - f_n(z_1)\), which are nowhere zero there.

0154 Theorem 28.6.5 Riemann mapping theorem

Every simply connected domain \(U \subsetneq \mathbb {C}\) is biholomorphic to \(\mathbb {D}\). More precisely, for \(z_0 \in U\) there is a unique biholomorphic \(\psi \colon U \to \mathbb {D}\) with \(\psi (z_0) = 0\) and \(\psi '(z_0) {\gt} 0\).

Proof â–¼

Let \(\mathcal{F}\) be the set of injective holomorphic maps \(U \to \mathbb {D}\) sending \(z_0\) to \(0\).

\(\mathcal{F} \neq \emptyset \). Choose \(b \notin U\). Since \(z - b\) is nowhere zero on the simply connected \(U\), the function \((z - b)'/(z - b)\) has a primitive \(h\) (Theorem 28.3.3); adjusting by a constant, \(e^{h} = z - b\). Let \(\phi = e^{h/2}\), so \(\phi ^2 = z - b\). Then \(\phi \) is injective, and \(\phi (z_1) = -\phi (z_2)\) implies \(z_1 = z_2\) and \(\phi (z_1) = 0\), which is impossible; so \(\phi (U)\) and \(-\phi (U)\) are disjoint. By Theorem 28.5.11, \(\phi (U)\) contains a disc \(D(w_0, \varrho )\), hence \(|\phi (z) + w_0| \geq \varrho \) on \(U\), and \(z \mapsto \varrho /(\phi (z) + w_0)\) maps \(U\) injectively into \(\mathbb {D}\). Composing with a suitable \(\phi _a\) (Proposition 28.6.2) gives an element of \(\mathcal{F}\).

An extremal element. Injective holomorphic maps have nonvanishing derivative, so \(\eta = \sup _{\psi \in \mathcal{F}}|\psi '(z_0)| {\gt} 0\). Take \(\psi _n \in \mathcal{F}\) with \(|\psi _n'(z_0)| \to \eta \); by Theorem 28.6.3 a subsequence converges uniformly on compact subsets to a holomorphic \(\psi \) with \(|\psi '(z_0)| = \eta {\gt} 0\), so \(\psi \) is nonconstant, hence injective by Theorem 28.6.4, with \(\psi (z_0) = 0\) and \(|\psi | \leq 1\); by the open mapping theorem \(\psi (U) \subseteq \mathbb {D}\). So \(\psi \in \mathcal{F}\) and \(|\psi '(z_0)| = \eta \).

Surjectivity. Suppose \(\alpha \in \mathbb {D} \setminus \psi (U)\). Then \(\phi _\alpha \circ \psi \) is nowhere zero on \(U\), so as above it has a holomorphic square root \(g\), which is injective because \(g^2\) is. Let \(F = \phi _{g(z_0)} \circ g \in \mathcal{F}\). Writing \(s(w) = w^2\), we have \(\psi = \phi _{-\alpha } \circ s \circ \phi _{-g(z_0)} \circ F = \Phi \circ F\) with \(\Phi \colon \mathbb {D} \to \mathbb {D}\) holomorphic and \(\Phi (0) = 0\). As \(s\) is not injective, \(\Phi \) is not injective, so \(\Phi \) is not a rotation and \(|\Phi '(0)| {\lt} 1\) by Lemma 28.6.1. Then \(\eta = |\psi '(z_0)| = |\Phi '(0)|\, |F'(z_0)| {\lt} |F'(z_0)| \leq \eta \), a contradiction. So \(\psi \) is a biholomorphism onto \(\mathbb {D}\), and multiplying by a unimodular constant makes \(\psi '(z_0) {\gt} 0\).

Uniqueness. If \(\psi _1, \psi _2\) both work, \(\psi _2 \circ \psi _1^{-1}\) is an automorphism of \(\mathbb {D}\) fixing \(0\) with positive derivative there, hence the identity by Proposition 28.6.2.

0155 Remark 28.6.6

A real function \(u\) on an open \(U \subseteq \mathbb {C}\) is harmonic if it is \(C^2\) with \(\partial _x^2u + \partial _y^2u = 0\). Real and imaginary parts of holomorphic functions are harmonic, by the Cauchy–Riemann equations and Theorem 26.10.5. Conversely, on a simply connected \(U\) every harmonic \(u\) is the real part of a holomorphic function: the function \(\partial _xu - i\partial _yu\) is holomorphic (its Cauchy–Riemann equations are the harmonicity of \(u\) and the symmetry of second derivatives), so it has a primitive \(f\), and \(\operatorname {Re}f\) differs from \(u\) by a constant. Harmonic functions therefore satisfy the mean value property and the maximum principle; this is the one-variable case of the Hodge theory of Chapter 41.
