30.7 The spectrum of a bounded operator032P

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

In finite dimensions an operator \(T\) fails to be invertible exactly at its eigenvalues. In infinite dimensions \(T - \lambda \) can be injective without being surjective, and the right generalisation of the set of eigenvalues is the spectrum. Throughout this section \(X\) is a complex Banach space and \(B(X) = B(X, X)\); note that \(\| ST\| \leq \| S\| \, \| T\| \), so \(\| T^n\| \leq \| T\| ^n\).

032Q Lemma 30.7.1 Neumann series

Let \(T \in B(X)\) with \(\| T\| {\lt} 1\). Then \(I - T\) is invertible in \(B(X)\), with

\[ (I - T)^{-1} = \sum _{n \geq 0}T^n, \qquad \| (I - T)^{-1}\| \leq \frac{1}{1 - \| T\| }. \]

More generally, if \(S \in B(X)\) is invertible and \(\| R\| {\lt} \| S^{-1}\| ^{-1}\), then \(S - R\) is invertible, and \((S - R)^{-1} \to S^{-1}\) as \(R \to 0\). So the invertible operators form an open subset of \(B(X)\), on which inversion is continuous.

Proof â–¼

The series converges absolutely, \(\sum \| T^n\| \leq \sum \| T\| ^n = (1 - \| T\| )^{-1}\), so it converges in the Banach space \(B(X)\) (Proposition 30.2.2(1) and Lemma 30.2.4). Its sum \(A\) satisfies \((I - T)\sum _{n \leq N}T^n = I - T^{N+1} = (\sum _{n \leq N}T^n)(I - T)\), and \(T^{N+1} \to 0\), so \((I - T)A = I = A(I - T)\). For the second statement write \(S - R = S(I - S^{-1}R)\) with \(\| S^{-1}R\| \leq \| S^{-1}\| \, \| R\| {\lt} 1\); then \((S - R)^{-1} = \sum _{n \geq 0}(S^{-1}R)^nS^{-1}\), and \(\| (S - R)^{-1} - S^{-1}\| \leq \sum _{n \geq 1}(\| S^{-1}\| \, \| R\| )^n\| S^{-1}\| \to 0\) as \(R \to 0\).

032R Definition 30.7.2

Let \(T \in B(X)\). The resolvent set \(\rho (T)\) is the set of \(\lambda \in \mathbb {C}\) such that \(T - \lambda = T - \lambda I\) is bijective; then its inverse \(R(\lambda ) = (T - \lambda )^{-1}\), the resolvent, is bounded by the open mapping theorem (Theorem 30.4.2(2)). The spectrum is \(\sigma (T) = \mathbb {C}\setminus \rho (T)\), and the spectral radius is \(r(T) = \sup \{ |\lambda | : \lambda \in \sigma (T)\} \). The point spectrum \(\sigma _p(T) \subseteq \sigma (T)\) is the set of eigenvalues, the \(\lambda \) for which \(T - \lambda \) is not injective.

If \(\dim X {\lt} \infty \), an injective operator is bijective, so \(\sigma (T) = \sigma _p(T)\) is the set of eigenvalues, the roots of the characteristic polynomial.

032S Theorem 30.7.3

Let \(X \neq 0\) be a complex Banach space and \(T \in B(X)\).

  1. The spectrum \(\sigma (T)\) is compact and contained in the disc \(\{ |\lambda | \leq \| T\| \} \).

  2. (Resolvent identity) \(R(\lambda ) - R(\mu ) = (\lambda - \mu )R(\lambda )R(\mu )\) for \(\lambda , \mu \in \rho (T)\), and for every bounded linear functional \(\Phi \) on \(B(X)\), the function \(\lambda \mapsto \Phi (R(\lambda ))\) is holomorphic on the open set \(\rho (T)\).

  3. (Gelfand) \(\sigma (T)\) is nonempty.

  4. (Spectral radius formula) \(r(T) = \lim _{n \to \infty }\| T^n\| ^{1/n} = \inf _{n \geq 1}\| T^n\| ^{1/n}\).

Proof â–¼

(1) If \(|\lambda | {\gt} \| T\| \), then \(T - \lambda = -\lambda (I - \lambda ^{-1}T)\) is invertible by Lemma 30.7.1. If \(\lambda \in \rho (T)\) and \(|\mu - \lambda | {\lt} \| R(\lambda )\| ^{-1}\), then \(T - \mu = (T - \lambda ) - (\mu - \lambda )I\) is invertible by the same lemma. So \(\rho (T)\) is open and \(\sigma (T)\) is closed and bounded, hence compact (Theorem 18.6.5).

(2) \(R(\lambda ) - R(\mu ) = R(\lambda )\bigl((T - \mu ) - (T - \lambda )\bigr)R(\mu ) = (\lambda - \mu )R(\lambda )R(\mu )\). As \(\mu \to \lambda \), \(R(\mu ) \to R(\lambda )\) by the continuity of inversion (Lemma 30.7.1), so \(\frac{R(\mu ) - R(\lambda )}{\mu - \lambda } = R(\mu )R(\lambda ) \to R(\lambda )^2\) in norm, and \(\Phi (R(\mu ))\) has the complex derivative \(\Phi (R(\lambda )^2)\) at \(\lambda \).

(3) Suppose \(\sigma (T) = \emptyset \). For \(|\lambda | \geq 2\| T\| \), the Neumann series gives \(R(\lambda ) = -\lambda ^{-1}\sum _{n \geq 0}\lambda ^{-n}T^n\), so \(\| R(\lambda )\| \leq |\lambda |^{-1}\sum _n2^{-n} = 2/|\lambda |\), which tends to \(0\) as \(|\lambda | \to \infty \). For \(\Phi \in B(X)^*\), the entire function \(\Phi (R(\lambda ))\) is therefore bounded (by continuity on the compact disc \(|\lambda | \leq 2\| T\| + 1\), and by \(2\| \Phi \| /|\lambda |\) outside), hence constant by Liouville’s theorem (Corollary 29.4.7), and the constant is its limit \(0\) at infinity. As this holds for every \(\Phi \), the Hahn–Banach theorem (Theorem 30.4.1), applied in the normed space \(B(X)\), gives \(R(0) = 0\). But \(R(0) = T^{-1}\) is invertible, and an invertible operator on \(X \neq 0\) is not zero.

(4) First, \(r(T) \leq \| T^n\| ^{1/n}\) for every \(n \geq 1\). Indeed, if \(\lambda \in \sigma (T)\), then \(\lambda ^n \in \sigma (T^n)\): we have \(T^n - \lambda ^n = (T - \lambda )P = P(T - \lambda )\) with \(P = \sum _{k=0}^{n-1}\lambda ^{n-1-k}T^k\), and if \(T^n - \lambda ^n\) had an inverse \(Q\), then \(PQ\) would be a right inverse and \(QP\) a left inverse of \(T - \lambda \), which would be bijective. So \(|\lambda |^n \leq \| T^n\| \) by (1). Hence \(r(T) \leq \inf _n\| T^n\| ^{1/n} \leq \liminf _n\| T^n\| ^{1/n}\).

Second, \(\limsup _n\| T^n\| ^{1/n} \leq r(T)\). Let \(\Phi \in B(X)^*\), and consider \(g(w) = \Phi (R(1/w))\) for \(0 {\lt} |w| {\lt} 1/r(T)\) (where \(1/r(T) = \infty \) if \(r(T) = 0\)), holomorphic by (2). For \(0 {\lt} |w| {\lt} 1/\| T\| \) the Neumann series gives

\[ g(w) = -\sum _{n \geq 0}\Phi (T^n)w^{n+1}, \]

a convergent power series, so \(g\) extends holomorphically to \(w = 0\) by \(g(0) = 0\), and is then holomorphic on the whole disc \(D(0, 1/r(T))\). By Theorem 29.4.2, its Taylor series at \(0\) converges on this whole disc; its coefficients are \(g^{(n+1)}(0)/(n + 1)! = -\Phi (T^n)\), the coefficients of the series above. So for every \(\lambda \) with \(|\lambda | {\gt} r(T)\) the series \(\sum _n\Phi (T^n)\lambda ^{-n-1}\) converges, and in particular \(\sup _n|\Phi (T^n/\lambda ^{n+1})| {\lt} \infty \) for every \(\Phi \in B(X)^*\). Now apply the uniform boundedness principle to the family of bounded linear functionals \(\Phi \mapsto \Phi (T^n/\lambda ^{n+1})\) on the Banach space \(B(X)^*\): their norms are \(\| T^n\| /|\lambda |^{n+1}\) by the Hahn–Banach theorem, so \(\| T^n\| \leq C|\lambda |^{n+1}\) for some \(C\) and all \(n\). Hence \(\limsup _n\| T^n\| ^{1/n} \leq |\lambda |\), and letting \(|\lambda |\) decrease to \(r(T)\) gives the claim. Together with the first part, the limit exists and equals \(r(T) = \inf _n\| T^n\| ^{1/n}\).

The proof of (4) is a nice interplay of the three main tools of this chapter: complex analysis enters through the radius of convergence of the Taylor series of the resolvent, and the uniform boundedness principle converts the bounds on each \(\Phi (T^n)\) into a bound on \(\| T^n\| \). Note that \(r(T)\) can be much smaller than \(\| T\| \).

032T Example 30.7.4

  1. Matrices. For \(A = \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\), \(\sigma (A) = \{ 0\} \) and \(r(A) = 0 {\lt} 1 = \| A\| \), in accordance with \(A^2 = 0\). For a matrix, \(r(A)\) is the largest absolute value of an eigenvalue, and the spectral radius formula says that \(\| A^n\| \) grows like \(r(A)^n\), up to subexponential factors: for \(A = \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\), \(A^n = \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & n \\ 0 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\) has norm between \(n\) and \(n + 2\), and \(\| A^n\| ^{1/n} \to 1\).

  2. Multiplication operators. Let \(g\) be a continuous function on \([0, 1]\) and \(M_gf = gf\) on \(L^2([0, 1])\). Then \(\sigma (M_g) = g([0, 1])\). If \(\lambda \notin g([0, 1])\), then \(1/(g - \lambda )\) is continuous, hence bounded, and \(M_{1/(g - \lambda )}\) is the inverse of \(M_g - \lambda \). If \(\lambda = g(x_0)\), then for every \(k\) there is an interval \(I_k \ni x_0\) of positive length on which \(|g - \lambda | {\lt} 1/k\), and \(f_k = \mathbf{1}_{I_k}/\| \mathbf{1}_{I_k}\| _2\) satisfies \(\| (M_g - \lambda )f_k\| _2 \leq 1/k\) with \(\| f_k\| _2 = 1\); so \(M_g - \lambda \) has no bounded inverse, and \(\lambda \in \sigma (M_g)\). The eigenvalues are the \(\lambda \) for which \(\{ g = \lambda \} \) has positive measure. For \(g(x) = x\), the spectrum of the self-adjoint operator \(M_x\) is the whole interval \([0, 1]\), and there are no eigenvalues at all.

  3. Shifts. For the left shift \(L(x_1, x_2, \ldots ) = (x_2, x_3, \ldots )\) on \(\ell ^2\), every \(\lambda \) with \(|\lambda | {\lt} 1\) is an eigenvalue (Counterexample 30.6.6(2)), and \(\| L\| = 1\); as \(\sigma (L)\) is closed, \(\sigma (L)\) is the closed unit disc. The right shift \(S = L^*\) has the same spectrum (Exercise 30.12.14), although it has no eigenvalues.

  4. The Volterra operator \(Vf(x) = \int _0^xf(t)\, dt\) on \(L^2([0, 1])\). By induction and Fubini’s theorem,

    \[ V^nf(x) = \int _0^x\frac{(x - t)^{n-1}}{(n - 1)!}f(t)\, dt, \]

    so \(|V^nf(x)| \leq \| f\| _1/(n - 1)! \leq \| f\| _2/(n - 1)!\) and \(\| V^n\| \leq 1/(n - 1)!\). For every \(K \geq 1\), the factors \(K + 1, \ldots , n - 1\) of \((n - 1)!\) are at least \(K\), so \(((n - 1)!)^{1/n} \geq K^{(n - 1 - K)/n} \to K\); hence \(\| V^n\| ^{1/n} \to 0\) and \(r(V) = 0\). So \(\sigma (V) = \{ 0\} \), nonempty as Gelfand’s theorem requires: \(V\) is not surjective, since \(Vf\) is always continuous. For \(\lambda \neq 0\) the inverse of \(V - \lambda \) is a Neumann series, which solves Volterra’s integral equation (Exercise 30.12.13).

For compact operators on Hilbert spaces the spectrum is as close to the finite-dimensional case as possible.

032U Corollary 30.7.5

Let \(H\) be an infinite-dimensional complex Hilbert space and \(K \in B(H)\) compact. Then \(0 \in \sigma (K)\), every nonzero \(\lambda \in \sigma (K)\) is an eigenvalue with finite-dimensional eigenspace, and for every \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\) only finitely many points of \(\sigma (K)\) satisfy \(|\lambda | \geq \varepsilon \). So \(\sigma (K)\) is countable, and \(0\) is its only possible accumulation point.

Proof â–¼

If \(0 \notin \sigma (K)\), then \(I = KK^{-1}\) would be compact (Proposition 30.6.2(1)), contradicting Proposition 30.2.2(3). For \(\lambda \neq 0\), \(K - \lambda = -\lambda (I - \lambda ^{-1}K)\) with \(\lambda ^{-1}K\) compact, so by the Fredholm alternative (Theorem 30.6.4) it is bijective as soon as it is injective, and its kernel is finite-dimensional. Suppose there were infinitely many distinct eigenvalues \(\lambda _1, \lambda _2, \ldots \) with \(|\lambda _n| \geq \varepsilon \), with eigenvectors \(x_n\). Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are linearly independent (as in finite dimensions: applying \(K - \lambda _j\) to a shortest nontrivial linear relation \(\sum c_ix_i = 0\), for an index \(j\) occurring in it, would give a shorter one). Let \(M_n = \operatorname {span}(x_1, \ldots , x_n)\), and choose unit vectors \(y_n \in M_n \cap M_{n-1}^\perp \). Since \((K - \lambda _n)x_j = (\lambda _j - \lambda _n)x_j\), we have \((K - \lambda _n)M_n \subseteq M_{n-1}\). For \(m {\lt} n\),

\[ Ky_n - Ky_m = \lambda _ny_n + \bigl((K - \lambda _n)y_n - Ky_m\bigr), \]

where the bracket lies in \(M_{n-1}\) and \(y_n \perp M_{n-1}\), so \(\| Ky_n - Ky_m\| \geq |\lambda _n| \geq \varepsilon \). So \((Ky_n)\) has no convergent subsequence, contradicting compactness.

032V Proposition 30.7.6

Let \(H \neq 0\) be a complex Hilbert space, \(T \in B(H)\) self-adjoint, and \(m = \inf _{\| x\| = 1}\langle Tx, x \rangle \), \(M = \sup _{\| x\| = 1}\langle Tx, x \rangle \). Then \(\sigma (T) \subseteq [m, M]\), both \(m\) and \(M\) lie in \(\sigma (T)\), and \(r(T) = \| T\| = \max (|m|, |M|)\).

Proof â–¼

The numbers \(\langle Tx, x \rangle = \langle x, Tx \rangle = \overline{\langle Tx, x \rangle }\) are real. Call an operator \(A\) bounded below if \(\| Ax\| \geq c\| x\| \) for some \(c {\gt} 0\); then \(A\) is injective with closed image (the preimages of a Cauchy sequence in the image form a Cauchy sequence). Let \(\lambda = \alpha + i\beta \) with \(\beta \neq 0\). Then \(\| (T - \lambda )x\| \, \| x\| \geq |\operatorname {Im}\langle (T - \lambda )x, x \rangle | = |\beta |\, \| x\| ^2\), so \(T - \lambda \) is bounded below; its image is closed, and dense because its orthogonal complement is \(\ker (T - \lambda )^* = \ker (T - \bar\lambda ) = 0\) (Theorem 30.3.4), the same estimate applying to \(\bar\lambda \). So \(\lambda \in \rho (T)\). If \(\lambda {\gt} M\) is real, then \(\langle (\lambda - T)x, x \rangle \geq (\lambda - M)\| x\| ^2\), and the same argument shows \(\lambda \in \rho (T)\); similarly for \(\lambda {\lt} m\). So \(\sigma (T) \subseteq [m, M]\).

The sesquilinear form \([x, y] = \langle (M - T)x, y \rangle \) is Hermitian and positive semidefinite, so it satisfies the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality \(|[x, y]|^2 \leq [x, x][y, y]\): choosing \(|c| = 1\) with \(c[y, x] = |[x, y]|\), the real quadratic polynomial \([x + tcy, x + tcy] = [x, x] + 2t|[x, y]| + t^2[y, y]\) is nonnegative for all real \(t\), which forces \([x, y] = 0\) if \([y, y] = 0\), and a nonpositive discriminant otherwise. Choose unit vectors \(x_n\) with \(\langle Tx_n, x_n \rangle \to M\), so \([x_n, x_n] \to 0\). With \(y = (M - T)x_n\),

\[ \| (M - T)x_n\| ^4 = [x_n, y]^2 \leq [x_n, x_n]\, [y, y] \leq [x_n, x_n]\, \| M - T\| \, \| (M - T)x_n\| ^2, \]

so \((M - T)x_n \to 0\) while \(\| x_n\| = 1\). Thus \(M - T\) is not bounded below and has no bounded inverse: \(M \in \sigma (T)\). Applying this to \(-T\) gives \(m \in \sigma (T)\). Finally \(\| T\| = \sup _{\| x\| = 1}|\langle Tx, x \rangle | = \max (|m|, |M|)\) by the first step of the proof of Theorem 30.6.3(1), which did not use compactness, and \(\max (|m|, |M|) \leq r(T) \leq \| T\| \) since \(m, M \in \sigma (T)\).

032W Remark 30.7.7

The spectral theorem for compact self-adjoint operators (Theorem 30.6.3) diagonalises \(T\) in an orthonormal basis. A general bounded self-adjoint operator, such as \(M_x\) in Example 30.7.4(2), may have no eigenvectors at all, and the correct generalisation is: every bounded self-adjoint operator on a separable Hilbert space is unitarily equivalent to a multiplication operator. That is, there are a finite measure space \((\Omega , \mu )\), a bounded real measurable function \(g\) on \(\Omega \) and a unitary \(U \colon H \to L^2(\Omega , \mu )\) with \(UTU^{-1} = M_g\) [RS80, Chapter VII]. It yields a functional calculus: \(f(T) = U^{-1}M_{f \circ g}U\) for bounded Borel functions \(f\) on \(\sigma (T)\), with \(\| f(T)\| \leq \sup _{\sigma (T)}|f|\) (equality for continuous \(f\)) and \(\sigma (f(T)) = f(\sigma (T))\) for continuous \(f\); for instance \(\sqrt{T}\) for \(T \geq 0\), \(e^{itT}\), or the spectral projections \(\mathbf{1}_{(-\infty , c]}(T)\). The same holds for normal operators (\(TT^* = T^*T\)), with complex \(g\), and, with more work, for the unbounded self-adjoint operators of quantum mechanics and for the Laplacian [RS80, Chapter VIII].
