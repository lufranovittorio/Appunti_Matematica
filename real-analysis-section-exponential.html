26.7 The exponential and trigonometric functions011N

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

011O Definition 26.7.1

For \(z \in \mathbb {C}\) let \(\exp (z) = e^z = \sum _{n \geq 0}z^n/n!\) (radius of convergence \(\infty \), by the ratio test), and for \(x \in \mathbb {R}\) let \(\cos x = \operatorname {Re} e^{ix}\) and \(\sin x = \operatorname {Im} e^{ix}\), so that \(\cos x = \sum _k(-1)^kx^{2k}/(2k)!\) and \(\sin x = \sum _k(-1)^kx^{2k+1}/(2k+1)!\).

011P Proposition 26.7.2

  1. \(e^{z+w} = e^ze^w\), \(e^z \neq 0\), \(\overline{e^z} = e^{\bar z}\), and \(\exp ' = \exp \).

  2. \(\exp \) restricts to a strictly increasing bijection \(\mathbb {R}\to (0, \infty )\), whose inverse \(\log \) is differentiable with \(\log '(x) = 1/x\) and \(\log (xy) = \log x + \log y\).

  3. \(\cos ^2 x + \sin ^2 x = 1\), \(\cos ' = -\sin \), \(\sin ' = \cos \), and \(\cos (x + y) = \cos x\cos y - \sin x\sin y\), \(\sin (x + y) = \sin x\cos y + \cos x\sin y\).

Proof â–¼

(1) By Proposition 26.5.6 and the binomial formula, \(e^ze^w = \sum _n\sum _k\frac{z^kw^{n-k}}{k!(n-k)!} = \sum _n(z + w)^n/n!\). So \(e^ze^{-z} = 1\). Conjugation is continuous and commutes with the partial sums. The derivative is Theorem 26.6.19. (2) For real \(x\), \(e^x = (e^{x/2})^2 {\gt} 0\), so \(\exp \) is strictly increasing by Theorem 26.6.6. For \(x \geq 0\), \(e^x \geq 1 + x\), and \(e^{-x} = 1/e^x\); so \(\exp \) is unbounded above and takes values arbitrarily close to \(0\), and by the intermediate value theorem its image is \((0, \infty )\). Apply Proposition 26.6.7. (3) \(|e^{ix}|^2 = e^{ix}\overline{e^{ix}} = e^{ix}e^{-ix} = 1\); \((e^{ix})' = ie^{ix}\) by Theorem 26.6.19 applied to the real power series \(\sum _n i^nx^n/n!\); the addition formulas are the real and imaginary parts of \(e^{i(x+y)} = e^{ix}e^{iy}\).

011Q Theorem 26.7.3

The function \(\cos \) has a unique zero in \((0, 2)\); call it \(\pi /2\). Then \(e^{i\pi /2} = i\), the map \(t \mapsto e^{it}\) is a surjective continuous homomorphism \(\mathbb {R}\to S^1 = \{ |z| = 1\} \) with kernel \(2\pi \mathbb {Z}\), and \(p(t) = e^{2\pi it}\) is a covering map \(\mathbb {R}\to S^1\). Every \(z \neq 0\) is \(re^{i\theta }\) with \(r = |z|\) and \(\theta \) unique modulo \(2\pi \).

Proof â–¼

For \(0 {\lt} x \leq 2\) the terms \(x^{2k+1}/(2k+1)!\) decrease, since consecutive ratios are \(x^2/((2k+2)(2k+3)) {\lt} 1\); by Lemma 26.5.5, \(\sin x \geq x - x^3/6 {\gt} 0\). So \(\cos \) is strictly decreasing on \([0, 2]\). The terms \(4^k/(2k)!\) of \(\cos 2\) decrease from \(k = 1\) on, so \(\cos 2 \leq 1 - 2 + 16/24 {\lt} 0\), while \(\cos 0 = 1\); by the intermediate value theorem there is a unique zero \(\pi /2 \in (0, 2)\). Then \(\sin (\pi /2) = 1\), so \(e^{i\pi /2} = i\) and \(e^{2\pi i} = i^4 = 1\).

Kernel: if \(0 {\lt} t {\lt} 2\pi \), write \(e^{it/4} = u + iv\) with \(u, v {\gt} 0\) (as \(0 {\lt} t/4 {\lt} \pi /2\)). If \(e^{it} = (u + iv)^4 = 1\), then \(\operatorname {Im}(u + iv)^4 = 4uv(u^2 - v^2) = 0\) forces \(u^2 = v^2 = 1/2\), and then \(\operatorname {Re}(u + iv)^4 = u^4 - 6u^2v^2 + v^4 = -1\), a contradiction. With periodicity, the kernel is \(2\pi \mathbb {Z}\).

Surjectivity: for \(a + ib \in S^1\) with \(a, b \geq 0\), choose \(t \in [0, \pi /2]\) with \(\cos t = a\) (intermediate value theorem); then \(\sin t = \sqrt{1 - a^2} = b\). The other quadrants are obtained by multiplying by powers of \(i = e^{i\pi /2}\). For the covering property, \(p\) is injective on \([a, a + 1)\) and identifies only \(a\) and \(a + 1\) in \([a, a + 1]\), so the argument of Proposition 18.4.5 applies verbatim. The polar form follows by applying this to \(z/|z|\).

011R Remark 26.7.4

The proof of Theorem 18.4.6 uses only that \(\mathbb {R}\) is simply connected and that the covering map has period \(4\) with fibres the translates of \(4\mathbb {Z}\); it applies verbatim to \(t \mapsto e^{2\pi it}\), with period \(1\), so \(\pi _1(S^1) \cong \mathbb {Z}\) is generated by the loop \(t \mapsto e^{2\pi it}\). For \(x {\gt} 0\) and \(s \in \mathbb {C}\) we define \(x^s = e^{s\log x}\); then \(\frac{d}{dx}x^s = sx^{s-1}\).

025C Proposition 26.7.5 Notable limits

  1. \(\lim _{x \to 0}\frac{e^x - 1}{x} = 1\), \(\lim _{x \to 0}\frac{\log (1 + x)}{x} = 1\), \(\lim _{x \to 0}\frac{\sin x}{x} = 1\) and \(\lim _{x \to 0}\frac{1 - \cos x}{x^2} = \frac12\).

  2. For every \(x \in \mathbb {R}\), \((1 + x/n)^n \to e^x\); in particular \((1 + 1/n)^n \to e = \exp (1)\).

  3. For \(k \in \mathbb {N}\) and \(a {\gt} 0\): \(x^ke^{-ax} \to 0\) and \((\log x)/x^a \to 0\) as \(x \to +\infty \), and \(x^a\log x \to 0\) as \(x \to 0^+\).

Proof â–¼

(1) The first three are the derivatives of \(\exp \) at \(0\), of \(\log \) at \(1\) and of \(\sin \) at \(0\) (Proposition 26.7.2), which are \(e^0 = 1\), \(1/1\) and \(\cos 0 = 1\). For the last, apply Theorem 26.6.9(1): \(\frac{1 - \cos x}{x^2}\) has the same limit as \(\frac{\sin x}{2x} \to \frac12\). (2) For \(x = 0\) this is clear. Otherwise, for \(n {\gt} |x|\), \(1 + x/n {\gt} 0\) and \((1 + x/n)^n = \exp (n\log (1 + x/n))\), with \(n\log (1 + x/n) = x\cdot \frac{\log (1 + x/n)}{x/n} \to x\) by (1) and Theorem 26.3.4 (as \(x/n \to 0\) and \(x/n \neq 0\)). By continuity of \(\exp \), \((1 + x/n)^n \to e^x\). (3) For \(x {\gt} 0\), \(e^{ax} \geq (ax)^{k+1}/(k+1)!\), one term of the exponential series, so \(0 {\lt} x^ke^{-ax} \leq (k+1)!/(a^{k+1}x) \to 0\). For \(x = e^t\), \((\log x)/x^a = te^{-at}\), which tends to \(0\) as \(t \to +\infty \) by the case \(k = 1\); since \(\log x \to +\infty \) as \(x \to +\infty \), Proposition 26.3.6 applies. Similarly \(x^a\log x = -te^{-at}\) for \(x = e^{-t}\), and \(t \to +\infty \) as \(x \to 0^+\).

025D Counterexample 26.7.6

  1. \(\sin (1/x)\) has no limit at \(0\): along \(x_n = 1/(2\pi n + \pi /2)\) it is \(1\), along \(y_n = 1/(\pi n)\) it is \(0\) (\(n \geq 1\)), and Theorem 26.3.4 applies. Since \(x_n - y_n \to 0\), \(\sin (1/x)\) is continuous but not uniformly continuous on \((0, 1)\). On the other hand \(x\sin (1/x) \to 0\) by the squeeze theorem, as \(|x\sin (1/x)| \leq |x|\).

  2. A derivative need not be continuous. Let \(f(x) = x^2\sin (1/x)\) for \(x \neq 0\) and \(f(0) = 0\). Then \(f'(0) = \lim _{x \to 0}x\sin (1/x) = 0\), and \(f'(x) = 2x\sin (1/x) - \cos (1/x)\) for \(x \neq 0\), which has no limit at \(0\). In accordance with Proposition 26.6.8 and Theorem 26.6.10, the discontinuity of \(f'\) at \(0\) is essential.

  3. The converse of de l’Hôpital’s rule fails. With \(f\) as in (2) and \(g(x) = x\), \(f(x)/g(x) = x\sin (1/x) \to 0\), while \(f'(x)/g'(x) = 2x\sin (1/x) - \cos (1/x)\) has no limit as \(x \to 0\).

025E Corollary 26.7.7 Inequality of arithmetic and geometric means

For \(x_1, \ldots , x_k {\gt} 0\) and weights \(t_i \geq 0\) with \(\sum t_i = 1\), \(\prod _ix_i^{t_i} \leq \sum _it_ix_i\). In particular \(\sqrt[k]{x_1\cdots x_k} \leq \frac{1}{k}(x_1 + \cdots + x_k)\), with equality if and only if all \(x_i\) are equal.

Proof â–¼

\(\log '' = -1/x^2 {\lt} 0\), so \(\log \) is concave (Proposition 26.6.17(3)), and by Jensen’s inequality \(\sum t_i\log x_i \leq \log \sum t_ix_i\). Apply \(\exp \), which is increasing. For the equality statement, let \(m = \frac1k\sum x_i\). As \(\log '\) is strictly decreasing, the mean value theorem gives \(\log x {\lt} \log m + (x - m)/m\) for every \(x {\gt} 0\) with \(x \neq m\) (the tangent inequality of Proposition 26.6.17(2) for \(-\log \), made strict). Averaging over \(x = x_1, \ldots , x_k\) gives \(\frac1k\sum \log x_i {\lt} \log m\) unless all \(x_i = m\).

011S Lemma 26.7.8

The function \(\beta (t) = e^{-1/t}\) for \(t {\gt} 0\) and \(\beta (t) = 0\) for \(t \leq 0\) has derivatives of all orders on \(\mathbb {R}\), all vanishing at \(0\). The function \(\gamma (t) = \beta (t)/(\beta (t) + \beta (1 - t))\) has derivatives of all orders, takes values in \([0, 1]\), and equals \(0\) for \(t \leq 0\) and \(1\) for \(t \geq 1\).

Proof â–¼

By induction, on \(t {\gt} 0\), \(\beta ^{(k)}(t) = p_k(1/t)e^{-1/t}\) for a polynomial \(p_k\). Since \(e^{s} \geq s^{m}/m!\) for \(s {\gt} 0\), \(s^je^{-s} \to 0\) as \(s \to \infty \); so \(\beta ^{(k)}(t) \to 0\) as \(t \to 0^+\), and the difference quotients of \(\beta ^{(k)}\) at \(0\) tend to \(0\), which by induction shows \(\beta ^{(k+1)}(0) = 0\). The denominator of \(\gamma \) never vanishes (one of \(t\), \(1 - t\) is positive), so \(\gamma \) has derivatives of all orders by Proposition 26.6.2, and the other properties are clear.

011T Counterexample 26.7.9

The function \(\beta \) of Lemma 26.7.8 has all derivatives zero at \(0\), so its Taylor series at \(0\) is zero, but \(\beta (t) \neq 0\) for \(t {\gt} 0\). Smooth functions need not be given by their Taylor series; complex differentiable functions are (Chapter 28).
