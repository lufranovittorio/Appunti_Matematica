26.6 Differentiation in one variable011G

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

011H Definition 26.6.1

Let \(I \subseteq \mathbb {R}\) be an interval with more than one point and \(f \colon I \to \mathbb {K}^m\). Then \(f\) is differentiable at \(a \in I\) with derivative \(f'(a)\) if \((f(x) - f(a))/(x - a) \to f'(a)\) as \(x \to a\) in \(I \setminus \{ a\} \). Equivalently, there is a function \(\phi \colon I \to \mathbb {K}^m\), continuous at \(a\), with \(f(x) - f(a) = \phi (x)(x - a)\); then \(f'(a) = \phi (a)\). The same definition with \(I\) replaced by an open subset of \(\mathbb {C}\) and \(x\) complex defines the complex derivative.

011I Proposition 26.6.2

Differentiable functions are continuous. Sums, products and quotients (where the denominator does not vanish) of differentiable scalar functions are differentiable with the usual formulas, and if \(f\) is differentiable at \(a\) and \(g\) at \(f(a)\), then \((g \circ f)'(a) = g'(f(a))f'(a)\).

Proof â–¼

Write \(f(x) - f(a) = \phi (x)(x - a)\) and \(g(y) - g(b) = \psi (y)(y - b)\) with \(\phi \), \(\psi \) continuous at \(a\), \(b = f(a)\). Continuity is clear. For products, \(fg(x) - fg(a) = (\phi (x)g(x) + f(a)\chi (x))(x - a)\) if \(g(x) - g(a) = \chi (x)(x - a)\). For \(1/f\), \(1/f(x) - 1/f(a) = -\phi (x)(x - a)/(f(x)f(a))\). For composites, \(g(f(x)) - g(f(a)) = \psi (f(x))\phi (x)(x - a)\) and \(\psi \circ f\) is continuous at \(a\). Evaluate the continuous factors at \(a\).

024Z Definition 26.6.3

Let \(f \colon I \to \mathbb {R}\) on an interval \(I\). Then \(f\) has a local maximum at \(c \in I\) if there is \(\delta {\gt} 0\) with \(f(x) \leq f(c)\) for all \(x \in I\) with \(|x - c| {\lt} \delta \), a strict local maximum if \(f(x) {\lt} f(c)\) for these \(x \neq c\), and similarly for (strict) local minima. A local extremum is a local maximum or minimum.

0250 Lemma 26.6.4 Fermat

If \(f \colon I \to \mathbb {R}\) has a local extremum at an interior point \(c\) of \(I\) and is differentiable at \(c\), then \(f'(c) = 0\).

Proof â–¼

Say \(c\) is a local maximum. For \(x {\gt} c\) close to \(c\), \((f(x) - f(c))/(x - c) \leq 0\), so \(f'(c) \leq 0\) by Corollary 26.3.5(2); for \(x {\lt} c\) the quotient is \(\geq 0\), so \(f'(c) \geq 0\).

0251 Counterexample 26.6.5

The converse fails: \(f(x) = x^3\) has \(f'(0) = 0\) and no extremum at \(0\). The hypothesis that \(c\) is interior is needed: \(f(x) = x\) on \([0, 1]\) has a maximum at \(1\) with \(f'(1) = 1\). And \(|x|\) has a minimum at \(0\), where it is not differentiable.

011J Theorem 26.6.6 Mean value theorem

Let \(f, g \colon [a, b] \to \mathbb {R}\) be continuous and differentiable on \((a, b)\).

  1. (Rolle) If \(f(a) = f(b)\), then \(f'(\xi ) = 0\) for some \(\xi \in (a, b)\).

  2. There is \(\xi \in (a, b)\) with \(f(b) - f(a) = f'(\xi )(b - a)\), and (Cauchy) some \(\eta \) with \((f(b) - f(a))g'(\eta ) = (g(b) - g(a))f'(\eta )\).

  3. If \(f' = 0\) on \((a, b)\), \(f\) is constant; if \(f' \geq 0\) (\({\gt} 0\)), \(f\) is increasing (strictly increasing).

Proof â–¼

(1) By Corollary 17.6.5, \(f\) attains a maximum and a minimum; if both are at endpoints, \(f\) is constant. Otherwise one is at some \(\xi \in (a, b)\), and \(f'(\xi ) = 0\) by Lemma 26.6.4. (2) Apply (1) to \(f(x) - \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}(x - a)\), and to \((f(b) - f(a))g(x) - (g(b) - g(a))f(x)\). (3) Apply (2) on subintervals.

011K Proposition 26.6.7

Let \(f \colon I \to \mathbb {R}\) be continuous and strictly monotone. Then \(J = f(I)\) is an interval, \(f^{-1} \colon J \to I\) is continuous, and if \(f\) is differentiable at \(a\) with \(f'(a) \neq 0\), then \(f^{-1}\) is differentiable at \(f(a)\) with derivative \(1/f'(a)\).

Proof â–¼

\(J\) is an interval by Corollary 17.5.5. A strictly monotone bijection of intervals maps subintervals onto subintervals, so \(f^{-1}\) maps open subintervals of \(J\) (relative to \(J\)) to open subintervals of \(I\), and is continuous. Write \(f(x) - f(a) = \phi (x)(x - a)\); strict monotonicity gives \(\phi (x) \neq 0\) for \(x \neq a\), and \(\phi (a) = f'(a) \neq 0\). So \(f^{-1}(y) - a = (y - f(a))/\phi (f^{-1}(y))\) with \(1/(\phi \circ f^{-1})\) continuous at \(f(a)\).

0252 Proposition 26.6.8 Limits of derivatives

Let \(f \colon [a, b) \to \mathbb {R}\) be continuous, differentiable on \((a, b)\), and suppose \(\lim _{x \to a^+}f'(x) = L \in \mathbb {R}\). Then \(f\) is differentiable at \(a\) (from the right) with \(f'(a) = L\). Consequently, if \(f\) is differentiable on an open interval containing \(c\) and \(f'\) has one-sided limits at \(c\), both equal \(f'(c)\): a derivative has neither removable nor jump discontinuities.

Proof â–¼

For \(a {\lt} x {\lt} b\), the mean value theorem gives \(\xi _x \in (a, x)\) with \((f(x) - f(a))/(x - a) = f'(\xi _x)\). If \(x_n \to a^+\), then \(\xi _{x_n} \to a^+\) by the squeeze theorem, so \(f'(\xi _{x_n}) \to L\); by Theorem 26.3.4, the difference quotient tends to \(L\). For the second statement, apply this on each side of \(c\).

0253 Theorem 26.6.9 de l’Hôpital

Let \(f, g \colon (a, b) \to \mathbb {R}\) be differentiable, with \(-\infty \leq a {\lt} b \leq +\infty \), and \(g'(x) \neq 0\) for all \(x\). Suppose that \(\lim _{x \to a^+}f'(x)/g'(x) = L \in \bar{\mathbb {R}}\) and that either

  1. \(\lim _{x \to a^+}f(x) = \lim _{x \to a^+}g(x) = 0\), or

  2. \(\lim _{x \to a^+}|g(x)| = +\infty \) and \(L \in \mathbb {R}\).

Then \(\lim _{x \to a^+}f(x)/g(x) = L\). The same holds for left limits at \(b\), and hence for two-sided limits.

Proof â–¼

Since \(g' \neq 0\), \(g\) is injective on \((a, b)\) by Rolle’s theorem, so \(g(x) \neq g(y)\) for \(x \neq y\), and by the Cauchy mean value theorem (Theorem 26.6.6(2)), for \(x {\lt} y\) in \((a, b)\) there is \(\xi \in (x, y)\) with

\[ \frac{f(x) - f(y)}{g(x) - g(y)} = \frac{f'(\xi )}{g'(\xi )}. \tag {$*$} \]

Case (1) with \(a \in \mathbb {R}\): extend \(f\) and \(g\) by \(f(a) = g(a) = 0\); they become continuous on \([a, b)\), and \((*)\) holds for \(x = a\) as well, with \(g(y) \neq 0\) for \(y {\gt} a\) by Rolle’s theorem. Let \(y_n \to a^+\) and choose \(\xi _n \in (a, y_n)\) with \(f(y_n)/g(y_n) = f'(\xi _n)/g'(\xi _n)\). Then \(\xi _n \to a^+\), so \(f(y_n)/g(y_n) \to L\), and Theorem 26.3.4 gives the claim. If \(a = -\infty \), apply the case just proved to \(F(t) = f(-1/t)\) and \(G(t) = g(-1/t)\) on an interval \((0, \eta )\), noting \(F'(t)/G'(t) = f'(-1/t)/g'(-1/t)\) and using Proposition 26.3.6.

Case (2). Let \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\) and choose \(c \in (a, b)\) with \(|f'(\xi )/g'(\xi ) - L| {\lt} \varepsilon \) for \(a {\lt} \xi {\lt} c\). Fix \(y \in (a, c)\). For \(a {\lt} x {\lt} y\), \((*)\) gives \(Q(x) = (f(x) - f(y))/(g(x) - g(y))\) with \(|Q(x) - L| {\lt} \varepsilon \). Since \(|g(x)| \to +\infty \), \(g(x) \neq 0\) for \(x\) close to \(a\), and

\[ \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} - L = \big(Q(x) - L\big)\Big(1 - \frac{g(y)}{g(x)}\Big) - L\frac{g(y)}{g(x)} + \frac{f(y)}{g(x)}. \]

As \(x \to a^+\) with \(y\) fixed, the last two terms tend to \(0\) and \(1 - g(y)/g(x) \to 1\), so \(|f(x)/g(x) - L| {\lt} 2\varepsilon \) for \(x\) close to \(a\). Left limits at \(b\) follow by applying the result to \(x \mapsto f(-x)\), \(g(-x)\).

0254 Theorem 26.6.10 Darboux

Let \(f \colon [a, b] \to \mathbb {R}\) be differentiable, with one-sided derivatives at the endpoints. Then \(f'\) takes every value between \(f'(a)\) and \(f'(b)\).

Proof â–¼

Let \(f'(a) {\lt} y {\lt} f'(b)\) (the other case follows by passing to \(-f\)) and \(g(x) = f(x) - yx\). Then \(g'(a) {\lt} 0 {\lt} g'(b)\). By Theorem 26.4.10, \(g\) attains a minimum at some \(c \in [a, b]\). As \(g'(a) {\lt} 0\), \((g(x) - g(a))/(x - a) {\lt} 0\) for \(x {\gt} a\) close to \(a\) (Proposition 26.3.3(2)), so \(g(x) {\lt} g(a)\) and \(c \neq a\); similarly \(c \neq b\). By Lemma 26.6.4, \(g'(c) = 0\), that is, \(f'(c) = y\).

011L Theorem 26.6.11 Taylor

Let \(f \colon [a, x] \to \mathbb {R}\) (or \([x, a]\)) have \(n\) continuous derivatives, with \(f^{(n+1)}\) existing on the open interval. Then there is \(\xi \) strictly between \(a\) and \(x\) with

\[ f(x) = \sum _{k=0}^n\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x - a)^k + \frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi )}{(n+1)!}(x - a)^{n+1}. \]
Proof â–¼

Let \(g(t) = f(x) - \sum _{k \leq n}f^{(k)}(t)(x - t)^k/k! - M(x - t)^{n+1}/(n+1)!\), with \(M\) chosen so that \(g(a) = 0\). Also \(g(x) = 0\). In the derivative the sum telescopes: \(g'(t) = -f^{(n+1)}(t)(x - t)^n/n! + M(x - t)^n/n!\). By Rolle’s theorem \(g'(\xi ) = 0\) for some \(\xi \) between \(a\) and \(x\), so \(M = f^{(n+1)}(\xi )\).

0255 Proposition 26.6.12 Taylor’s formula with Peano remainder

Let \(n \geq 1\), let \(f \colon I \to \mathbb {R}\) be \(n - 1\) times differentiable on an interval \(I\), and suppose \(f^{(n)}(a)\) exists at a point \(a \in I\). Let \(T_n(x) = \sum _{k=0}^nf^{(k)}(a)(x - a)^k/k!\). Then

\[ \lim _{x \to a}\frac{f(x) - T_n(x)}{(x - a)^n} = 0, \]

that is, \(f(x) = T_n(x) + o((x - a)^n)\) as \(x \to a\).

Proof â–¼

Let \(R = f - T_n\). Then \(R\) is \(n - 1\) times differentiable, \(R^{(k)}(a) = 0\) for \(0 \leq k \leq n\), and each \(R^{(k)}\) with \(k \leq n - 1\) is continuous at \(a\). For \(n = 1\) the claim is \((R(x) - R(a))/(x - a) \to R'(a) = 0\). For \(n \geq 2\), apply Theorem 26.6.9(1) \(n - 1\) times, on each side of \(a\), to the quotients \(R^{(j)}(x)/(x - a)^{n-j}\), \(0 \leq j \leq n - 2\), whose numerators and denominators tend to \(0\) and whose denominators have nonvanishing derivatives for \(x \neq a\). It remains to see that the last quotient tends to \(0\):

\[ \frac{R^{(n-1)}(x)}{n!\, (x - a)} = \frac{1}{n!}\cdot \frac{R^{(n-1)}(x) - R^{(n-1)}(a)}{x - a} \longrightarrow \frac{R^{(n)}(a)}{n!} = 0. \]
0256 Corollary 26.6.13 Second derivative test

Let \(f\) be differentiable on an open interval containing \(c\), with \(f'(c) = 0\), and suppose \(f''(c)\) exists. If \(f''(c) {\gt} 0\), then \(f\) has a strict local minimum at \(c\); if \(f''(c) {\lt} 0\), a strict local maximum.

Proof â–¼

By Proposition 26.6.12 with \(n = 2\), \((f(x) - f(c))/(x - c)^2 \to f''(c)/2 {\gt} 0\), so \(f(x) - f(c) {\gt} 0\) for \(x \neq c\) close to \(c\) (Proposition 26.3.3(2)).

0257 Counterexample 26.6.14

If \(f''(c) = 0\) the test says nothing: \(x^4\), \(-x^4\) and \(x^3\) all have vanishing first and second derivatives at \(0\), and have a minimum, a maximum and no extremum there.

0258 Definition 26.6.15

A function \(f \colon I \to \mathbb {R}\) on an interval is convex if \(f((1 - t)x + ty) \leq (1 - t)f(x) + tf(y)\) for all \(x, y \in I\) and \(t \in [0, 1]\), and strictly convex if the inequality is strict whenever \(x \neq y\) and \(0 {\lt} t {\lt} 1\). It is (strictly) concave if \(-f\) is (strictly) convex.

0259 Lemma 26.6.16 Three slopes

A function \(f \colon I \to \mathbb {R}\) is convex if and only if for all \(x {\lt} y {\lt} z\) in \(I\),

\[ \frac{f(y) - f(x)}{y - x} \leq \frac{f(z) - f(x)}{z - x} \leq \frac{f(z) - f(y)}{z - y}. \]
Proof â–¼

Write \(y = (1 - t)x + tz\) with \(t = (y - x)/(z - x) \in (0, 1)\). Convexity at this triple says \((z - x)f(y) \leq (z - y)f(x) + (y - x)f(z)\). Multiplying out, each of the two displayed inequalities is equivalent to this one. Since every \(t \in (0, 1)\) and \(x {\lt} z\) arise, and the cases \(t \in \{ 0, 1\} \) or \(x = y\) are trivial, the lemma follows (for \(x {\gt} y\) exchange the roles).

025A Proposition 26.6.17

Let \(f \colon I \to \mathbb {R}\) on an open interval \(I\).

  1. If \(f\) is convex, it is continuous, it has one-sided derivatives \(f'_-(x) \leq f'_+(x)\) at every point, and \(f'_+(x) \leq \frac{f(y) - f(x)}{y - x} \leq f'_-(y)\) for \(x {\lt} y\).

  2. If \(f\) is differentiable, the following are equivalent: \(f\) is convex; \(f'\) is increasing; \(f(y) \geq f(x) + f'(x)(y - x)\) for all \(x, y \in I\).

  3. If \(f\) is twice differentiable, \(f\) is convex if and only if \(f'' \geq 0\); if \(f'' {\gt} 0\), \(f\) is strictly convex.

  4. (Jensen) If \(f\) is convex, \(x_1, \ldots , x_k \in I\), \(t_i \geq 0\) and \(\sum t_i = 1\), then \(f(\sum t_ix_i) \leq \sum t_if(x_i)\).

Proof â–¼

(1) By Lemma 26.6.16, the slope \(s(x, y) = (f(y) - f(x))/(y - x)\) is increasing in each variable. For fixed \(x\), \(s(x, y)\) decreases as \(y \downarrow x\) and is bounded below by \(s(w, x)\) for any \(w {\lt} x\) in \(I\) (which exists, \(I\) being open); so \(f'_+(x) = \lim _{y \to x^+}s(x, y)\) exists by Proposition 26.3.9, and \(f'_+(x) \geq s(w, x)\). Letting \(w \uparrow x\) gives \(f'_-(x)\), with \(f'_-(x) \leq f'_+(x)\). The inequality for \(x {\lt} y\) follows from the monotonicity of slopes. The existence of one-sided derivatives implies continuity, as \(f(y) - f(x) = s(x, y)(y - x) \to 0\). (2) If \(f\) is convex and differentiable, (1) gives \(f'(x) = f'_+(x) \leq f'_-(y) = f'(y)\) for \(x {\lt} y\). If \(f'\) is increasing and \(y {\gt} x\), the mean value theorem gives \(f(y) - f(x) = f'(\xi )(y - x) \geq f'(x)(y - x)\); for \(y {\lt} x\), \(f'(\xi ) \leq f'(x)\) and \(y - x {\lt} 0\) give the same inequality. Finally, if the tangent inequality holds, let \(z = (1 - t)x + ty\); adding \((1 - t)\) times \(f(x) \geq f(z) + f'(z)(x - z)\) and \(t\) times \(f(y) \geq f(z) + f'(z)(y - z)\) gives \((1 - t)f(x) + tf(y) \geq f(z)\). (3) \(f'' \geq 0\) if and only if \(f'\) is increasing (Theorem 26.6.6(3) and, conversely, \(f'' = \lim \) of nonnegative quotients); apply (2). If \(f'' {\gt} 0\), \(f'\) is strictly increasing and the inequalities in the proof of (2) become strict for \(y \neq x\), hence so does the final one for \(x \neq y\), \(0 {\lt} t {\lt} 1\). (4) Induction on \(k\): for \(t_k {\lt} 1\), \(\sum t_ix_i = (1 - t_k)\sum _{i{\lt}k}\frac{t_i}{1 - t_k}x_i + t_kx_k\), and the inner point lies in \(I\) because \(I\) is an interval.

025B Counterexample 26.6.18

A convex function need not be differentiable: \(|x|\) has \(f'_-(0) = -1 {\lt} 1 = f'_+(0)\). On a closed interval a convex function need not be continuous at the endpoints: the function equal to \(0\) on \([0, 1)\) and to \(1\) at \(1\) is convex. The converse of the last statement of (3) fails: \(x^4\) is strictly convex, but its second derivative vanishes at \(0\).

011M Theorem 26.6.19

Let \(f(z) = \sum _na_nz^n\) have radius of convergence \(R {\gt} 0\). Then \(f\) is complex differentiable on \(\{ |z| {\lt} R\} \) with \(f'(z) = \sum _nna_nz^{n-1}\). Hence \(f\) has complex derivatives of all orders there, and \(a_n = f^{(n)}(0)/n!\). The same holds for real \(z\) in \((-R, R)\).

Proof â–¼

Let \(g(z) = \sum na_nz^{n-1}\), which has radius \(R\) by Theorem 26.5.7. Fix \(|z| {\lt} \rho {\lt} R\) and \(h \neq 0\) with \(|z| + |h| \leq \rho \). By the binomial formula, \((z + h)^n - z^n - nz^{n-1}h = \sum _{k \geq 2}\binom {n}{k}z^{n-k}h^k\), and \(\binom {n}{k} \leq \binom {n}{2}\binom {n-2}{k-2}\) for \(k \geq 2\), so its absolute value is at most \(\binom {n}{2}|h|^2(|z| + |h|)^{n-2} \leq n^2|h|^2\rho ^{n-2}\). Hence \(|(f(z + h) - f(z))/h - g(z)| \leq |h|\sum _n n^2|a_n|\rho ^{n-2}\), and the last series converges. Iterating gives all derivatives, and evaluating at \(0\) gives \(a_n\).
