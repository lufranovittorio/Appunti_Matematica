33.11 Manifolds of finite regularity03GD

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

Definition 33.2.1 asks the transition maps to be \(C^\infty \). Asking less gives a whole scale of notions between topological and smooth manifolds. They matter in analysis and geometric measure theory, where objects arise as limits or as solutions of equations and are not known in advance to be smooth. On a manifold of class \(C^k\) one can differentiate only as often as the transition maps allow. This section explains the scale, shows by examples what separates its levels, and connects its lowest levels to the rectifiable sets of measure theory.

03GE Definition 33.11.1

Let \(M\) be a topological \(n\)-manifold and \(k \in \{ 0, 1, 2, \ldots , \infty \} \). Two charts are \(C^k\)-compatible if the transition maps are of class \(C^k\) (for \(k = 0\): homeomorphisms, so all charts are compatible). A \(C^k\) atlas is an atlas of pairwise \(C^k\)-compatible charts, a \(C^k\) structure is a maximal \(C^k\) atlas, and a \(C^k\) manifold is a topological manifold with a \(C^k\) structure. Replacing “of class \(C^k\)” by other classes of maps gives:

  1. real analytic (\(C^\omega \)) manifolds, whose transition maps are given locally by convergent power series;

  2. Lipschitz manifolds, whose transition maps are locally bi-Lipschitz: each point of the domain has a neighbourhood on which the map and its inverse are Lipschitz;

  3. manifolds of class \(C^{k,\alpha }\), for \(0 {\lt} \alpha \leq 1\), whose transition maps are of class \(C^k\) with \(k\)-th derivatives locally \(\alpha \)-Hölder continuous, \(|D^kf(x) - D^kf(y)| \leq C|x - y|^\alpha \). The case \(C^{1,1}\) means \(C^1\) with locally Lipschitz derivative.

Submanifolds of \(\mathbb {R}^N\) of each class are defined by slice charts of that class, as in Definition 33.6.1; equivalently, a \(k\)-dimensional subset that is, near each of its points and after a rotation of coordinates, the graph of a map \(\mathbb {R}^k \supseteq U \to \mathbb {R}^{N-k}\) of that class. (For \(C^\infty \) this is Proposition 33.6.4 together with the implicit function theorem; the same proof works for every class \(C^k\) with \(k \geq 1\).)

The classes are ordered: \(C^\infty \Rightarrow C^{k+1} \Rightarrow C^{k,1} \Rightarrow C^{k,\alpha } \Rightarrow C^k \Rightarrow \cdots \Rightarrow C^1 \Rightarrow \) Lipschitz \(\Rightarrow C^0\), each implication meaning that an atlas of the first kind is one of the second kind (a \(C^1\) map is locally Lipschitz by the mean value inequality). Each implication is strict, already for curves in the plane.

03GF Example 33.11.2

  1. Lipschitz, not \(C^1\). The boundary of the square \([0, 1]^2\) is near each corner the graph of a function like \(y = |x|\) after a rotation by \(45^\circ \), which is Lipschitz with Lipschitz inverse along the curve, but has no tangent line at the corners. So it is a Lipschitz submanifold of \(\mathbb {R}^2\), and not a \(C^1\) one. The same holds for the boundary of every convex polygon, and more generally of every compact convex set with nonempty interior, which is locally the graph of a convex, hence locally Lipschitz, function.

  2. \(C^1\), not \(C^{1,1}\). The graph of \(f(x) = |x|^{3/2}\) is a \(C^1\) curve, as \(f^{\prime }(x) = \frac32\operatorname {sgn}(x)|x|^{1/2}\) is continuous. It is not \(C^{1,1}\) near \(0\) in this parametrisation, as \(|f^{\prime }(x) - f^{\prime }(0)|/|x| = \frac32|x|^{-1/2}\) is unbounded; it is \(C^{1,1/2}\).

  3. \(C^{1,1}\), not \(C^2\). The stadium, the boundary of a rectangle with two half-discs of radius \(r\) attached to two opposite sides, is made of two segments and two semicircles meeting tangentially. Near a junction point it is the graph of \(f(x) = r\) for \(x \leq 0\) and \(f(x) = \sqrt{r^2 - x^2}\) for \(x \geq 0\), with the centre of the half-disc at the origin. Then \(f^{\prime }(x) = 0\) for \(x \leq 0\) and \(f^{\prime }(x) = -x/\sqrt{r^2 - x^2}\) for \(x \geq 0\), which is continuous and Lipschitz near \(0\), as its derivative is bounded on each side. But \(f^{\prime \prime }\) jumps from \(0\) to \(-1/r\): the curvature jumps from \(0\) on the segment to \(1/r\) on the circle. A \(C^{1,1}\) curve has a curvature that exists almost everywhere and is bounded, without being continuous.

  4. \(C^2\), not \(C^3\). The graph of \(x \mapsto |x|^3\) is \(C^2\), not \(C^3\). More generally the graph of \(|x|^{k+\alpha }\) for \(0 {\lt} \alpha {\lt} 1\) is \(C^{k,\alpha }\) and no better near \(0\).

03GG Remark 33.11.3

What each level allows. Every construction of this chapter costs derivatives. On a \(C^k\) manifold with \(k \geq 1\) tangent spaces are defined, through curves or through charts. The tangent bundle \(TM\) is a \(C^{k-1}\) manifold, as its transition maps \((x, v) \mapsto (\psi \circ \varphi ^{-1}(x), D(\psi \circ \varphi ^{-1})_x(v))\) involve the first derivatives of the transition maps. A Riemannian metric can be at best of class \(C^{k-1}\); its Christoffel symbols involve one more derivative and its curvature two, so continuous curvature needs a \(C^3\) manifold and a \(C^2\) metric.

For submanifolds of \(\mathbb {R}^N\) the count is easiest to see on graphs. Take a hypersurface that near a point is the graph of a function \(u \colon U \to \mathbb {R}\), \(U \subseteq \mathbb {R}^n\) open, that is, the image of \(F(x) = (x, u(x))\). Its tangent plane at \(F(x)\) is spanned by the vectors \(\partial _iF(x) = e_i + \partial _iu(x)\, e_{n+1}\), so it is determined by the first derivatives \(\nabla u(x)\). The way the hypersurface bends, its second fundamental form (defined in Chapter 40), measures how the tangent plane turns as the point moves, so it involves the second derivatives. Concretely, its matrix in the basis \(\partial _1F, \ldots , \partial _nF\) consists of the components of the second derivatives of the parametrisation along the unit normal \(\nu \), \(h_{ij} = \langle \partial _i\partial _jF, \nu \rangle \); the tangential components of \(\partial _i\partial _jF\) only reflect the choice of coordinates (they are nonzero for suitable coordinates even on a plane). For a graph, \(\partial _i\partial _jF = \partial _i\partial _ju\, e_{n+1}\) is vertical, and the upward unit normal is \(\nu = (-\nabla u, 1)/\sqrt{1 + |\nabla u|^2}\), so

\[ h_{ij}(x) = \frac{\partial _i\partial _ju(x)}{\sqrt{1 + |\nabla u(x)|^2}} . \]

So the second fundamental form is the Hessian of \(u\) divided by a factor that involves only first derivatives: \(1/\sqrt{1 + |\nabla u|^2} = \langle e_{n+1}, \nu \rangle \) is the cosine of the angle between the tangent plane and the horizontal, and only that fraction of the vertical vector \(\partial _i\partial _jF\) is normal. Where the tangent plane is horizontal, \(\nabla u(x) = 0\), the factor is \(1\) and \(h\) is exactly the Hessian. The curvatures themselves are computed from \(h\) and the first fundamental form \(g_{ij} = \delta _{ij} + \partial _iu\, \partial _ju\), again of first order; for a curve, \(n = 1\), the curvature is \(u^{\prime \prime }/(1 + (u^{\prime })^2)^{3/2}\). Since \(u\) is at least \(C^1\), all these factors are continuous and nonzero. Hence the second fundamental form at \(F(x)\) exists exactly when the second derivatives of \(u\) exist at \(x\), and it is locally bounded, or continuous, exactly when they are. So the question is when \(u\) has second derivatives.

  1. If the submanifold is \(C^2\), \(u\) is \(C^2\) and the second fundamental form exists everywhere and is continuous.

  2. If it is only \(C^{1,1}\), then \(u\) is \(C^1\) and \(\nabla u \colon U \to \mathbb {R}^n\) is Lipschitz, with some constant \(L\), but it need not be differentiable. Rademacher’s theorem (Theorem 28.19.2), applied to the Lipschitz map \(\nabla u\), says that \(\nabla u\) is differentiable at almost every \(x \in U\); at such a point the second derivatives \(\partial _i\partial _ju(x)\) exist, and so does the second fundamental form. The second derivatives are moreover bounded by \(L\), hence so is \(h\), whose denominator is at least \(1\): if a map \(g\) with Lipschitz constant \(L\) is differentiable at \(x\), then \(|Dg(x)v| = \lim _{t \to 0}|g(x + tv) - g(x)|/|t| \leq L|v|\). So the second fundamental form exists at almost every point (for Lebesgue measure on \(U\), which corresponds to the area measure on the graph) and is bounded, but it need not be continuous: for the stadium of Example 33.11.2(3) the curvature is \(0\) on the segments and \(1/r\) on the circles, and it is undefined at the four junction points.

  3. If it is only \(C^{1,\alpha }\) with \(\alpha {\lt} 1\), the second derivatives may be unbounded: for \(|x|^{3/2}\) the second derivative \(\frac34|x|^{-1/2}\) blows up at \(0\).

Bounded curvature without continuity is exactly what many geometric arguments need, which is why \(C^{1,1}\) is a natural class: for instance, a compact \(C^{1,1}\) hypersurface has a tubular neighbourhood of positive width on which the nearest point is unique, as for \(C^2\) ones: it is a set of positive reach in the sense of [Fed59]. One level lower, on a Lipschitz manifold the tangent planes themselves exist only almost everywhere, by the same theorem applied to \(u\) instead of \(\nabla u\), which is still enough to integrate differential forms with bounded measurable coefficients.

03GH Theorem 33.11.4 Whitney

Let \(1 \leq k {\lt} \infty \). Every \(C^k\) structure on a manifold contains a \(C^\infty \) atlas, so every \(C^k\) manifold is \(C^k\)-diffeomorphic to a smooth manifold; and two smooth manifolds that are \(C^k\)-diffeomorphic are diffeomorphic.

This is [Hir76, Chapter 2, Section 2]. So for \(k \geq 1\) the finite regularity of a manifold is not a restriction: one may always pass to a smooth structure, and statements about manifolds of class \(C^k\) reduce to smooth ones. What remains is the regularity of objects inside a smooth manifold, such as submanifolds, maps, metrics or solutions of equations, and there the scale above is essential: the stadium of Example 33.11.2(3) is a \(C^{1,1}\) submanifold of \(\mathbb {R}^2\), diffeomorphic to the circle as an abstract manifold, but not a \(C^2\) submanifold. For \(k = 0\) the theorem fails: there are topological manifolds with no smooth structure at all, for instance a closed simply connected \(4\)-manifold whose intersection form is the \(E_8\) form, which exists by Freedman’s theorem [Fre82] and admits no smooth structure by Donaldson’s theorem [Don83].

03GI Remark 33.11.5

Rectifiable sets: manifolds up to measure zero. At the bottom of the scale, geometric measure theory replaces submanifolds by the countably \(k\)-rectifiable sets of Definition 28.22.8: sets covered, up to an \(\mathcal{H}^k\)-null set, by countably many Lipschitz images of subsets of \(\mathbb {R}^k\). Every \(k\)-dimensional submanifold \(S \subseteq \mathbb {R}^N\) of class \(C^1\), in particular every smooth one, is countably \(k\)-rectifiable: \(S\) is second countable, so it is covered by countably many slice charts, and the inverse of each is a \(C^1\) map from an open subset of \(\mathbb {R}^k\) onto an open part of \(S\), whose image is countably \(k\)-rectifiable by Proposition 28.22.9(3); then use part (1) of the same proposition. Conversely, a set is countably \(k\)-rectifiable if and only if, up to an \(\mathcal{H}^k\)-null set, it is contained in a countable union of \(C^1\) submanifolds of dimension \(k\) [Fed69, 3.2.29]. So a rectifiable set is a “manifold almost everywhere”: at \(\mathcal{H}^k\)-almost every point it has an approximate tangent plane, and the area formula integrates over it. But it may be far from a manifold globally. The union \(\bigcup _{q \in \mathbb {Q}\cap [0, 1]}\{ q\} \times [0, 1]\) of countably many vertical segments is countably \(1\)-rectifiable, and it is dense in the square \([0, 1]^2\). No point of it has a neighbourhood homeomorphic to an interval: every neighbourhood of a point in it is disconnected, split by the vertical lines with irrational abscissa. Rectifiable sets with integer multiplicities and orientations are the integral currents mentioned in Chapter 37, the generalised surfaces in which Plateau’s problem is solved.
