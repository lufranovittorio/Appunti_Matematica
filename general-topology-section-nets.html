18.12 Nets and filters039Q

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

In metric spaces sequences describe everything: closures, continuity and compactness. In general spaces they do not (Counterexample 18.3.8). The reason is that a point may have no countable neighbourhood basis, so that no sequence can “approach” it through all its neighbourhoods. The remedy is to index by sets larger than \(\mathbb {N}\), ordered only partially: what one needs of \(\mathbb {N}\) is only that any two indices have a common later one.

039R Definition 18.12.1

A directed set is a nonempty set \(D\) with a relation \(\leq \) that is reflexive and transitive and such that any two elements \(d_1, d_2\) have an upper bound \(d \geq d_1, d_2\). A net in a space \(X\) is a map \(D \to X\), \(d \mapsto x_d\), from a directed set. It converges to \(x\), written \(x_d \to x\), if for every open \(U \ni x\) there is \(d_0\) with \(x_d \in U\) for all \(d \geq d_0\) (the net is eventually in \(U\)). A point \(x\) is a cluster point of the net if for every open \(U \ni x\) and every \(d_0\) there is \(d \geq d_0\) with \(x_d \in U\) (the net is frequently in \(U\)).

039S Example 18.12.2

  1. A sequence is a net indexed by \(\mathbb {N}\), and the definitions agree with the usual ones.

  2. The neighbourhood net. For \(x \in X\) the set \(\mathcal{N}_x\) of open neighbourhoods of \(x\), ordered by reverse inclusion (\(U \leq V\) if \(V \subseteq U\)), is directed, an upper bound of \(U\) and \(V\) being \(U \cap V\). If \(x_U \in U\) is chosen for every \(U\), then \(x_U \to x\): for \(U \ni x\) open and \(V \geq U\), \(x_V \in V \subseteq U\). This is the basic example; it approaches \(x\) through all neighbourhoods at once.

  3. Unordered sums. Let \((a_i)_{i \in I}\) be real numbers indexed by any set. The finite subsets \(F \subseteq I\), ordered by inclusion, form a directed set, and \(s_F = \sum _{i \in F}a_i\) is a net. If all \(a_i \geq 0\) and \(s = \sup _Fs_F {\lt} \infty \), then \(s_F \to s\): given \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\) choose \(F_0\) with \(s_{F_0} {\gt} s - \varepsilon \); for \(F \supseteq F_0\), \(s - \varepsilon {\lt} s_F \leq s\). This is how sums \(\sum _{i \in I}\) over uncountable index sets, for example in Hilbert spaces with uncountable orthonormal bases, are defined.

  4. Riemann sums. The tagged partitions of \([a, b]\), ordered by refinement of the partitions, form a directed set (a common refinement of two partitions is obtained by taking the union of their division points), and Riemann sums of a function \(f\) form a net. The Riemann integral of Chapter 27 can be defined as the limit of this net.

039T Proposition 18.12.3

Let \(X\) be a space.

  1. A point \(x\) lies in \(\overline{A}\) if and only if some net in \(A\) converges to \(x\).

  2. A map \(f \colon X \to Y\) is continuous at \(x\) if and only if \(f(x_d) \to f(x)\) for every net \(x_d \to x\). So \(f\) is continuous if and only if it preserves convergence of nets.

  3. \(X\) is Hausdorff if and only if every net in \(X\) has at most one limit.

  4. A net in a product \(\prod _iX_i\) converges to \(x\) if and only if \(p_i(x_d) \to p_i(x)\) for every \(i\).

  5. If \(X\) is first countable, nets may be replaced by sequences in (1) and (2).

Proof â–¼

(1) If \(x_d \to x\) with \(x_d \in A\), every open \(U \ni x\) contains some \(x_d\), so meets \(A\), and \(x \in \overline{A}\) by Lemma 18.2.4. Conversely, if \(x \in \overline{A}\), every \(U \in \mathcal{N}_x\) meets \(A\); choosing \(x_U \in U \cap A\) gives a net in \(A\) converging to \(x\) (Example 18.12.2(2)).

(2) Let \(f\) be continuous at \(x\) (Remark 18.3.6) and \(x_d \to x\). For open \(V \ni f(x)\) there is an open \(U \ni x\) with \(f(U) \subseteq V\), and \(x_d\) is eventually in \(U\), so \(f(x_d)\) is eventually in \(V\). Conversely, if \(f\) is not continuous at \(x\), there is an open \(V \ni f(x)\) such that no \(U \in \mathcal{N}_x\) has \(f(U) \subseteq V\). Choose \(x_U \in U\) with \(f(x_U) \notin V\). Then \(x_U \to x\), but \(f(x_U)\) is never in \(V\).

(3) Let \(X\) be Hausdorff and \(x_d \to x\), \(x_d \to y\) with \(x \neq y\). For disjoint open \(U \ni x\), \(V \ni y\) there are \(d_1\), \(d_2\) with \(x_d \in U\) for \(d \geq d_1\) and \(x_d \in V\) for \(d \geq d_2\); an upper bound \(d\) of \(d_1, d_2\) gives \(x_d \in U \cap V = \emptyset \), a contradiction. Conversely, suppose \(x \neq y\) have no disjoint neighbourhoods. The pairs \((U, V) \in \mathcal{N}_x \times \mathcal{N}_y\), ordered by reverse inclusion in both components, form a directed set; choose \(x_{(U, V)} \in U \cap V\). This net converges to \(x\) and to \(y\).

(4) The projections are continuous, so (2) gives one direction. Conversely, let \(p_i(x_d) \to p_i(x)\) for all \(i\), and let \(W = \prod _iU_i\) be a basic open set containing \(x\), with \(U_i = X_i\) except for \(i \in \{ i_1, \ldots , i_k\} \) (Proposition 18.4.7(1)). There are \(d_1, \ldots , d_k\) with \(p_{i_j}(x_d) \in U_{i_j}\) for \(d \geq d_j\); for \(d\) above an upper bound of \(d_1, \ldots , d_k\), which exists by induction on \(k\), \(x_d \in W\). Every open set containing \(x\) contains such a \(W\).

(5) Let \(U_1, U_2, \ldots \) be open neighbourhoods of \(x\) such that every open set containing \(x\) contains one of them, and \(V_n = U_1 \cap \cdots \cap U_n\). If \(x_n \in V_n\) for all \(n\), then \(x_n \to x\), since \(x_m \in V_m \subseteq V_n \subseteq U_n\) for \(m \geq n\). In the proofs of (1) and (2), use the \(V_n\) in place of \(\mathcal{N}_x\).

By (4), convergence in a product is coordinatewise convergence; this is why the product topology on a space of functions \(\prod _{s \in S}\mathbb {R}= \mathbb {R}^S\) is called the topology of pointwise convergence. The following example shows that sequences are not enough there.

039U Example 18.12.4

In \(\mathbb {R}^\mathbb {R}\) with the product topology let \(A\) be the set of functions \(f \colon \mathbb {R}\to \{ 0, 1\} \) with \(f(s) = 1\) for all but finitely many \(s\). The zero function lies in \(\overline{A}\): a basic neighbourhood of \(0\) restricts the values at finitely many points \(s_1, \ldots , s_k\), and contains the function that vanishes at these points and equals \(1\) elsewhere. A net in \(A\) converging to \(0\) is given by \(f_F = \mathbf{1}_{\mathbb {R}\setminus F}\), indexed by the finite sets \(F \subseteq \mathbb {R}\). But no sequence \(f_n \in A\) converges to \(0\): the sets \(\{ f_n = 0\} \) are finite, so their union is countable, and at a point \(s\) outside it \(f_n(s) = 1\) for all \(n\), so \(f_n(s) \not\to 0\). So \(\mathbb {R}^\mathbb {R}\) is not first countable at \(0\), by Proposition 18.12.3(5).

For compactness one needs the analogue of subsequences. A subnet may run through the original net in a more complicated way than a subsequence, possibly repeating indices; what matters is only that it goes arbitrarily far out.

039V Definition 18.12.5

A subnet of a net \((x_d)_{d \in D}\) is a net \((x_{\varphi (e)})_{e \in E}\), where \(E\) is a directed set and \(\varphi \colon E \to D\) is a map such that for every \(d_0 \in D\) there is \(e_0 \in E\) with \(\varphi (e) \geq d_0\) for all \(e \geq e_0\).

A subsequence \((x_{n_k})\), with \(n_1 {\lt} n_2 {\lt} \cdots \), is a subnet, as \(n_k \geq k\). If \(x_d \to x\), every subnet converges to \(x\): if \(x_d \in U\) for \(d \geq d_0\), then \(x_{\varphi (e)} \in U\) for \(e \geq e_0\).

039W Lemma 18.12.6

A point \(x\) is a cluster point of a net \((x_d)_{d \in D}\) if and only if some subnet converges to \(x\).

Proof â–¼

Let \((x_{\varphi (e)})\) converge to \(x\), let \(U \ni x\) be open and \(d_0 \in D\). There are \(e_0\) with \(\varphi (e) \geq d_0\) for \(e \geq e_0\) and \(e_1\) with \(x_{\varphi (e)} \in U\) for \(e \geq e_1\); for an upper bound \(e\) of \(e_0, e_1\), \(d = \varphi (e) \geq d_0\) and \(x_d \in U\). So \(x\) is a cluster point.

Conversely, let \(x\) be a cluster point, and let \(E\) be the set of pairs \((d, U)\) with \(d \in D\), \(U \in \mathcal{N}_x\) and \(x_d \in U\), ordered by \((d, U) \leq (d^{\prime }, U^{\prime })\) if \(d \leq d^{\prime }\) and \(U^{\prime } \subseteq U\). It is nonempty, as \((d, X) \in E\) for every \(d\). It is directed: given \((d_1, U_1)\) and \((d_2, U_2)\), let \(d_0\) be an upper bound of \(d_1, d_2\); as \(x\) is a cluster point, there is \(d \geq d_0\) with \(x_d \in U_1 \cap U_2\), and \((d, U_1 \cap U_2)\) is an upper bound. Let \(\varphi (d, U) = d\). Given \(d_0\), every \((d, U) \geq (d_0, X)\) has \(d \geq d_0\), so this is a subnet. It converges to \(x\): for \(U \in \mathcal{N}_x\) choose \(d\) with \(x_d \in U\); then for \((d^{\prime }, U^{\prime }) \geq (d, U)\) we have \(x_{d^{\prime }} \in U^{\prime } \subseteq U\).

039X Theorem 18.12.7

For a space \(X\) the following are equivalent:

  1. \(X\) is compact;

  2. every net in \(X\) has a cluster point;

  3. every net in \(X\) has a convergent subnet.

Proof â–¼

(2) and (3) are equivalent by Lemma 18.12.6.

(1)\(\Rightarrow \)(2): Suppose the net \((x_d)\) has no cluster point. Then every \(x \in X\) has an open \(U_x \ni x\) and an index \(d_x\) with \(x_d \notin U_x\) for all \(d \geq d_x\). Finitely many \(U_{x_1}, \ldots , U_{x_n}\) cover \(X\). For an upper bound \(d\) of \(d_{x_1}, \ldots , d_{x_n}\), the point \(x_d\) lies in none of the \(U_{x_j}\), a contradiction.

(2)\(\Rightarrow \)(1): Suppose the open cover \(\mathcal{U}\) has no finite subcover. The finite subfamilies \(\mathcal{F} \subseteq \mathcal{U}\), ordered by inclusion, form a directed set, and we may choose \(x_{\mathcal{F}} \notin \bigcup \mathcal{F}\). Any \(x \in X\) lies in some \(U \in \mathcal{U}\), and \(x_{\mathcal{F}} \notin U\) for all \(\mathcal{F} \supseteq \{ U\} \); so \(x\) is not a cluster point.

For metric spaces, compactness is equivalent to sequential compactness (Theorem 18.7.4); in general neither implies the other. Here is a compact space that is not sequentially compact.

039Y Example 18.12.8

Let \(S = \{ 0, 1\} ^\mathbb {N}\) be the set of \(0\)-\(1\) sequences, and \(X = \{ 0, 1\} ^S\), a product of discrete two-point spaces, which is compact by Tychonoff’s theorem (Theorem 18.6.8). Let \(f_n \in X\) be the point \(f_n(s) = s(n)\). Given \(n_1 {\lt} n_2 {\lt} \cdots \), choose \(s \in S\) with \(s(n_k) = 0\) for even \(k\) and \(s(n_k) = 1\) for odd \(k\). Then \(f_{n_k}(s)\) alternates between \(1\) and \(0\), so by Proposition 18.12.3(4) the subsequence \((f_{n_k})\) does not converge. So \((f_n)\) has no convergent subsequence, although it has convergent subnets by Theorem 18.12.7.

Filters are a second, equivalent language, often more convenient because they need no index set. We use the definitions of Chapter 5: a filter on a set \(X\) is a family \(\mathcal{F}\) of subsets with \(X \in \mathcal{F}\), \(\emptyset \notin \mathcal{F}\), closed under finite intersections and supersets, and an ultrafilter is a filter containing \(A\) or \(X \setminus A\) for every \(A \subseteq X\) (Definition 5.3.1). Every family of subsets with the finite intersection property lies in an ultrafilter (Lemma 5.3.2).

039Z Definition 18.12.9

A filter \(\mathcal{F}\) on a space \(X\) converges to \(x\) if every open set containing \(x\) belongs to \(\mathcal{F}\). For a map \(f \colon X \to Y\), the image filter is \(f_*\mathcal{F} = \{ B \subseteq Y : f^{-1}(B) \in \mathcal{F}\} \).

The neighbourhood filter of \(x\), the sets containing an open neighbourhood of \(x\), converges to \(x\), and a filter converges to \(x\) if and only if it contains the neighbourhood filter. A net \((x_d)\) defines the filter of sets \(B\) that contain a tail \(\{ x_d : d \geq d_0\} \), which converges to \(x\) exactly when the net does; so filters and nets describe the same convergence. The image filter is a filter (\(f^{-1}(\emptyset ) = \emptyset \) and preimages preserve intersections and inclusions), and if \(\mathcal{F}\) is an ultrafilter, so is \(f_*\mathcal{F}\), since \(f^{-1}(Y \setminus B) = X \setminus f^{-1}(B)\). If \(f\) is continuous and \(\mathcal{F} \to x\), then \(f_*\mathcal{F} \to f(x)\): for open \(V \ni f(x)\), \(f^{-1}(V)\) is an open set containing \(x\), so it lies in \(\mathcal{F}\).

03A0Lean Theorem 18.12.10

A space \(X\) is compact if and only if every ultrafilter on \(X\) converges.

Proof â–¼

Let \(X\) be compact and \(\mathcal{U}\) an ultrafilter without limit. Every \(x\) has an open \(U_x \ni x\) with \(U_x \notin \mathcal{U}\), so \(X \setminus U_x \in \mathcal{U}\). Finitely many \(U_{x_1}, \ldots , U_{x_n}\) cover \(X\), and then \(\emptyset = \bigcap _j(X \setminus U_{x_j}) \in \mathcal{U}\), a contradiction.

Conversely, let \(\mathcal{C}\) be an open cover without finite subcover. Then the complements \(X \setminus U\), \(U \in \mathcal{C}\), have the finite intersection property, so they lie in an ultrafilter \(\mathcal{U}\). If \(\mathcal{U} \to x\) and \(x \in U \in \mathcal{C}\), then \(U \in \mathcal{U}\) and \(X \setminus U \in \mathcal{U}\), so \(\emptyset \in \mathcal{U}\), a contradiction.

With ultrafilters Tychonoff’s theorem has a two-line proof, which shows clearly where the axiom of choice enters: in choosing the limits.

03A1 Corollary 18.12.11

A product of compact spaces is compact.

Proof â–¼

Let \(\mathcal{U}\) be an ultrafilter on \(X = \prod _iX_i\). Each \((p_i)_*\mathcal{U}\) is an ultrafilter on the compact space \(X_i\), so by Theorem 18.12.10 we may choose a limit \(x_i\) of it, and let \(x = (x_i)_i\). A basic open set containing \(x\) is \(W = p_{i_1}^{-1}(U_1) \cap \cdots \cap p_{i_k}^{-1}(U_k)\) with \(U_j \ni x_{i_j}\) open; then \(U_j \in (p_{i_j})_*\mathcal{U}\), that is, \(p_{i_j}^{-1}(U_j) \in \mathcal{U}\), and so \(W \in \mathcal{U}\). Hence every open set containing \(x\) is in \(\mathcal{U}\), \(\mathcal{U} \to x\), and \(X\) is compact by the theorem.

03A2 Example 18.12.12

Limits along an ultrafilter. An ultrafilter on \(\mathbb {N}\) is free if it is not principal; free ultrafilters exist, since the cofinite subsets of \(\mathbb {N}\) have the finite intersection property, and an ultrafilter containing them contains no finite set, so is not principal. Let \(\mathcal{U}\) be a free ultrafilter and \((a_n)\) a bounded real sequence, \(a \colon \mathbb {N}\to [-M, M]\). The ultrafilter \(a_*\mathcal{U}\) on the compact Hausdorff space \([-M, M]\) has a unique limit (uniqueness as in Proposition 18.12.3(3)), written \(\lim _{\mathcal{U}}a_n\): it is the number \(L\) such that \(\{ n : |a_n - L| {\lt} \varepsilon \} \in \mathcal{U}\) for every \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\). Every bounded sequence has such a limit, for instance \(\lim _{\mathcal{U}}(-1)^n\) is \(1\) or \(-1\) according as \(\mathcal{U}\) contains the even or the odd numbers. The limit is linear and multiplicative, lies between \(\liminf a_n\) and \(\limsup a_n\), and equals \(\lim a_n\) when this exists, since every set \(\{ n : |a_n - L| {\lt} \varepsilon \} \) is then cofinite. This is the device behind ultraproducts (Chapter 5). The limit is not shift invariant (for \((-1)^n\) and its shift it gives opposite values), but its value on the averages \(\frac1n(a_1 + \cdots + a_n)\) is, since the averages of \((a_n)\) and of \((a_{n+1})\) differ by at most \(2M/n\); this gives a Banach limit, a shift-invariant positive linear functional on bounded sequences extending \(\lim \).

03A3 Remark 18.12.13

Nets were introduced by Moore and Smith (1922) and filters by Cartan (1937); Bourbaki’s General Topology is written in the language of filters. Sequences still suffice in first countable spaces (Proposition 18.12.3(5)), which include all metric spaces and manifolds, and they are what one uses there. The exotic compact spaces of Example 18.12.8 and of the next section show why nets or filters are unavoidable in general.
