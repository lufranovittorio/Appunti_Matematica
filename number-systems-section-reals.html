4.8 The real numbers009Y

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

009Z Definition 4.8.1

Let \(\mathcal{C}\) be the set of Cauchy sequences in \(\mathbb {Q}\) and \(\mathcal{N} \subseteq \mathcal{C}\) the set of sequences converging to \(0\). For \(x, y \in \mathcal{C}\) write \(x \sim y\) if \(x - y \in \mathcal{N}\). The set of real numbers is \(\mathbb {R}= \mathcal{C}/{\sim }\). Operations are defined termwise: \([x] + [y] = [(x_n + y_n)_n]\) and \([x] \cdot [y] = [(x_n y_n)_n]\). A real number \([x]\) is positive if there are \(\varepsilon \in \mathbb {Q}\), \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\), and \(N\) with \(x_n {\gt} \varepsilon \) for all \(n \geq N\), and \([x] {\lt} [y]\) means that \([y] - [x]\) is positive. A rational number \(q\) is identified with the class of the constant sequence \((q)_n\).

00A0 Lemma 4.8.2

  1. Every \(x \in \mathcal{C}\) is bounded, and \(\mathcal{C}\) is closed under termwise sums and products.

  2. \(\mathcal{N}\) is closed under sums, and \(xz \in \mathcal{N}\) for \(x \in \mathcal{C}\) and \(z \in \mathcal{N}\). In particular \(\sim \) is an equivalence relation compatible with sums and products.

  3. If \(x \in \mathcal{C} \setminus \mathcal{N}\), there are a rational \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\) and \(N\) such that either \(x_n {\gt} \varepsilon \) for all \(n \geq N\), or \(x_n {\lt} -\varepsilon \) for all \(n \geq N\).

Proof â–¼

(1) Boundedness is proved as in Proposition 4.7.6. If \(|x_n|, |y_n| \leq M\), then \(|x_ny_n - x_my_m| \leq M|y_n - y_m| + M|x_n - x_m|\), and similarly for sums. (2) If \(|x_n| \leq M\) and \(z \to 0\), then \(|x_n z_n| \leq M|z_n| \to 0\). Sums are similar. If \(x \sim x'\) and \(y \sim y'\), then \(xy - x'y' = x(y - y') + y'(x - x') \in \mathcal{N}\). (3) Since \(x \notin \mathcal{N}\), there is a rational \(\delta {\gt} 0\) such that \(|x_n| \geq \delta \) for infinitely many \(n\). Choose \(N\) with \(|x_n - x_m| {\lt} \delta /2\) for \(m, n \geq N\), and \(m \geq N\) with \(|x_m| \geq \delta \). If \(x_m \geq \delta \) then \(x_n {\gt} \delta /2\) for all \(n \geq N\); if \(x_m \leq -\delta \) then \(x_n {\lt} -\delta /2\) for all \(n \geq N\). Take \(\varepsilon = \delta /2\).

00A1 Theorem 4.8.3

\(\mathbb {R}\) is a complete ordered field, and \(\mathbb {Q}\to \mathbb {R}\) is an injective order-preserving ring homomorphism. By Theorem 4.7.7, \(\mathbb {R}\) is the unique complete ordered field up to unique isomorphism.

Proof â–¼

By Lemma 4.8.2, the operations are well defined, and the ring axioms hold termwise. If \([x] \neq 0\), then by part (3) of the lemma there is \(N\) with \(|x_n| {\gt} \varepsilon \) for \(n \geq N\). Let \(y_n = x_n^{-1}\) for \(n \geq N\) and \(y_n = 0\) otherwise; since \(|y_n - y_m| = |x_m - x_n|/|x_n x_m| \leq \varepsilon ^{-2}|x_m - x_n|\) for \(m, n \geq N\), \(y\) is Cauchy, and \(xy - 1\) is eventually \(0\). So \(\mathbb {R}\) is a field.

Positivity is well defined: if \(x_n {\gt} \varepsilon \) for \(n \geq N\) and \(z \in \mathcal{N}\), then \(x_n + z_n {\gt} \varepsilon /2\) for large \(n\). By part (3), every nonzero real number is either positive or its negative is positive, and not both; sums and products of positive numbers are positive. It follows that \({\lt}\) is a strict total order compatible with the ring operations, so \(\mathbb {R}\) is an ordered field. The embedding of \(\mathbb {Q}\) is clearly an injective order-preserving homomorphism. \(\mathbb {R}\) is Archimedean: if \(|x_n| \leq M \in \mathbb {N}\) for all \(n\), then \([x] {\lt} M + 1\).

Convergence of rational approximations. Let \(x \in \mathcal{C}\). For rational \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\) choose \(N\) with \(|x_n - x_m| {\lt} \varepsilon /2\) for \(m, n \geq N\); then for \(n \geq N\) the sequence \((\varepsilon - |x_n - x_m|)_m\) is eventually \({\gt} \varepsilon /2\), so \(|[x] - x_n| {\lt} \varepsilon \) in \(\mathbb {R}\). Thus the rational sequence \((x_n)\) converges to \([x]\) in \(\mathbb {R}\); by density of \(\mathbb {Q}\) this holds for every real \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\).

Completeness. Let \(A \subseteq \mathbb {R}\) be nonempty and bounded above. Let \(u_0\) be the least integer that is an upper bound of \(A\), and \(a_0\) an integer smaller than some element of \(A\); both exist by the Archimedean property and Theorem 4.4.2, and \(a_0 {\lt} u_0\). Define rationals \(a_n {\lt} u_n\) by recursion: let \(m = (a_n + u_n)/2\); if \(m\) is an upper bound of \(A\), put \((a_{n+1}, u_{n+1}) = (a_n, m)\), and otherwise \((a_{n+1}, u_{n+1}) = (m, u_n)\). Then every \(u_n\) is an upper bound, no \(a_n\) is, the sequences are monotone, and \(u_n - a_n = (u_0 - a_0)/2^n \leq (u_0 - a_0)/n\) for \(n \geq 1\). Hence \((u_n)\) and \((a_n)\) are Cauchy sequences in \(\mathbb {Q}\) with the same class \(s \in \mathbb {R}\), and both converge to \(s\) in \(\mathbb {R}\). If \(a \in A\), then \(a \leq u_n\) for all \(n\), hence \(a \leq s\). If \(t {\lt} s\), then \(a_n {\gt} t\) for some \(n\), and since \(a_n\) is not an upper bound, neither is \(t\). So \(s = \sup A\).

00A2 Remark 4.8.4

Dedekind’s construction [Ded72] defines a real number as a cut: a nonempty proper subset \(\alpha \subsetneq \mathbb {Q}\) which is downward closed and has no largest element. The order is inclusion and the supremum of a bounded set of cuts is their union. It also produces a complete ordered field (Exercise 4.10.1), which by Theorem 4.7.7 is canonically isomorphic to the one constructed above. See [Rud76, Appendix to Chapter 1].

00A3 Notation 4.8.5

For \(a \leq b\) in \(\mathbb {R}\) we write \([a, b] = \{ x : a \leq x \leq b\} \), \((a, b) = \{ x : a {\lt} x {\lt} b\} \), and similarly \([a, b)\), \((a, b]\), \([a, \infty )\) and so on. We use the extended real line \([-\infty , \infty ]\), obtained by adding a least and a greatest element, where convenient.

00A4 Proposition 4.8.6

\(|\mathbb {R}| = 2^{\aleph _0}\). In particular \(\mathbb {R}\) is uncountable.

Proof â–¼

The map \(x \mapsto \{ q \in \mathbb {Q}: q {\lt} x\} \) is injective by density of \(\mathbb {Q}\), so \(\mathbb {R}\preceq \mathcal{P}(\mathbb {Q})\). Since \(\mathbb {Q}\) is countable (it injects into \(\mathbb {Z}\times \mathbb {Z}\), which injects into \(\mathbb {N}\times \mathbb {N}\), which is countable by Corollary 3.6.13), \(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb {Q}) \preceq \mathcal{P}(\mathbb {N})\).

Conversely, for \(S \subseteq \mathbb {N}\) let \(f(S) = \sup \{ \sum _{n \in S, n {\lt} N} 2 \cdot 3^{-(n+1)} : N \in \mathbb {N}\} \); the partial sums are at most \(\sum _{n {\lt} N} 2 \cdot 3^{-(n+1)} = 1 - 3^{-N} {\lt} 1\). Let \(S \neq T\) and let \(n\) be the least element of the symmetric difference, say \(n \in S \setminus T\). The sums over \(S\) and \(T\) agree below \(n\), the term \(2 \cdot 3^{-(n+1)}\) occurs only for \(S\), and the terms of \(T\) above \(n\) add up to at most \(\sum _{k = n+1}^{M} 2 \cdot 3^{-(k+1)} {\lt} 3^{-(n+1)}\). Hence \(f(S) - f(T) \geq 2 \cdot 3^{-(n+1)} - 3^{-(n+1)} {\gt} 0\), so \(f\) is injective. By Theorem 3.6.2, \(\mathbb {R}\approx \mathcal{P}(\mathbb {N})\), and \(\mathbb {R}\) is uncountable by Theorem 3.6.3.
