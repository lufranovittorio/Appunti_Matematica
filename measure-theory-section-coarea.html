28.21 The coarea formula0357

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

The area formula measures a set by parametrising it. The coarea formula measures it by slicing. The model is Fubini’s theorem: the volume of \(A \subseteq \mathbb {R}^n\) is the integral of the \((n-1)\)-dimensional volumes of the slices \(A \cap \{ x_n = t\} \). If the slices are the level sets \(f^{-1}(t)\) of a function \(f\) which are not parallel hyperplanes, the slab between the levels \(t\) and \(t + dt\) has thickness about \(dt/|\nabla f|\): where the levels are crowded, the slab is thin. So the volume of the slab is about \(\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\text{slice})\, dt/|\nabla f|\), and integrating \(|\nabla f|\) instead of \(1\) compensates exactly:

\[ \int _A|\nabla f|\, d\lambda ^n = \int _\mathbb {R}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\bigl(A \cap f^{-1}(t)\bigr)\, dt . \]

We prove this for \(C^1\) functions without critical points; the general statement is in Remark 28.20.6. Throughout, \(n \geq 2\).

0358 Lemma 28.21.1

Let \(v_1, \ldots , v_{n-1} \in \mathbb {R}^n\) be linearly independent, \(V\) the \(n \times (n - 1)\) matrix with these columns, \(\nu \) a unit vector orthogonal to all \(v_i\), and \(w \in \mathbb {R}^n\). Then

\[ |\det (v_1, \ldots , v_{n-1}, w)| = JV \cdot |\langle w, \nu \rangle | . \]

In words: the volume of a parallelepiped is the \((n-1)\)-dimensional volume of its base times its height. Recall that \(JV = \sqrt{\det (V^TV)}\) is the \((n-1)\)-dimensional volume of the base (Lemma 28.20.2).

Proof â–¼

Write \(w = u + \langle w, \nu \rangle \nu \) with \(u\) in the span of the \(v_i\). The determinant is linear in the last column and vanishes when the columns are dependent, so \(\det (V, w) = \langle w, \nu \rangle \det (V, \nu )\). For the matrix \(M = (V, \nu )\), \(M^TM\) is block diagonal with blocks \(V^TV\) and \(\langle \nu , \nu \rangle = 1\), because \(V^T\nu = 0\). Hence \((\det M)^2 = \det (M^TM) = \det (V^TV) = (JV)^2\).

0359 Theorem 28.21.2 Coarea formula

Let \(U \subseteq \mathbb {R}^n\) be open and \(f \colon U \to \mathbb {R}\) of class \(C^1\) with \(\nabla f(x) \neq 0\) for all \(x \in U\). For every Borel set \(A \subseteq U\) the function \(t \mapsto \mathcal{H}^{n-1}(A \cap f^{-1}(t))\) is Lebesgue measurable, and

\[ \int _A|\nabla f|\, d\lambda ^n = \int _\mathbb {R}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\bigl(A \cap f^{-1}(t)\bigr)\, dt . \]

More generally, for every Borel function \(g \colon U \to [0, \infty ]\),

\begin{equation} \int _Ug\, |\nabla f|\, d\lambda ^n = \int _\mathbb {R}\Bigl(\int _{f^{-1}(t)}g\, d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\Bigr)dt . \end{equation}
28.5

Proof â–¼

Step 1: local coordinates adapted to \(f\). Let \(x_0 \in U\). Some partial derivative \(\partial _if(x_0)\) is nonzero. A permutation of the coordinates is an isometry of \(\mathbb {R}^n\), so it preserves \(\lambda ^n\) (Lemma 28.11.1), \(\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\) and \(|\nabla f|\); so we may assume \(\partial _nf(x_0) \neq 0\). The map \(\psi (x) = (x_1, \ldots , x_{n-1}, f(x))\) has \(\det D\psi (x_0) = \partial _nf(x_0) \neq 0\). By the inverse function theorem (Theorem 27.11.2) there are open sets \(V \ni x_0\) and \(W\) such that \(\psi \colon V \to W\) is a \(C^1\)-diffeomorphism. Let \(\phi = \psi ^{-1} \colon W \to V\), and write points of \(W \subseteq \mathbb {R}^{n-1} \times \mathbb {R}\) as \((y, t)\). Since \(\psi (\phi (y, t)) = (y, t)\),

\[ f(\phi (y, t)) = t \qquad \text{for } (y, t) \in W . \]

For fixed \(t\) let \(W_t = \{ y : (y, t) \in W\} \), open in \(\mathbb {R}^{n-1}\), and \(\phi _t = \phi (-, t) \colon W_t \to \mathbb {R}^n\). It is injective and \(C^1\), and \(D\phi _t\) is injective, as its columns are the first \(n - 1\) columns of the invertible matrix \(D\phi \). Its image is \(V \cap f^{-1}(t)\): a point \(x \in V\) has \(f(x) = t\) if and only if \(\psi (x) = (y, t)\) for some \(y\).

Step 2: the Jacobian identity. We claim \(|\det D\phi (y, t)|\, |\nabla f(\phi (y, t))| = J\phi _t(y)\). Differentiating \(f(\phi (y, t)) = t\) with the chain rule gives \(\langle \nabla f, \partial _{y_i}\phi \rangle = 0\) for \(i {\lt} n\) and \(\langle \nabla f, \partial _t\phi \rangle = 1\), all evaluated at \(\phi (y, t)\) resp. \((y, t)\). So \(\nu = \nabla f/|\nabla f|\) is a unit vector orthogonal to the columns \(\partial _{y_i}\phi \) of \(D\phi _t\), and Lemma 28.21.1 with \(w = \partial _t\phi \) gives

\[ |\det D\phi | = J\phi _t\cdot |\langle \partial _t\phi , \nu \rangle | = \frac{J\phi _t}{|\nabla f|} . \]

Step 3: the formula on \(V\). Let \(A \subseteq V\) be Borel, and \(h = \mathbf{1}_A \circ \phi \), a Borel function on \(W\). By the change of variables formula (Theorem 28.11.2) for \(\phi \colon W \to V\) and Step 2,

\[ \int _A|\nabla f|\, d\lambda ^n = \int _Wh(y, t)\, |\nabla f(\phi (y, t))|\, |\det D\phi (y, t)|\, d\lambda ^n(y, t) = \int _Wh(y, t)\, J\phi _t(y)\, d\lambda ^n(y, t) . \]

The integrand, extended by \(0\) outside \(W\), is a nonnegative Borel function on \(\mathbb {R}^{n-1} \times \mathbb {R}\). By Tonelli’s theorem (Theorem 28.10.3 and Remark 28.10.5) the function \(t \mapsto \int _{W_t}h(y, t)J\phi _t(y)\, dy\) is measurable and the last integral equals \(\int _\mathbb {R}\bigl(\int _{W_t}h(y, t)J\phi _t(y)\, dy\bigr)dt\). By the area formula (Theorem 28.20.3) for \(\phi _t\) and the Borel set \(B_t = \{ y \in W_t : \phi (y, t) \in A\} \), whose image is \(A \cap f^{-1}(t)\) by Step 1,

\[ \int _{W_t}h(y, t)J\phi _t(y)\, dy = \int _{B_t}J\phi _t\, d\lambda ^{n-1} = \mathcal{H}^{n-1}\bigl(A \cap f^{-1}(t)\bigr). \]

This proves the theorem for Borel subsets of \(V\).

Step 4: globalisation. \(U\) is a countable union of compact sets, each covered by finitely many of the open sets \(V\) of Step 1; so \(U = \bigcup _jV_j\) for countably many such sets. Let \(E_j = V_j \setminus \bigcup _{i {\lt} j}V_i\), disjoint Borel sets covering \(U\). For a Borel set \(A \subseteq U\), apply Step 3 to \(A \cap E_j \subseteq V_j\) and sum over \(j\). On the left, \(\sum _j\int _{A \cap E_j}|\nabla f| = \int _A|\nabla f|\) by monotone convergence. On the right, for every \(t\) the sets \(A \cap E_j \cap f^{-1}(t)\) are disjoint Borel sets with union \(A \cap f^{-1}(t)\), and \(\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\) is a measure on Borel sets (Proposition 28.17.3), so \(\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(A \cap f^{-1}(t)) = \sum _j\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(A \cap E_j \cap f^{-1}(t))\), a sum of measurable functions of \(t\); integrate with monotone convergence (Theorem 28.4.3). Finally, 28.5 is the first formula for \(g = \mathbf{1}_A\); it follows for simple Borel functions by linearity and for all Borel \(g \geq 0\) by monotone convergence, applied on both sides.

For \(f(x) = x_n\) the coarea formula is Fubini’s theorem. Its most important special case is integration in polar coordinates.

035B Corollary 28.21.3 Polar coordinates

Let \(S^{n-1} = \{ x \in \mathbb {R}^n : |x| = 1\} \) and \(\omega _n = \lambda ^n(B(0, 1))\). Then \(\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(S^{n-1}) = n\omega _n\), and for every Borel function \(g \colon \mathbb {R}^n \to [0, \infty ]\),

\[ \int _{\mathbb {R}^n}g\, d\lambda ^n = \int _0^\infty \Bigl(\int _{|x| = r}g\, d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\Bigr)dr = \int _0^\infty r^{n-1}\Bigl(\int _{S^{n-1}}g(r\theta )\, d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\theta )\Bigr)dr . \]

In particular, if \(g(x) = h(|x|)\) is radial, \(\int _{\mathbb {R}^n}g\, d\lambda ^n = n\omega _n\int _0^\infty h(r)r^{n-1}\, dr\).

Proof â–¼

Apply Theorem 28.21.2 to \(f(x) = |x|\) on \(U = \mathbb {R}^n \setminus \{ 0\} \). Here \(\nabla f(x) = x/|x|\), so \(|\nabla f| = 1\), and the point \(0\) is a null set. This gives the first equality. For the second, \(\theta \mapsto r\theta \) is a similarity with ratio \(r\) from \(S^{n-1}\) onto the sphere of radius \(r\), so \(\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\{ |x| = r\} \cap G) = r^{n-1}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(S^{n-1} \cap r^{-1}G)\) for Borel sets \(G\) (Proposition 28.17.3(3)), which is the second equality for \(g = \mathbf{1}_G\); pass to simple functions and to limits by monotone convergence. For \(g = \mathbf{1}_{B(0, 1)}\) we get \(\omega _n = \mathcal{H}^{n-1}(S^{n-1})\int _0^1r^{n-1}\, dr = \mathcal{H}^{n-1}(S^{n-1})/n\).

035C Example 28.21.4

  1. Spheres. \(\mathcal{H}^1(S^1) = 2\omega _2 = 2\pi \) and \(\mathcal{H}^2(S^2) = 3\omega _3 = 3 \cdot \frac43\pi = 4\pi \), in agreement with Example 28.20.4. The formula \(\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(S^{n-1}) = n\omega _n\) says that the volume of the ball, \(\omega _nR^n\), has derivative \(n\omega _nR^{n-1}\) in \(R\) equal to the area of the sphere of radius \(R\): adding a thin shell of thickness \(dR\) adds volume (area)\(\cdot dR\).

  2. Integrability of powers. For \(a \in \mathbb {R}\), \(\int _{|x| {\lt} 1}|x|^{-a}\, dx = n\omega _n\int _0^1r^{n-1-a}\, dr\) is finite if and only if \(a {\lt} n\), and \(\int _{|x| {\gt} 1}|x|^{-a}\, dx = n\omega _n\int _1^\infty r^{n-1-a}\, dr\) is finite if and only if \(a {\gt} n\). So \(|x|^{-a}\) is never integrable on all of \(\mathbb {R}^n\), and the borderline exponent is the dimension, as for \(\sum _{k \in \mathbb {Z}^n \setminus 0}|k|^{-a}\).

  3. The volume of balls. By Fubini’s theorem and Example 28.11.3, \(\int _{\mathbb {R}^n}e^{-|x|^2}dx = (\int _\mathbb {R}e^{-t^2}dt)^n = \pi ^{n/2}\). By the corollary and the substitution \(u = r^2\) it also equals \(n\omega _n\int _0^\infty e^{-r^2}r^{n-1}\, dr = \frac{n\omega _n}2\int _0^\infty e^{-u}u^{n/2 - 1}\, du = \frac{n\omega _n}2\Gamma (\frac n2)\). Hence \(\omega _n = \pi ^{n/2}/\Gamma (\frac n2 + 1)\), which solves Exercise 28.23.7: \(\omega _1 = 2\), \(\omega _2 = \pi \), \(\omega _3 = \frac43\pi \), \(\omega _4 = \frac{\pi ^2}{2}\), and \(\omega _n \to 0\) very fast as \(n \to \infty \).

035D Remark 28.21.5

  1. Critical points. For a Lipschitz \(f\) the coarea formula holds without excluding critical points [EG92, Section 3.4]. On the critical set \(Z = \{ \nabla f = 0\} \) the left side vanishes, so the formula contains the statement that \(\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(Z \cap f^{-1}(t)) = 0\) for almost every \(t\). This is remarkable because the set of critical values \(f(Z)\) can have positive measure: Sard’s theorem, which says it is null, needs \(f\) of class \(C^n\), and Whitney constructed a \(C^1\) function on \(\mathbb {R}^2\) that is not constant on a connected arc of critical points [Whi35]; its critical values fill an interval, yet each level set meets \(Z\) in an \(\mathcal{H}^1\)-null set.

  2. Isoperimetry and Sobolev inequalities. For \(u \in C^1_c(\mathbb {R}^n)\) the coarea formula reads \(\int |\nabla u| = \int _\mathbb {R}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\{ u = t\} )\, dt\) (in the general version), and \(\{ u = t\} \) is, for almost every \(t {\gt} 0\), the boundary of \(\{ u {\gt} t\} \). The isoperimetric inequality \(\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial E) \geq n\omega _n^{1/n}\lambda ^n(E)^{(n-1)/n}\), with equality for balls, then gives the Sobolev inequality \(\| u\| _{n/(n-1)} \leq (n\omega _n^{1/n})^{-1}\| \nabla u\| _1\) with its best constant; conversely, the Sobolev inequality applied to smoothed indicator functions gives back the isoperimetric inequality. This equivalence between a geometric and an analytic inequality is due to Federer and Fleming and to Maz’ya [FF60].
