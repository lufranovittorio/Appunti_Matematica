39.3 Volume and integration02JD

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

A metric measures the lengths of tangent vectors, hence the volumes of small parallelepipeds, and so it determines how to integrate functions over the manifold. For a surface in \(\mathbb {R}^3\) this is the classical surface integral \(\iint f\, dA\); the point of this section is that it is an instance of the integration of forms of Chapter 35, and that the same formulas work for submanifolds of any dimension. In a chart \((U, \varphi )\) with coordinates \(x^1, \ldots , x^n\) we write \(g_{ij} = g(\partial _i, \partial _j)\) and \(\det g\) for the determinant of the matrix \((g_{ij})\), a smooth positive function on \(U\); \(\lambda \) is Lebesgue measure.

02JE Lemma 39.3.1

Let \(y^1, \ldots , y^n\) be the coordinates of a second chart, with metric coefficients \(\tilde g_{ab} = g(\partial /\partial y^a, \partial /\partial y^b)\). Then on the intersection of the two chart domains

\[ \tilde g_{ab} = \sum _{i,j}\frac{\partial x^i}{\partial y^a}\frac{\partial x^j}{\partial y^b}\, g_{ij}, \qquad \sqrt{\det \tilde g} = \Bigl|\det \Bigl(\frac{\partial x^i}{\partial y^a}\Bigr)\Bigr|\sqrt{\det g}. \]
Proof â–¼

By Proposition 32.4.7, \(\partial /\partial y^a = \sum _i(\partial x^i/\partial y^a)\, \partial /\partial x^i\); expanding \(g\) bilinearly gives the first formula. In matrices it reads \(\tilde g = P^TgP\) with \(P = (\partial x^i/\partial y^a)\), and taking determinants, \(\det \tilde g = (\det P)^2\det g\).

02JF Proposition 39.3.2 Riemannian measure

Let \((M, g)\) be a Riemannian manifold. There is a unique measure \(\mu _g\) on the Borel sets of \(M\) such that for every chart \((U, \varphi )\) and every Borel set \(A \subseteq U\),

\[ \mu _g(A) = \int _{\varphi (A)}\sqrt{\det g} \circ \varphi ^{-1}\, d\lambda . \]

Consequently \(\int _Af\, d\mu _g = \int _{\varphi (A)}(f\sqrt{\det g}) \circ \varphi ^{-1}\, d\lambda \) for every measurable \(f \geq 0\) and every integrable \(f\) on \(A\). We write \(dV\) for \(\mu _g\), and \(dA\) and \(ds\) in dimensions \(2\) and \(1\) (the volume, the area and the arc length), and \(\mathrm{vol}(M) = \mu _g(M)\).

Proof â–¼

For a chart \((U, \varphi )\) the formula defines a measure \(\mu _U\) on the Borel subsets of \(U\), the image under the homeomorphism \(\varphi ^{-1}\) of the measure with density \(\sqrt{\det g} \circ \varphi ^{-1}\) on \(\varphi (U)\). Two such measures agree where both are defined: let \(A\) be a Borel subset of \(U \cap V\) for a second chart \((V, \psi )\) with coordinates \(y\), and let \(\tau = \varphi \circ \psi ^{-1}\), whose Jacobian matrix is \((\partial x^i/\partial y^a)\). By Lemma 39.3.1, \(\sqrt{\det \tilde g} \circ \psi ^{-1} = (\sqrt{\det g} \circ \varphi ^{-1} \circ \tau )\, |\det D\tau |\), so by the change of variables formula (Theorem 27.10.2) applied to \(\tau \colon \psi (A) \to \varphi (A)\),

\[ \int _{\psi (A)}\sqrt{\det \tilde g} \circ \psi ^{-1}\, d\lambda = \int _{\psi (A)}(\sqrt{\det g} \circ \varphi ^{-1} \circ \tau )\, |\det D\tau |\, d\lambda = \int _{\varphi (A)}\sqrt{\det g} \circ \varphi ^{-1}\, d\lambda . \]

Cover \(M\) by countably many chart domains \(U_1, U_2, \ldots \) (\(M\) is second countable), let \(B_1 = U_1\) and \(B_k = U_k \setminus (U_1 \cup \cdots \cup U_{k-1})\), and put \(\mu _g(A) = \sum _k\mu _{U_k}(A \cap B_k)\). This is a measure, being a countable sum of measures. If \(A\) is a Borel subset of a chart domain \(U\), the compatibility just proved gives \(\mu _{U_k}(A \cap B_k) = \mu _U(A \cap B_k)\), so \(\mu _g(A) = \sum _k\mu _U(A \cap B_k) = \mu _U(A)\). Conversely, any measure with the stated property assigns to \(A\) the value \(\sum _k\mu _{U_k}(A \cap B_k)\), which proves uniqueness. The formula for integrals follows from the one for sets, first for simple functions, then by monotone convergence (Theorem 27.4.2) for \(f \geq 0\), and by linearity for integrable \(f\).

02JG Proposition 39.3.3 Riemannian volume form

Let \((M, g)\) be an oriented Riemannian \(n\)-manifold. There is a unique \(n\)-form \(\mathrm{vol}_g\) with \(\mathrm{vol}_g(e_1, \ldots , e_n) = 1\) for every positively oriented orthonormal basis \((e_1, \ldots , e_n)\) of a tangent space. In a positively oriented chart

\[ \mathrm{vol}_g = \sqrt{\det g}\; dx^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge dx^n, \]

and \(\int _Mf\, \mathrm{vol}_g = \int _Mf\, d\mu _g\) for every continuous function \(f\) with compact support.

Proof â–¼

Let \(p\) lie in a positively oriented chart, and let \((e_1, \ldots , e_n)\) be a positively oriented orthonormal basis of \(T_pM\). Write \(e_a = \sum _iP^i_a\partial _i\) with an invertible matrix \(P = (P^i_a)\). Then \(\delta _{ab} = \langle e_a, e_b\rangle = (P^TgP)_{ab}\), so \((\det P)^2\det g = 1\), and \(\det P {\gt} 0\) because both bases are positively oriented; hence \(\det P = 1/\sqrt{\det g}\) and

\[ \bigl(\sqrt{\det g}\; dx^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge dx^n\bigr)(e_1, \ldots , e_n) = \sqrt{\det g}\, \det \bigl(dx^i(e_a)\bigr) = \sqrt{\det g}\, \det P = 1 . \]

Since \(\Lambda ^nT^*_pM\) is one-dimensional, an \(n\)-form at \(p\) is determined by its value on one basis. So on each positively oriented chart domain the smooth form \(\sqrt{\det g}\, dx^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge dx^n\) is the only form with the required property; the forms of two charts therefore agree on the overlap, and together they define the smooth \(n\)-form \(\mathrm{vol}_g\), which is unique. If \(f\) is supported in a positively oriented chart, both integrals equal \(\int (f\sqrt{\det g}) \circ \varphi ^{-1}\, d\lambda \), by Definition 35.4.1 and Proposition 39.3.2; in general write \(f = \sum _i\psi _if\) with a partition of unity subordinate to positively oriented charts and use linearity of both sides.

For a surface \(\mathrm{vol}_g\) is the area form \(dA\). By Proposition 35.3.2, a non-orientable manifold, such as the Möbius band or \(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^2\), has no nowhere vanishing \(n\)-form, hence no volume form; but it still has a Riemannian measure, so areas and integrals of functions make sense on it. The integral of a function needs a metric, the integral of an \(n\)-form needs an orientation, and the volume form converts one into the other.

02JH Proposition 39.3.4 Volume of parametrised submanifolds

Let \(M \subseteq \mathbb {R}^N\) be a \(k\)-dimensional submanifold with the induced metric, and \(x \colon \Omega \to M\) a parametrisation (Proposition 32.6.15(3)), with \(x_i = \partial x/\partial u^i\). Then for every measurable \(f \geq 0\) on \(x(\Omega )\),

\[ \int _{x(\Omega )}f\, d\mu = \int _\Omega f(x(u))\, \mathcal{J}(u)\, du, \qquad \mathcal{J} = \sqrt{\det \bigl(\langle x_i, x_j\rangle \bigr)} = \Bigl(\sum _I\Bigl(\det \frac{\partial x^I}{\partial u}\Bigr)^2\Bigr)^{1/2}, \]

where \(I\) runs over the subsets \(\{ i_1 {\lt} \cdots {\lt} i_k\} \) of \(\{ 1, \ldots , N\} \), and \(\partial x^I/\partial u\) is the \(k \times k\) matrix formed by the rows \(i_1, \ldots , i_k\) of the Jacobian matrix \(Dx\). In particular:

  1. for a curve (\(k = 1\)), \(\mathcal{J} = |x^{\prime }(t)|\), and \(\int f\, ds = \int f(x(t))\, |x^{\prime }(t)|\, dt\);

  2. for a surface in \(\mathbb {R}^3\), with \(E = |x_u|^2\), \(F = \langle x_u, x_v\rangle \) and \(G = |x_v|^2\), \(\mathcal{J} = \sqrt{EG - F^2} = |x_u \times x_v|\);

  3. for a hypersurface (\(k = N - 1\)) with unit normal \(\nu \), \(\mathcal{J} = |\det (\nu , x_1, \ldots , x_k)|\);

  4. for the graph of a smooth function \(\phi \colon \Omega \to \mathbb {R}\), parametrised by \(x(u) = (u, \phi (u))\), \(\mathcal{J} = \sqrt{1 + |\nabla \phi |^2}\).

Proof â–¼

The inverse \(x^{-1}\) is a chart of \(M\) (Lemma 32.6.3), whose coordinate vector field \(\partial /\partial u^i\) is \(x_i\); so \(g_{ij} = \langle x_i, x_j\rangle \) and the formula is Proposition 39.3.2. For the second expression of \(\mathcal{J}\), Proposition 11.5.2 gives \(\det (\langle x_i, x_j\rangle ) = |x_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge x_k|^2\) in \(\Lambda ^k\mathbb {R}^N\). Writing \(x_j = \sum _i(\partial x^i/\partial u^j)e_i\) and expanding by multilinearity, the terms with a repeated \(e_i\) vanish, and collecting the terms with the same set of indices \(I\) (the Leibniz formula for the determinant) gives \(x_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge x_k = \sum _I\det (\partial x^I/\partial u)\, e_I\); as the \(e_I\) are orthonormal, \(|x_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge x_k|^2 = \sum _I\det (\partial x^I/\partial u)^2\).

(1) is the case of a single vector. (2) \(EG - F^2 = |x_u|^2|x_v|^2 - \langle x_u, x_v\rangle ^2 = |x_u \times x_v|^2\) (Lagrange’s identity); equivalently, the three \(2 \times 2\) minors of \(Dx\) are, up to sign, the components of \(x_u \times x_v\). (3) The matrix \(P\) with columns \(\nu , x_1, \ldots , x_k\) satisfies \(P^TP = \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & (\langle x_i, x_j\rangle ) \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\), since \(|\nu | = 1\) and \(\langle \nu , x_i\rangle = 0\); so \((\det P)^2 = \det (\langle x_i, x_j\rangle )\). (4) Here \(x_i = (e_i, \partial _i\phi )\), so \(\langle x_i, x_j\rangle = \delta _{ij} + \partial _i\phi \, \partial _j\phi \): this is the matrix \(I + ww^T\) with \(w = \nabla \phi \), which is the identity on \(w^\perp \) and multiplies \(w\) by \(1 + |w|^2\), so its determinant is \(1 + |w|^2\).

02JI Remark 39.3.5

In classical surface theory [dC76, Chapter 2] the area of a region \(R\) contained in the image of a parametrisation \(x\), and the integral of a function over it, are defined by

\[ \iint _Rf\, dA = \iint _{x^{-1}(R)}f(x(u, v))\, |x_u \times x_v|\, du\, dv, \]

and one checks by hand that this does not depend on the parametrisation. By Proposition 39.3.4(2) this is the integral with respect to the Riemannian measure of the induced metric: surface integration is integration on a manifold, and nothing new. The independence of the parametrisation is Lemma 39.3.1 together with the change of variables formula, and a region that is not contained in one parametrisation is cut into pieces, or treated with a partition of unity, as in Definition 35.4.1. When the surface is oriented, \(\iint _Rf\, dA = \int _Rf\, \mathrm{vol}_S\) is the integral of the \(2\)-form \(f\, \mathrm{vol}_S\) (Proposition 39.3.3). The same holds in every dimension and codimension: the length of a curve (Definition 39.2.1) is its one-dimensional volume, by (1), and the \(k\)-dimensional volume of a piece of a \(k\)-dimensional submanifold of \(\mathbb {R}^N\) is the integral of the square root of the sum of the squares of the \(k \times k\) minors.

For hypersurfaces of \(\mathbb {R}^{n+1}\) the volume form has a simple expression through the unit normal, and it turns integrals of \(n\)-forms into flux integrals. We write \(\Omega = dx^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge dx^{n+1}\) for the standard volume form of \(\mathbb {R}^{n+1}\), and, for a vector field \(F\) defined along \(M\) (for instance the unit normal \(\nu \)), \((\iota _F\Omega )|_M\) for the \(n\)-form on \(M\) whose value at \(p\) is the restriction of \(\iota _{F(p)}\Omega _p\) to \(T_pM\).

02JJ Proposition 39.3.6 Volume form of a hypersurface; flux

Let \(M \subseteq \mathbb {R}^{n+1}\) be a hypersurface oriented by a unit normal field \(\nu \) (Proposition 35.3.4). Then

\[ \mathrm{vol}_M = (\iota _\nu \Omega )|_M = \Bigl(\sum _{i=1}^{n+1}(-1)^{i-1}\nu ^i\, dx^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \widehat{dx^i} \wedge \cdots \wedge dx^{n+1}\Bigr)\Big|_M, \]

and for every vector field \(F\) along \(M\), \((\iota _F\Omega )|_M = \langle F, \nu \rangle \, \mathrm{vol}_M\). In \(\mathbb {R}^3\), for a surface \(S\): \(dA = (\nu ^1\, dy \wedge dz + \nu ^2\, dz \wedge dx + \nu ^3\, dx \wedge dy)|_S\), and the flux of \(F\) through \(S\) is

\[ \int _SF^1\, dy \wedge dz + F^2\, dz \wedge dx + F^3\, dx \wedge dy = \iint _S\langle F, \nu \rangle \, dA . \]
Proof â–¼

By Proposition 35.3.4 the orientation of \(M\) is that of the form \((v_1, \ldots , v_n) \mapsto \det (\nu , v_1, \ldots , v_n) = \Omega (\nu , v_1, \ldots , v_n) = (\iota _\nu \Omega )(v_1, \ldots , v_n)\). If \((e_1, \ldots , e_n)\) is a positively oriented orthonormal basis of \(T_pM\), then \((\nu (p), e_1, \ldots , e_n)\) is an orthonormal basis of \(\mathbb {R}^{n+1}\), positive by the same proposition, so its determinant is \(1\). By the uniqueness in Proposition 39.3.3, \((\iota _\nu \Omega )|_M = \mathrm{vol}_M\). The coordinate expression is \(\iota _\nu (dx^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge dx^{n+1}) = \sum _i(-1)^{i-1}dx^i(\nu )\, dx^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \widehat{dx^i} \wedge \cdots \wedge dx^{n+1}\), which follows from Proposition 11.4.5 by induction on the number of factors. For \(F\), write \(F = \langle F, \nu \rangle \nu + F^\top \) with \(F^\top \) tangent to \(M\). For \(v_1, \ldots , v_n \in T_pM\) the \(n + 1\) vectors \(F^\top , v_1, \ldots , v_n\) lie in the \(n\)-dimensional space \(T_pM\), hence are linearly dependent, and \(\Omega (F^\top , v_1, \ldots , v_n) = 0\); so \((\iota _F\Omega )|_M = \langle F, \nu \rangle (\iota _\nu \Omega )|_M\). In \(\mathbb {R}^3\), \(\iota _\nu (dx \wedge dy \wedge dz) = \nu ^1\, dy \wedge dz - \nu ^2\, dx \wedge dz + \nu ^3\, dx \wedge dy\) and \(-dx \wedge dz = dz \wedge dx\); the flux formula is the integral of \((\iota _F\Omega )|_S = \langle F, \nu \rangle \, dA\), by Proposition 39.3.3.

02P5 Remark 39.3.7

It is worth spelling out what the flux formula means, because the classical notation hides it. The integrand \(F^1\, dy \wedge dz + F^2\, dz \wedge dx + F^3\, dx \wedge dy\) is a \(2\)-form on \(\mathbb {R}^3\) (or on a neighbourhood of \(S\)), and “\(|_S\)” means its pullback \(\iota ^*\) by the inclusion \(\iota \colon S \to \mathbb {R}^3\) (Definition 35.2.1): the \(2\)-form on \(S\) whose value on \(v, w \in T_pS\) is the value of the original form on the same vectors, now regarded as vectors of \(\mathbb {R}^3\). It is this pullback that one integrates over \(S\).

In a positively oriented parametrisation \(x(u, v) = (x(u, v), y(u, v), z(u, v))\) of \(S\), pulling back is substitution: \(x^*dy = d(y \circ x) = y_u\, du + y_v\, dv\), and so on, so that

\[ x^*(dy \wedge dz) = (y_uz_v - y_vz_u)\, du \wedge dv = \frac{\partial (y, z)}{\partial (u, v)}\, du \wedge dv, \]

and similarly \(x^*(dz \wedge dx) = \frac{\partial (z, x)}{\partial (u, v)}\, du \wedge dv\) and \(x^*(dx \wedge dy) = \frac{\partial (x, y)}{\partial (u, v)}\, du \wedge dv\). These three \(2 \times 2\) minors of the Jacobian matrix are exactly the components of the cross product \(x_u \times x_v\). Hence

\[ x^*\bigl(F^1\, dy \wedge dz + F^2\, dz \wedge dx + F^3\, dx \wedge dy\bigr) = \langle F \circ x, x_u \times x_v\rangle \, du \wedge dv = \langle F \circ x, \nu \rangle \, |x_u \times x_v|\, du \wedge dv, \]

because \(x_u \times x_v = |x_u \times x_v|\, \nu \) for a parametrisation that is positively oriented with respect to \(\nu \) (by Proposition 35.3.4, \((x_u, x_v)\) is positive exactly when \(\det (\nu , x_u, x_v) = \langle \nu , x_u \times x_v\rangle {\gt} 0\)). Since \(|x_u \times x_v|\, du\, dv\) is the area element (Proposition 39.3.4), integrating gives the flux formula again: this is a second proof of the proposition in \(\mathbb {R}^3\), by direct computation in a chart.

This also explains the classical notation. One writes \(d\vec\Sigma = \nu \, dA\) for the “vector surface element” and \(\iint _SF \cdot d\vec\Sigma = \iint _SF_1\, dy\, dz + F_2\, dz\, dx + F_3\, dx\, dy\), as in the usual statement \(\iint _S\operatorname {curl}F \cdot d\vec\Sigma = \oint _{\partial S}F \cdot d\vec r\) of Stokes’ theorem. The symbols \(dy\, dz\) there are not mere notation: they are the pullbacks to \(S\) of the \(2\)-forms \(dy \wedge dz\), and so on, and the identity \(d\vec\Sigma = \nu \, dA\) is the statement of the proposition, \((dy \wedge dz, dz \wedge dx, dx \wedge dy)|_S = \nu \, \mathrm{vol}_S\). Geometrically, \((dy \wedge dz)|_S(v, w)\) is the signed area of the projection to the \((y, z)\)-plane of the parallelogram spanned by \(v\) and \(w\), while \(\mathrm{vol}_S(v, w)\) is the area of the parallelogram itself; the components of \(\nu \) are the ratios between the two, the cosines of the angles between the tangent plane and the coordinate planes.

02P6 Example 39.3.8

Let \(S\) be the graph of a smooth function \(h\) over a region \(R \subseteq \mathbb {R}^2\), with the parametrisation \(x(u, v) = (u, v, h(u, v))\) and the upward normal. The minors are \(\frac{\partial (y, z)}{\partial (u, v)} = \det \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ h_u & h_v \end{smallmatrix}\bigr) = -h_u\), \(\frac{\partial (z, x)}{\partial (u, v)} = -h_v\) and \(\frac{\partial (x, y)}{\partial (u, v)} = 1\), so \(x_u \times x_v = (-h_u, -h_v, 1)\), \(\nu = (-h_u, -h_v, 1)/(1 + h_u^2 + h_v^2)^{1/2}\), and the flux of \(F\) through \(S\) is

\[ \iint _S\langle F, \nu \rangle \, dA = \iint _R\bigl(-F^1h_u - F^2h_v + F^3\bigr)(u, v, h(u, v))\, du\, dv . \]

For instance the flux of \(F = (0, 0, 1)\) through any graph over \(R\) is the area of \(R\): the vertical field only sees the projection of the surface to the horizontal plane, \((dx \wedge dy)|_S = \nu ^3\, dA\). For the upper unit hemisphere, \(h = (1 - u^2 - v^2)^{1/2}\) on the open unit disc, and the flux of \(F(x) = x\) is \(\iint (u^2/h + v^2/h + h)\, du\, dv = \iint du\, dv/h = \int _0^{2\pi }\! \! \int _0^1\frac{r\, dr\, d\varphi }{(1 - r^2)^{1/2}} = 2\pi \), which is also \(\iint _S\langle x, \nu \rangle \, dA = \iint _S1\, dA\), the area of the hemisphere, as \(\nu (x) = x\) on the sphere.

02JK Corollary 39.3.9 Divergence theorem

Let \(D \subseteq \mathbb {R}^{n+1}\) be a compact subset that is a smooth \((n+1)\)-manifold with boundary (Definition 35.4.5), with the charts given by charts of \(\mathbb {R}^{n+1}\), for instance a closed ball. Let \(\nu \) be the outward unit normal along \(\partial D\), and \(F\) a smooth vector field on a neighbourhood of \(D\), with divergence \(\operatorname {div}F = \sum _i\partial _iF^i\). Then

\[ \int _D\operatorname {div}F\, d\lambda = \int _{\partial D}\langle F, \nu \rangle \, dA . \]
Proof â–¼

By Proposition 11.4.5, \(\iota _F\Omega = \sum _i(-1)^{i-1}F^i\, dx^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \widehat{dx^i} \wedge \cdots \wedge dx^{n+1}\), and \(d(\iota _F\Omega ) = \sum _i\partial _iF^i\, \Omega = (\operatorname {div}F)\, \Omega \), as in the proof of Theorem 35.4.8. Stokes’ theorem gives \(\int _D(\operatorname {div}F)\, \Omega = \int _{\partial D}\iota _F\Omega \), where \(D\) carries the standard orientation of \(\mathbb {R}^{n+1}\), so that the left side is \(\int _D\operatorname {div}F\, d\lambda \), and \(\partial D\) the induced orientation. We check that the induced orientation is the one defined by \(\nu \); then the right side is \(\int _{\partial D}\langle F, \nu \rangle \, dA\) by Proposition 39.3.6. In a positively oriented chart in which \(D\) is \(\{ x^{n+1} \geq 0\} \), the vector \(w = -\partial _{n+1}\) points outward, and \(\det (w, \partial _1, \ldots , \partial _n) = (-1)^{n+1}\) with respect to the chart; by Definition 35.4.5, \((x^1, \ldots , x^n)\) is positively oriented on \(\partial D\) exactly when \((-1)^{n+1} = 1\). So a basis \((v_1, \ldots , v_n)\) of \(T_p\partial D\) is positive for the induced orientation if and only if \((w, v_1, \ldots , v_n)\) is a positive basis of \(\mathbb {R}^{n+1}\). The same holds with \(\nu \) in place of \(w\), because \(w = c\nu + t\) with \(c {\gt} 0\) and \(t\) tangent to \(\partial D\), so that \(\det (w, v_1, \ldots , v_n) = c\det (\nu , v_1, \ldots , v_n)\). By Proposition 35.3.4 this is the orientation defined by \(\nu \).

The divergence theorem is the many-variable version of the fundamental theorem of calculus, and it gives integration by parts on a region with a boundary term. Write \(\Delta _0 = \sum _i\partial _i^2\) for the Euclidean Laplacian with the sign of analysis (so \(\Delta _0 = -\Delta \) for the Hodge Laplacian of Definition 39.13.2), \(\nabla f\) for the gradient, and \(\partial _\nu f = \langle \nabla f, \nu \rangle \) for the outward normal derivative.

02P7 Corollary 39.3.10 Integration by parts and Green’s identities

Let \(D\) and \(\nu \) be as in Corollary 39.3.9, and \(f\), \(g\) smooth functions on a neighbourhood of \(D\). Then

  1. \(\displaystyle \int _D(\partial _if)\, g\, d\lambda = \int _{\partial D}fg\, \nu ^i\, dA - \int _Df\, \partial _ig\, d\lambda \);

  2. (first Green identity) \(\displaystyle \int _D\bigl(g\, \Delta _0f + \langle \nabla g, \nabla f\rangle \bigr)\, d\lambda = \int _{\partial D}g\, \partial _\nu f\, dA\);

  3. (second Green identity) \(\displaystyle \int _D\bigl(g\, \Delta _0f - f\, \Delta _0g\bigr)\, d\lambda = \int _{\partial D}\bigl(g\, \partial _\nu f - f\, \partial _\nu g\bigr)\, dA\).

Proof â–¼

(1) Apply Corollary 39.3.9 to \(F = fg\, e_i\): \(\operatorname {div}F = \partial _i(fg) = (\partial _if)g + f\, \partial _ig\) and \(\langle F, \nu \rangle = fg\, \nu ^i\). (2) Apply it to \(F = g\nabla f\): \(\operatorname {div}F = \sum _i\partial _i(g\, \partial _if) = \langle \nabla g, \nabla f\rangle + g\, \Delta _0f\) and \(\langle F, \nu \rangle = g\, \partial _\nu f\). (3) Subtract from (2) the same identity with \(f\) and \(g\) exchanged; the symmetric terms \(\langle \nabla g, \nabla f\rangle \) cancel.

For \(n + 1 = 1\) and \(D = [a, b]\), \(\nu = -1\) at \(a\) and \(+1\) at \(b\), and (1) is the integration by parts of one variable (Corollary 26.8.4); when \(f\) or \(g\) vanishes near \(\partial D\), it is Proposition 27.10.4. With \(g = 1\), (2) says that the total flux of \(\nabla f\) through the boundary is \(\int _D\Delta _0f\); with \(g = f\) it gives \(\int _D|\nabla f|^2 = \int _{\partial D}f\, \partial _\nu f - \int _Df\, \Delta _0f\), the starting point of the energy methods for the Laplace equation (Exercise 39.15.19).

Stokes’ theorem for \(1\)-forms on surfaces gives the two other classical theorems of vector calculus, which Example 35.4.11 mentioned without formulas.

02P8 Corollary 39.3.11 Green’s theorem

Let \(D \subseteq \mathbb {R}^2\) be a compact region with smooth boundary, as in Corollary 39.3.9, with \(\partial D\) oriented as the boundary of \(D\) (so that \(D\) lies to the left of the direction of motion), and \(P\), \(Q\) smooth functions near \(D\). Then

\[ \oint _{\partial D}P\, dx + Q\, dy = \iint _D\Bigl(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\Bigr)dx\, dy . \]

In particular the area of \(D\) is \(\frac12\oint _{\partial D}x\, dy - y\, dx\).

Proof â–¼

By Theorem 35.2.6, \(d(P\, dx + Q\, dy) = P_y\, dy \wedge dx + Q_x\, dx \wedge dy = (Q_x - P_y)\, dx \wedge dy\), and Stokes’ theorem (Theorem 35.4.8) gives the formula, the integral of the \(2\)-form \((Q_x - P_y)\, dx \wedge dy\) over \(D\) with its standard orientation being the Lebesgue integral. In the boundary orientation, an outward vector \(w\) followed by a positive tangent vector \(T\) is a positive basis of \(\mathbb {R}^2\) (Definition 35.4.5), so \(T\) is \(w\) rotated by \(+\pi /2\) and \(D\) is on the left. For the area take \(P = -y/2\), \(Q = x/2\), with \(Q_x - P_y = 1\).

For the ellipse \(x = a\cos t\), \(y = b\sin t\), \(0 \leq t \leq 2\pi \), the area formula gives \(\frac12\int _0^{2\pi }(ab\cos ^2t + ab\sin ^2t)\, dt = \pi ab\). Green’s theorem is also the divergence theorem in the plane: if \(T = (T^1, T^2)\) is the positive unit tangent of \(\partial D\), the outward normal is \(\nu = (T^2, -T^1)\), so for \(F = (Q, -P)\) one has \(\langle F, \nu \rangle = PT^1 + QT^2\) and \(\operatorname {div}F = Q_x - P_y\): the flux of \(F\) out of \(D\) is the circulation of \((P, Q)\) along \(\partial D\).

02P9 Corollary 39.3.12 Stokes’ theorem of vector calculus

Let \(\Sigma \subseteq \mathbb {R}^3\) be a surface oriented by a unit normal field \(\nu \), and \(S \subseteq \Sigma \) a compact region with smooth boundary (a \(2\)-manifold with boundary in the charts of \(\Sigma \)). Orient \(\partial S\) as the boundary of \(S\): a tangent vector \(T\) of \(\partial S\) is positive when \(\nu \times T\) points into \(S\), that is, walking along \(\partial S\) with one’s head in the direction of \(\nu \), the region \(S\) is on the left. Then for a smooth vector field \(F\) on a neighbourhood of \(S\),

\[ \iint _S\langle \operatorname {curl}F, \nu \rangle \, dA = \oint _{\partial S}F^1\, dx + F^2\, dy + F^3\, dz = \int _{\partial S}\langle F, T\rangle \, ds, \]

where \(T\) is the positive unit tangent and \(ds\) the arc length.

Proof â–¼

Let \(\alpha = F^1\, dx + F^2\, dy + F^3\, dz\) and \(\iota \colon S \to \mathbb {R}^3\) the inclusion. By Example 35.2.11, \(d\alpha = G^1\, dy \wedge dz + G^2\, dz \wedge dx + G^3\, dx \wedge dy\) with \(G = \operatorname {curl}F\), and by Proposition 39.3.6, applied to the surface \(\Sigma \), \(\iota ^*d\alpha = \langle G, \nu \rangle \, \mathrm{vol}_S\). Since pullback commutes with \(d\) (Theorem 35.2.6), Stokes’ theorem on the compact manifold with boundary \(S\) gives \(\iint _S\langle G, \nu \rangle \, dA = \int _Sd(\iota ^*\alpha ) = \int _{\partial S}\iota ^*\alpha \), and the last integral is the line integral of \(\alpha \) along \(\partial S\) in its boundary orientation (Definition 35.2.13), which is \(\int \langle F, T\rangle \, ds\) in a parametrisation by arc length. It remains to describe the boundary orientation. By Definition 35.4.5, \(T\) is positive when \((w, T)\) is a positive basis of \(T_pS\) for an outward vector \(w\), and by Proposition 35.3.4 this means \(\det (\nu , w, T) {\gt} 0\). For \(w\) and \(T\) unit and orthogonal (which we may assume), \((\nu , w, T)\) is then a positive orthonormal basis of \(\mathbb {R}^3\), so \(\nu \times T = -w\), which points into \(S\).

02PA Example 39.3.13

Let \(S\) be the closed upper unit hemisphere, with the outward normal \(\nu (x) = x\), and \(F = (-y, x, 0)\), the rotation field, with \(\operatorname {curl}F = (0, 0, 2)\). The boundary is the equator, and its boundary orientation is counterclockwise seen from above: at \((1, 0, 0)\) the inward direction in \(S\) is \((0, 0, 1)\), and \(\nu \times T = (1, 0, 0) \times (0, 1, 0) = (0, 0, 1)\) for \(T = (0, 1, 0)\). With \(\gamma (t) = (\cos t, \sin t, 0)\), the right side is \(\int _0^{2\pi }(\sin ^2t + \cos ^2t)\, dt = 2\pi \). The left side is \(\iint _S2z\, dA\), and by Example 39.3.8 (with \(F = (0, 0, 2)\)) it is twice the area of the unit disc, \(2\pi \). The same value is obtained for every surface with boundary the equator and the corresponding orientation, for instance the flat unit disc with \(\nu = (0, 0, 1)\), where Stokes’ theorem is Green’s theorem: the flux of a curl through a surface only depends on its boundary, because \(\operatorname {curl}F\, \cdot \, d\vec\Sigma \) is an exact \(2\)-form.

02JL Example 39.3.14

  1. The sphere. The map \(x(\theta , \varphi ) = r(\sin \theta \cos \varphi , \sin \theta \sin \varphi , \cos \theta )\) on \((0, \pi ) \times (0, 2\pi )\) parametrises the sphere of radius \(r\) minus a closed half great circle. That half circle has area \(0\): without its two endpoints it is the segment \(\varphi = 0\) for the same formula on \((0, \pi ) \times (-\pi , \pi )\), a Lebesgue null set, and points have measure \(0\). Here \(E = r^2\), \(F = 0\), \(G = r^2\sin ^2\theta \), so \(dA = r^2\sin \theta \, d\theta \, d\varphi \) and the area is \(\int _0^{2\pi }\int _0^\pi r^2\sin \theta \, d\theta \, d\varphi = 4\pi r^2\).

  2. The torus of revolution. For \(0 {\lt} b {\lt} a\), \(x(u, v) = ((a + b\cos u)\cos v, (a + b\cos u)\sin v, b\sin u)\) has \(E = b^2\), \(F = 0\) and \(G = (a + b\cos u)^2\), so the area is \(\int _0^{2\pi }\int _0^{2\pi }b(a + b\cos u)\, du\, dv = 4\pi ^2ab\): the length \(2\pi b\) of the rotated circle times the length \(2\pi a\) of the path of its centre.

  3. Graphs. The upper hemisphere is the graph of \(\phi = \sqrt{1 - u^2 - v^2}\) over the open unit disc, with \(1 + |\nabla \phi |^2 = 1/(1 - u^2 - v^2)\). In polar coordinates its area is \(\int _0^{2\pi }\int _0^1\rho (1 - \rho ^2)^{-1/2}\, d\rho \, d\theta = 2\pi \), half of \(4\pi \).

  4. Spheres and balls. For \(F(x) = x\) on \(\mathbb {R}^{n+1}\), \(\operatorname {div}F = n + 1\), and on the unit sphere \(\nu (x) = x\) and \(\langle F, \nu \rangle = 1\). By Corollary 39.3.9, \(\mathrm{vol}(S^n) = (n + 1)\, \lambda (B^{n+1})\): for instance \(2\pi = 2 \cdot \pi \) and \(4\pi = 3 \cdot \frac{4\pi }{3}\).
