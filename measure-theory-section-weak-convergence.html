28.15 Weak convergence of measures030C

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

Sequences of measures appear whenever something discrete approximates something continuous, or something smooth approximates something singular. Riemann sums are integrals against finitely many point masses that approximate Lebesgue measure; in probability the distributions of normalised sums of random variables approximate the Gaussian distribution; smooth bumps of total mass \(1\) concentrating at a point approximate the Dirac measure; and in geometric measure theory one studies limits of the area measures of a sequence of surfaces, for instance of a minimising sequence for the area. Convergence \(\mu _k(A) \to \mu (A)\) for all Borel sets \(A\) is far too strong for these purposes (Example 28.15.2(1)). The useful notion tests the measures against continuous functions. As in Section 28.14, \(X\) is a locally compact Hausdorff space with a countable basis.

030D Definition 28.15.1

A sequence of Radon measures \(\mu _k\) on \(X\) converges weakly to a Radon measure \(\mu \), written \(\mu _k \rightharpoonup \mu \), if

\[ \int _Xf\, d\mu _k \to \int _Xf\, d\mu \qquad \text{for every } f \in C_c(X). \]

The limit is unique, by the uniqueness in Theorem 28.14.6. In probability theory this is called vague convergence, and weak convergence of probability measures means testing against all bounded continuous functions; the two agree when no mass is lost (Exercise 28.23.14). In functional analysis it is the weak-\(*\) convergence of functionals on \(C_c(X)\).

030E Example 28.15.2

  1. Moving point masses. If \(x_k \to x\) in \(X\), then \(\delta _{x_k} \rightharpoonup \delta _x\), since \(f(x_k) \to f(x)\) for continuous \(f\). On \(\mathbb {R}\), \(\delta _{1/k} \rightharpoonup \delta _0\), but \(\delta _{1/k}(\{ 0\} ) = 0\) does not converge to \(\delta _0(\{ 0\} ) = 1\), and \(\delta _{1/k}((0, 1)) = 1\) does not converge to \(\delta _0((0, 1)) = 0\). So weak convergence does not imply convergence on compact sets, nor on open sets; Proposition 28.15.3 gives the inequalities that do hold.

  2. Riemann sums. On \(\mathbb {R}\) let \(\mu _k = \frac1k\sum _{j=1}^k\delta _{j/k}\). Then \(\mu _k \rightharpoonup \mathbf{1}_{[0, 1]}\lambda \). Indeed \(f \in C_c(\mathbb {R})\) is uniformly continuous, being continuous with compact support; with \(\omega (\delta ) = \sup \{ |f(s) - f(t)| : |s - t| \leq \delta \} \), which tends to \(0\) with \(\delta \),

    \[ \Bigl|\int f\, d\mu _k - \int _0^1f\, d\lambda \Bigr| = \Bigl|\sum _{j=1}^k\int _{(j-1)/k}^{j/k}\bigl(f(j/k) - f(t)\bigr)\, dt\Bigr| \leq \omega (1/k) \to 0. \]

    Each \(\mu _k\) lives on a finite set, of Lebesgue measure \(0\), while the limit is absolutely continuous with respect to \(\lambda \).

  3. Approximate identities. Let \(\phi \geq 0\) be continuous on \(\mathbb {R}\) with support in \([-1, 1]\) and \(\int \phi \, d\lambda = 1\), and \(\phi _k(x) = k\phi (kx)\). Then \(\int \phi _k\, d\lambda = 1\) (Theorem 28.11.2), \(\phi _k\) vanishes outside \([-1/k, 1/k]\), and for \(f \in C_c(\mathbb {R})\)

    \[ \Bigl|\int f\phi _k\, d\lambda - f(0)\Bigr| = \Bigl|\int \bigl(f(x) - f(0)\bigr)\phi _k(x)\, dx\Bigr| \leq \omega (1/k), \]

    so \(\phi _k\lambda \rightharpoonup \delta _0\): absolutely continuous measures converge to a singular one.

  4. Loss of mass. On \(\mathbb {R}\), \(\delta _k \rightharpoonup 0\), since every \(f \in C_c(\mathbb {R})\) vanishes at \(k\) for \(k\) large; and \(\frac1k\mathbf{1}_{[0, k]}\lambda \rightharpoonup 0\), since \(|\int _0^kf\, d\lambda |/k \leq \| f\| _1/k\). All these measures have total mass \(1\), the limit has mass \(0\): mass can escape to infinity, by moving away or by spreading out.

  5. Oscillation. Let \(g \geq 0\) be continuous on \(\mathbb {R}\) and periodic of period \(2\pi \), with mean value \(\bar g = \frac1{2\pi }\int _0^{2\pi }g\, d\lambda \). Then \(g(kx)\lambda \rightharpoonup \bar g\lambda \); for instance \((1 + \sin kx)\lambda \rightharpoonup \lambda \), although the densities \(1 + \sin kx\) do not converge pointwise (at \(x = \pi /2\) they run through \(2, 1, 0, 1, 2, \ldots \)). To see this, let \(f \in C_c(\mathbb {R})\) with support in \([a, b]\) and \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\), and choose \(a = a_0 {\lt} \cdots {\lt} a_N = b\) with \(|f(s) - f(a_j)| \leq \varepsilon \) for \(s \in [a_{j-1}, a_j]\). An interval \([k\alpha , k\beta ]\) consists of \(\lfloor k(\beta - \alpha )/2\pi \rfloor \) full periods and a remainder shorter than \(2\pi \), so \(\int _\alpha ^\beta g(kx)\, dx = \frac1k\int _{k\alpha }^{k\beta }g(t)\, dt = (\beta - \alpha )\bar g + r\) with \(|r| \leq 4\pi \| g\| _\infty /k\). Replacing \(f\) by \(f(a_j)\) on \([a_{j-1}, a_j]\) costs at most \(\varepsilon (b - a)\| g\| _\infty \) in \(\int fg(kx)\, dx\) and in \(\bar g\int f\, dx\), so

    \[ \Bigl|\int f(x)g(kx)\, dx - \bar g\int f(x)\, dx\Bigr| \leq 2\varepsilon (b - a)\| g\| _\infty + \frac{4\pi N\| f\| _\infty \| g\| _\infty }{k}, \]

    whose \(\limsup \) as \(k \to \infty \) is at most \(2\varepsilon (b - a)\| g\| _\infty \). Fast oscillations average out; this is the measure-theoretic content of the Riemann–Lebesgue lemma.

030F Proposition 28.15.3

Let \(\mu _k \rightharpoonup \mu \) be Radon measures on \(X\).

  1. \(\mu (U) \leq \liminf _k\mu _k(U)\) for every open \(U\).

  2. \(\mu (K) \geq \limsup _k\mu _k(K)\) for every compact \(K\).

  3. \(\mu _k(A) \to \mu (A)\) for every Borel set \(A\) with compact closure and \(\mu (\partial A) = 0\).

Proof â–¼

(1) By inner regularity, \(\mu (U)\) is the supremum of \(\mu (K)\) over compact \(K \subseteq U\); for such \(K\), Lemma 18.9.5 gives \(f \prec U\) with \(f = 1\) on \(K\), and \(\mu (K) \leq \int f\, d\mu \leq \mu (U)\). So \(\mu (U) = \sup \{ \int f\, d\mu : f \prec U\} \). For \(f \prec U\), \(\int f\, d\mu = \lim _k\int f\, d\mu _k \leq \liminf _k\mu _k(U)\).

(2) Let \(V \supseteq K\) be open. By Lemma 18.9.5 there is \(f \prec V\) with \(f = 1\) on \(K\), and \(\limsup _k\mu _k(K) \leq \lim _k\int f\, d\mu _k = \int f\, d\mu \leq \mu (V)\). By outer regularity, the infimum of \(\mu (V)\) over such \(V\) is \(\mu (K)\).

(3) The interior \(\operatorname {int}A\) and the closure \(\overline{A}\) differ by \(\partial A\), so \(\mu (\operatorname {int}A) = \mu (A) = \mu (\overline{A})\). By (1) and (2), \(\mu (A) = \mu (\operatorname {int}A) \leq \liminf _k\mu _k(\operatorname {int}A) \leq \liminf _k\mu _k(A) \leq \limsup _k\mu _k(A) \leq \limsup _k\mu _k(\overline{A}) \leq \mu (\overline{A}) = \mu (A)\).

In Example 28.15.2(1) the inequalities are strict: \(\delta _0((0, 1)) = 0 {\lt} 1\) and \(\delta _0(\{ 0\} ) = 1 {\gt} 0\); the sets \((0, 1)\) and \(\{ 0\} \) have the point \(0\), of \(\delta _0\)-measure \(1\), in their boundary. In (2) of the same example, (3) says that the proportion of the points \(j/k\) lying in an interval \([\alpha , \beta ] \subseteq [0, 1]\) tends to \(\beta - \alpha \).

The main theorem about weak convergence is a compactness theorem: bounded mass on compact sets suffices for a convergent subsequence. Its proof needs a countable supply of test functions.

030G Lemma 28.15.4

Let \(K_1 \subseteq K_2 \subseteq \cdots \) be an exhaustion of \(X\) by compact sets with \(K_m \subseteq \operatorname {int}K_{m+1}\). There is a countable set \(\mathcal{D} \subseteq C_c(X)\) such that for every \(m\), every \(f \in C_c(X)\) with \(\operatorname {supp}f \subseteq K_m\) and every \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\) there is \(g \in \mathcal{D}\) with \(\operatorname {supp}g \subseteq K_{m+1}\) and \(\| f - g\| _\infty {\lt} \varepsilon \).

Proof â–¼

First, \(C(L, \mathbb {R})\) has a countable dense subset for every compact metric space \((L, d)\). Indeed \(L\) has a countable dense subset \(\{ x_1, x_2, \ldots \} \) (for each \(j\), finitely many balls of radius \(1/j\) cover \(L\); take their centres). The functions \(u_i(y) = d(x_i, y)\) separate points: if \(y \neq z\) and \(d(x_i, y) {\lt} d(y, z)/2\), then \(d(x_i, z) {\gt} d(y, z)/2 {\gt} d(x_i, y)\). By the Stone–Weierstrass theorem (Theorem 27.14.2) the polynomials in finitely many of the \(u_i\) are dense in \(C(L, \mathbb {R})\), and so are those with rational coefficients, which form a countable set, since the \(u_i\) are bounded and real coefficients can be approximated by rational ones.

Each \(K_{m+1}\) is a compact metric space, \(X\) being metrisable; let \(\mathcal{D}_m \subseteq C(K_{m+1}, \mathbb {R})\) be countable and dense. By Lemma 18.9.5 choose \(\chi _m \in C_c(X)\) with values in \([0, 1]\), \(\chi _m = 1\) on \(K_m\) and \(\operatorname {supp}\chi _m \subseteq \operatorname {int}K_{m+1}\). For \(u \in \mathcal{D}_m\) the function \(\chi _mu\), extended by \(0\) outside \(K_{m+1}\), is continuous on \(X\): it is continuous on the open set \(\operatorname {int}K_{m+1}\) and vanishes on the open set \(X \setminus \operatorname {supp}\chi _m\), and these two open sets cover \(X\). Let \(\mathcal{D}\) be the set of all these functions, for all \(m\). Given \(f\) with support in \(K_m\), choose \(u \in \mathcal{D}_m\) with \(|f - u| {\lt} \varepsilon \) on \(K_{m+1}\); then \(f = \chi _mf\), so \(|f - \chi _mu| = \chi _m|f - u| {\lt} \varepsilon \) on \(K_{m+1}\), and both functions vanish outside \(K_{m+1}\).

030H Theorem 28.15.5 Weak compactness of Radon measures

Let \((\mu _k)\) be a sequence of Radon measures on \(X\) with \(\sup _k\mu _k(K) {\lt} \infty \) for every compact \(K \subseteq X\). Then a subsequence converges weakly to a Radon measure.

Proof â–¼

Let \((K_m)\) be an exhaustion as in Lemma 28.15.4, \(C_m = \sup _k\mu _k(K_m)\), and \(\mathcal{D} = \{ g_1, g_2, \ldots \} \) the countable set of the lemma. For each \(i\) the sequence \(\int g_i\, d\mu _k\) is bounded, by \(\| g_i\| _\infty \sup _k\mu _k(\operatorname {supp}g_i)\), so it has a convergent subsequence (Theorem 27.2.12). Choose a subsequence along which \(\int g_1\, d\mu _k\) converges, a further subsequence along which \(\int g_2\, d\mu _k\) converges, and so on; the diagonal sequence, the \(k\)-th term of the \(k\)-th subsequence, is a subsequence of each of them from its \(i\)-th term on. Renaming it \((\mu _k)\), \(\int g\, d\mu _k\) converges for every \(g \in \mathcal{D}\).

Let \(f \in C_c(X)\), with support in some \(K_m\), and \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\). Choose \(g \in \mathcal{D}\) with support in \(K_{m+1}\) and \(\| f - g\| _\infty {\lt} \varepsilon \). Then

\[ \Bigl|\int f\, d\mu _k - \int f\, d\mu _l\Bigr| \leq 2\varepsilon C_{m+1} + \Bigl|\int g\, d\mu _k - \int g\, d\mu _l\Bigr|, \]

so \(\int f\, d\mu _k\) is a Cauchy sequence. Its limit \(\Lambda f\) is linear and positive in \(f\), so by Theorem 28.14.6 it is \(\int f\, d\mu \) for a Radon measure \(\mu \), and \(\mu _k \rightharpoonup \mu \).

030I Corollary 28.15.6

Let \(X\) be a compact metric space. Every sequence of Borel probability measures on \(X\) has a subsequence converging weakly to a Borel probability measure.

Proof â–¼

A compact metric space is locally compact and Hausdorff, and it has a countable basis: the balls of radius \(1/j\) centred at the points of finite sets of such balls covering \(X\). Every Borel measure on \(X\) that is finite is a Radon measure (Proposition 28.14.2), and \(\mu _k(X) = 1\) bounds the masses. Theorem 28.15.5 gives a weakly convergent subsequence, and its limit \(\mu \) satisfies \(\mu (X) = \int 1\, d\mu = \lim \int 1\, d\mu _k = 1\), as \(1 \in C_c(X)\) for compact \(X\).

030J Remark 28.15.7

  1. The hypothesis cannot be dropped: \(k\delta _0\) has no weakly convergent subsequence on \(\mathbb {R}\). On non-compact spaces the limit of probability measures may have smaller mass, by Example 28.15.2(4); by Proposition 28.15.3(1) applied to \(U = X\), mass can only be lost, never gained.

  2. Theorem 28.15.5 is a case of the Banach–Alaoglu theorem: bounded sets of linear functionals are compact for the weak-\(*\) topology (Chapter 30).

  3. In the calculus of variations and in geometric measure theory this is the basic source of limits. A minimising sequence of surfaces for the area, all contained in a fixed ball, has area measures with bounded mass, and a subsequence of these converges weakly to a Radon measure; the hard part, the regularity theory, is to show that the limit is again the area measure of a surface, perhaps with singularities. For curves, Exercise 28.23.15 shows that length can jump in the limit.
