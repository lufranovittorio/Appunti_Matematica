10.7 Canonical forms00J5

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

Let \(f \colon V \to V\) be as in the previous section. By Example 8.7.2, \(V\) is a \(k[x]\)-module \(V_f\) with \(x\) acting as \(f\); it is finitely generated and torsion, being annihilated by \(\chi _f\).

00J6 Definition 10.7.1

The companion matrix of a monic \(g = x^d + c_{d-1}x^{d-1} + \cdots + c_0\) is the \(d \times d\) matrix \(C(g)\) with \(1\)’s below the diagonal, last column \((-c_0, \ldots , -c_{d-1})^T\) and zeros elsewhere; it is the matrix of multiplication by \(x\) on \(k[x]/(g)\) in the basis \(1, x, \ldots , x^{d-1}\). The Jordan block \(J_e(\lambda )\) is the \(e \times e\) matrix with \(\lambda \) on the diagonal, \(1\) directly above the diagonal and zeros elsewhere.

00J7 Lemma 10.7.2

The minimal and characteristic polynomials of \(C(g)\) both equal \(g\). The minimal and characteristic polynomials of \(J_e(\lambda )\) both equal \((x - \lambda )^e\).

Proof â–¼

On \(k[x]/(g)\) a polynomial \(p\) acts as zero iff \(p \cdot 1 = p\) lies in \((g)\), so the minimal polynomial is \(g\). The characteristic polynomial is monic of degree \(d\) and divisible by \(g\) (Definition 10.6.4), hence equal to \(g\). The matrix \(J_e(\lambda )\) is the matrix of \(x\) on \(k[x]/((x - \lambda )^e)\) in the basis \((x - \lambda )^{e-1}, \ldots , (x - \lambda ), 1\), and the same argument applies.

00J8 Theorem 10.7.3 Rational canonical form

There are unique monic polynomials \(f_1 \mid f_2 \mid \cdots \mid f_s\) of positive degree such that \(f\) has, in a suitable basis, the block diagonal matrix \(C(f_1) \oplus \cdots \oplus C(f_s)\). Moreover \(m_f = f_s\) and \(\chi _f = f_1 \cdots f_s\). Two endomorphisms (or matrices) are similar if and only if they have the same \(f_i\), the invariant factors.

Proof â–¼

By Theorem 8.11.3, \(V_f \cong \bigoplus _i k[x]/(f_i)\) with \(f_1 \mid \cdots \mid f_s\) monic of positive degree (there is no free part since \(V_f\) is torsion), uniquely determined. Bases of the summands as in Definition 10.7.1 give the block form. An isomorphism \(V_f \cong V_g\) of \(k[x]\)-modules is the same as a linear isomorphism \(h\) with \(hf = gh\), so similarity corresponds to isomorphism of modules. The polynomial \(f_s\) kills every summand and is the minimal polynomial of the last one, so \(m_f = f_s\); the characteristic polynomial of a block matrix is the product of those of the blocks, which gives \(\chi _f\) by Lemma 10.7.2.

00J9 Theorem 10.7.4 Jordan normal form

Suppose \(\chi _f\) splits into linear factors over \(k\) (for instance, \(k\) algebraically closed). Then \(f\) has, in a suitable basis, a block diagonal matrix \(J_{e_1}(\lambda _1) \oplus \cdots \oplus J_{e_r}(\lambda _r)\), and the multiset of pairs \((\lambda _i, e_i)\) is uniquely determined by \(f\).

Proof â–¼

By Corollary 8.11.4, \(V_f \cong \bigoplus _i k[x]/(p_i^{e_i})\) with \(p_i\) monic irreducible, uniquely. Each \(p_i\) divides \(\chi _f\), hence is \(x - \lambda _i\). Use the bases of Lemma 10.7.2.

00JA Example 10.7.5

A nilpotent endomorphism (\(f^m = 0\) for some \(m\)) over any field has \(\chi _f = x^n\) (its only possible eigenvalue is \(0\), and \(m_f \mid x^m\)), so it is similar to a direct sum of blocks \(J_e(0)\); the sizes of the blocks form a partition of \(n\), and they classify nilpotent endomorphisms up to similarity. Such nilpotent operators, and their Jordan decompositions, organise the limiting behaviour of variations of Hodge structure (Chapter 67).

02GK Counterexample 10.7.6

The hypothesis that \(\chi _f\) splits cannot be dropped. Over \(\mathbb {R}\), the rotation matrix \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\) is not similar to any block diagonal matrix of Jordan blocks. Such a matrix is upper triangular, and the determinant of an upper triangular matrix is the product of its diagonal entries (in the Leibniz expansion every other permutation meets an entry below the diagonal), so its characteristic polynomial is \(\prod _i(x - a_{ii})\), which splits over \(\mathbb {R}\). But similar matrices have the same characteristic polynomial, and \(\chi _A = x^2 + 1\) has no real root. The rational canonical form (Theorem 10.7.3) of \(A\) over \(\mathbb {R}\) is the companion matrix \(C(x^2 + 1)\), which is \(A\) itself; over \(\mathbb {C}\), where \(x^2 + 1 = (x - i)(x + i)\), \(A\) is similar to \(J_1(i) \oplus J_1(-i)\).

02GL Proposition 10.7.7

An endomorphism \(f\) has a block diagonal matrix of Jordan blocks in some basis if and only if \(\chi _f\) splits into linear factors over \(k\). Every square matrix over \(k\) is similar to such a matrix if and only if \(k\) is algebraically closed.

Proof â–¼

If \(\chi _f\) splits, use Theorem 10.7.4. Conversely, a block diagonal matrix of Jordan blocks \(J_{e_i}(\lambda _i)\) is upper triangular, so its characteristic polynomial is \(\prod _i(x - \lambda _i)^{e_i}\), as in Counterexample 10.7.6; and \(\chi _f\) is the characteristic polynomial of any matrix of \(f\). For the second statement, if \(k\) is algebraically closed every \(\chi _f\) splits. If \(k\) is not, some monic polynomial \(g\) of degree \(d \geq 1\) does not split, and the companion matrix \(C(g)\) has characteristic polynomial \(g\) (Lemma 10.7.2), so it is not similar to a matrix of Jordan blocks.

When \(\chi _f\) does not split, the primary decomposition still gives a canonical form. By Corollary 8.11.4, \(V_f \cong \bigoplus _ik[x]/(p_i^{e_i})\) with \(p_i\) monic irreducible, uniquely, so \(f\) is similar to the block diagonal matrix \(C(p_1^{e_1}) \oplus \cdots \oplus C(p_r^{e_r})\), the primary rational canonical form; for \(p_i = x - \lambda _i\) one may use \(J_{e_i}(\lambda _i)\) instead of \(C(p_i^{e_i})\). Over \(\mathbb {R}\) there is a convenient replacement for \(C(p^e)\) as well.

02GM Proposition 10.7.8 Real Jordan form

For \(a \in \mathbb {R}\) and \(b {\gt} 0\) let \(R = \begin{pmatrix} a & -b \\ b & a \end{pmatrix}\) and let the real Jordan block \(J_e(a, b)\) be the \(2e \times 2e\) block matrix with \(R\) on the diagonal, the \(2 \times 2\) identity matrix directly above the diagonal, and zeros elsewhere. Then \(J_e(a, b)\) is similar to \(C(p^e)\), where \(p = (x - a)^2 + b^2\). Consequently every real square matrix is similar to a block diagonal matrix of blocks \(J_e(\lambda )\) with \(\lambda \in \mathbb {R}\) and \(J_e(a, b)\) with \(b {\gt} 0\), and the multiset of these blocks is uniquely determined.

Proof â–¼

Write \(M = J_e(a, b) = D + N\), where \(D\) is block diagonal with blocks \(R\) and \(N\) has the identity blocks above the diagonal. The matrices \(D\) and \(N\) commute, since both are built from \(2 \times 2\) blocks that commute with each other; \(N^e = 0\) and \(N^{e-1} \neq 0\). The characteristic polynomial of \(R\) is \(p\), so \(p(R) = 0\) by Theorem 10.6.3 and \(p(D) = 0\). Hence

\[ p(M) = (D - aI + N)^2 + b^2I = p(D) + 2(D - aI)N + N^2 = N\bigl(2(D - aI) + N\bigr). \]

The matrix \(D - aI\) is block diagonal with invertible blocks \(\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & -b \\ b & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\), and it commutes with the nilpotent matrix \(N\); so \(U = 2(D - aI) + N\) is invertible, with inverse \(\sum _{j \geq 0}(-1)^j(2(D - aI))^{-j-1}N^j\), a finite sum. As \(N\) and \(U\) commute, \(p(M)^j = N^jU^j\), which vanishes if and only if \(j \geq e\). The minimal polynomial of \(M\) divides a power of the irreducible \(p\), so it is \(p^e\); the characteristic polynomial is \(\chi _R^e = p^e\), since \(M\) is block upper triangular. By Theorem 10.7.3, the invariant factors of \(M\) have product \(p^e\) and the last one equals \(p^e\), so there is only one, and \(M\) is similar to \(C(p^e)\). The monic irreducible real polynomials are the \(x - \lambda \) and the \((x - a)^2 + b^2\) with \(b {\gt} 0\) (Theorem 9.7.3: a nonreal root \(a + ib\) comes with its conjugate), so the last statement follows from the primary rational canonical form.

For example, the rotation of Counterexample 10.7.6 is the real Jordan block \(J_1(0, 1)\).

We turn to a decomposition that makes sense without any splitting. An endomorphism \(s\) is called semisimple if its minimal polynomial is separable (Definition 9.4.1), that is, has no repeated roots in a splitting field. We need that the minimal polynomial does not change under extension of the field.

02GN Lemma 10.7.9

Let \(L/k\) be a field extension and \(A \in M_n(k)\). The minimal polynomial of \(A\) over \(L\) equals its minimal polynomial over \(k\). In particular \(A\) is semisimple over \(k\) if and only if it is semisimple over \(L\), and \(A\) is semisimple if and only if it is diagonalisable over a splitting field of \(m_A\).

Proof â–¼

Let \(d\) be the least integer such that \(I, A, \ldots , A^d\) are linearly dependent over \(k\). Linear dependence of finitely many vectors of \(k^{n^2}\) over \(k\) or over \(L\) means that the matrix having them as columns has rank less than the number of vectors, that is, that all its maximal minors vanish; this does not depend on the field. So \(d\) is the same over \(k\) and over \(L\), and the relation \(A^d = -\sum _{j{\lt}d}c_jA^j\) is unique over \(L\), because \(I, \ldots , A^{d-1}\) are independent over \(L\); as it exists with \(c_j \in k\), the minimal polynomials agree. The last statement follows from Theorem 10.6.6, applied over the splitting field.

02GO Theorem 10.7.10 Jordan–Chevalley decomposition

Let \(k\) be a perfect field (Definition 9.4.3) and \(f\) an endomorphism of \(V\). There are unique endomorphisms \(s\) and \(n\) of \(V\) with \(f = s + n\), \(s\) semisimple, \(n\) nilpotent, and \(sn = ns\). Moreover \(s\) and \(n\) are polynomials in \(f\) with coefficients in \(k\).

Proof â–¼

Step 1: existence when \(\chi _f\) splits, over any field. Let \(\chi _f = \prod _i(x - \lambda _i)^{e_i}\) with distinct \(\lambda _i\). By the kernel lemma (Lemma 10.6.5), \(V = \bigoplus _iV_i\) with \(V_i = \ker (f - \lambda _i)^{e_i}\), since \(\chi _f(f) = 0\). The ideals \(((x - \lambda _i)^{e_i})\) are pairwise coprime, so by the Chinese remainder theorem (Theorem 8.3.3) there is \(q \in k[x]\) with \(q \equiv \lambda _i \bmod (x - \lambda _i)^{e_i}\) for all \(i\). Then \(s = q(f)\) acts on \(V_i\) as \(\lambda _i\), so it is diagonalisable, hence semisimple, and \(n = f - s\) acts on \(V_i\) as \(f - \lambda _i\), which is nilpotent there; so \(n\) is nilpotent. Both are polynomials in \(f\), so they commute.

Step 2: uniqueness, over any field, for decompositions as in Step 1. Let \(f = s' + n'\) be another decomposition. Then \(s'\) and \(n'\) commute with \(f = s' + n'\), hence with the polynomials \(s\) and \(n\) in \(f\). Now \(s - s' = n' - n\). The right side is nilpotent: \(n\) and \(n'\) commute, so \((n' - n)^{2m}\) is a sum of terms \(n'^jn^{2m-j}\) which vanish for \(m\) large. The left side is semisimple: over a splitting field \(L\) of \(m_sm_{s'}\), the commuting endomorphisms \(s\) and \(s'\) are diagonalisable (Lemma 10.7.9), hence simultaneously diagonalisable (Theorem 10.6.6), so \(s - s'\) is diagonalisable over \(L\). A diagonalisable nilpotent endomorphism is \(0\), as its eigenvalues are \(0\). So \(s = s'\) and \(n = n'\).

Step 3: \(k\) perfect. Let \(g\) be the product of the distinct monic irreducible factors of \(\chi _f\). Each is separable because \(k\) is perfect (Proposition 9.4.4), and distinct monic irreducible polynomials have no common root, so \(g\) is separable. Let \(L\) be a splitting field of \(g\); then \(\chi _f\) splits over \(L\), and \(L/k\) is Galois with group \(G\) and \(L^G = k\) (Theorem 9.5.3). Let \(A \in M_n(k)\) be the matrix of \(f\) in a basis. By Steps 1 and 2 over \(L\), \(A = S + N\) uniquely, with \(S = q(A)\) for some \(q \in L[x]\). For \(\sigma \in G\), applying \(\sigma \) to all entries is a ring automorphism of \(M_n(L)\) fixing \(A\), so \(A = \sigma (S) + \sigma (N)\), with \(\sigma (N)\) nilpotent, \(\sigma (S)\sigma (N) = \sigma (N)\sigma (S)\), and \(\sigma (S)\) semisimple: its minimal polynomial is \(\sigma (m_S)\), which is separable since \(am_S + bm_S' = 1\) implies \(\sigma (a)\sigma (m_S) + \sigma (b)\sigma (m_S)' = 1\) (Proposition 9.4.2). By uniqueness \(\sigma (S) = S\), so the entries of \(S\) lie in \(L^G = k\). Then \(S\) and \(N = A - S\) have entries in \(k\), and \(S\) is semisimple over \(k\) by Lemma 10.7.9. Any decomposition over \(k\) is one over \(L\), so uniqueness over \(k\) follows from Step 2. Finally, with \(d = \deg m_A\), the matrices \(I, A, \ldots , A^{d-1}\) form a basis of \(L[A]\) over \(L\) (by the lemma, over \(L\) too the minimal polynomial has degree \(d\)); writing \(S = \sum _jc_jA^j\) and applying \(\sigma \) shows \(\sigma (c_j) = c_j\), so the \(c_j\) lie in \(k\).

02GP Counterexample 10.7.11

Perfectness cannot be dropped. Let \(k = \mathbb {F}_p(t)\) and \(f\) the endomorphism of \(k^p\) with matrix \(C(x^p - t)\), whose minimal polynomial is \(x^p - t\) (Lemma 10.7.2), irreducible but inseparable (Counterexample 9.4.5). As a \(k[x]\)-module, \(V_f \cong k[x]/(x^p - t)\) is a field, so \(f\) has no invariant subspaces other than \(0\) and \(V\): it is “irreducible”, the opposite of nilpotent. Yet \(f\) is not semisimple, and it has no decomposition \(f = s + n\) as in Theorem 10.7.10 with \(s\) and \(n\) defined over \(k\). Indeed, over \(L = k(\beta )\) with \(\beta ^p = t\) we have \(x^p - t = (x - \beta )^p\), so \(\chi _f\) splits and \(f - \beta \) is nilpotent. A decomposition over \(k\) would be one over \(L\), where by uniqueness (Steps 1 and 2 of the proof) \(s = \beta I\); but \(\beta \notin k\). So over imperfect fields “irreducible” and “semisimple” diverge, and the semisimple part of \(f\) exists only after an inseparable extension.
