10.7 Gaussian and Eisenstein integers030W

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

In Section 10.5 the Gaussian integers were a tool for proving Fermat’s theorem on sums of two squares. Here we study the ring \(\mathbb {Z}[i]\) for its own sake: we determine all its primes, which describes how each rational prime factors there, and we use the factorisation to count the representations of an integer as a sum of two squares. The same method applied to the ring \(\mathbb {Z}[\omega ]\) of Eisenstein integers answers the analogous question for \(x^2 + xy + y^2\). Finally, the ring \(\mathbb {Z}[\sqrt{-5}]\) shows that unique factorisation, on which everything here rests, can fail; measuring this failure is one of the origins of algebraic number theory.

Recall that \(\mathbb {Z}[i]\) is a Euclidean domain, hence a UFD, with multiplicative norm \(N(a + bi) = a^2 + b^2\) and units \(\pm 1\), \(\pm i\) (Section 10.5). Two elements are associates if they differ by a unit factor; in a UFD every nonzero nonunit is a product of primes, unique up to order and associates (Theorem 8.5.4), and irreducible elements are prime. An element whose norm is a rational prime is irreducible: if \(z = uv\), then \(N(u)N(v)\) is prime, so \(N(u) = 1\) or \(N(v) = 1\).

030X Theorem 10.7.1 Gaussian primes

Up to associates, the primes of \(\mathbb {Z}[i]\) are:

  1. \(1 + i\), with \(2 = -i(1 + i)^2\);

  2. the rational primes \(q \equiv 3 \pmod4\);

  3. for each rational prime \(p \equiv 1 \pmod4\), written \(p = a^2 + b^2\), the two elements \(a + bi\) and \(a - bi\), which are not associates, with \(p = (a + bi)(a - bi)\).

Proof â–¼

The elements listed are prime. \(N(1 + i) = 2\) and \(N(a \pm bi) = p\) are rational primes, so these elements are irreducible, hence prime. A rational prime \(q \equiv 3 \pmod4\) is irreducible in \(\mathbb {Z}[i]\): if \(q = zw\) with nonunits, then \(q^2 = N(z)N(w)\) forces \(N(z) = q\), so \(q\) would be a sum of two squares, contradicting Theorem 10.5.1. The associates of \(a + bi\) are \(a + bi\), \(-a - bi\), \(-b + ai\) and \(b - ai\); if \(a - bi\) were one of them, then \(b = 0\), or \(a = 0\), or \(a = \pm b\), and \(p = a^2 + b^2\) would be a square or even, which it is not.

Every prime is associate to one of them. Let \(\pi \) be a prime of \(\mathbb {Z}[i]\). Then \(\pi \) divides \(N(\pi ) = \pi \bar\pi \), a positive integer greater than \(1\); factoring it into rational primes, \(\pi \) divides one of them, say \(p\). Then \(N(\pi )\) divides \(N(p) = p^2\), so \(N(\pi ) = p\) or \(p^2\). If \(N(\pi ) = p^2\), then \(p/\pi \) has norm \(1\), so \(\pi \) is associate to \(p\), and \(p\) is prime in \(\mathbb {Z}[i]\); this excludes \(p = 2 = -i(1 + i)^2\) and \(p \equiv 1 \pmod4\), which are products of two nonunits, so \(p \equiv 3 \pmod4\). If \(N(\pi ) = p\), then \(p\) is a sum of two squares, so \(p = 2\) or \(p \equiv 1 \pmod4\). For \(p = 2\), \(\pi = x + yi\) with \(x^2 + y^2 = 2\), so \(x, y = \pm 1\) and \(\pi \) is associate to \(1 + i\). For \(p \equiv 1 \pmod4\), \(\pi \) divides \(p = (a + bi)(a - bi)\), hence divides one of the two factors, which has the same norm; so \(\pi \) is associate to \(a + bi\) or to \(a - bi\).

So a rational prime \(p\) behaves in one of three ways in \(\mathbb {Z}[i]\): it ramifies (\(p = 2\) becomes a unit times the square of a prime), it stays inert (\(p \equiv 3 \pmod4\) remains prime), or it splits into two non-associate primes (\(p \equiv 1 \pmod4\)). Which case occurs is decided by \(p\) modulo \(4\), that is, by the Legendre symbol \((\frac{-1}{p})\) (Corollary 10.4.3); this is the simplest instance of a reciprocity law, describing the factorisation of primes in an extension by congruence conditions.

030Y Example 10.7.2

To factor \(z = 7 + i\), factor its norm: \(N(z) = 50 = 2 \cdot 5^2\). So \(z\) is divisible by \(1 + i\) and by two primes of norm \(5\), each associate to \(2 + i\) or \(2 - i\). Indeed \((7 + i)/(1 + i) = (7 + i)(1 - i)/2 = 4 - 3i\), and \(4 - 3i = -i(3 + 4i) = -i(2 + i)^2\), so

\[ 7 + i = -i(1 + i)(2 + i)^2 . \]

Note that \(2 - i\) does not divide \(7 + i\), although it divides \(N(7 + i)\): the two primes above \(5\) must be kept apart. As an application, \(7 + i\) is not a square times a unit, although its norm \(50\) is twice a square.

The factorisation counts the representations of an integer as a sum of two squares, refining Theorem 10.5.3. Let \(r_2(n)\) be the number of pairs \((x, y) \in \mathbb {Z}^2\) with \(x^2 + y^2 = n\), signs and order counted; and let \(\chi \) be the function on positive integers with \(\chi (d) = 0\) for even \(d\), \(\chi (d) = 1\) for \(d \equiv 1 \pmod4\) and \(\chi (d) = -1\) for \(d \equiv 3 \pmod4\). It is completely multiplicative, since the product of two odd numbers is \(\equiv 1 \pmod4\) exactly when both or neither are \(\equiv 3 \pmod4\).

030Z Theorem 10.7.3 Jacobi’s two squares theorem

For every \(n \geq 1\),

\[ r_2(n) = 4\sum _{d \mid n}\chi (d) = 4\bigl(d_1(n) - d_3(n)\bigr), \]

where \(d_1(n)\) and \(d_3(n)\) are the numbers of positive divisors of \(n\) congruent to \(1\) and to \(3\) modulo \(4\).

Proof â–¼

\(r_2(n)\) is the number of \(z \in \mathbb {Z}[i]\) with \(N(z) = n\). Write \(n = 2^e\prod _jp_j^{a_j}\prod _kq_k^{b_k}\) with distinct primes \(p_j \equiv 1\) and \(q_k \equiv 3 \pmod4\), and choose primes \(\pi _j\) with \(p_j = \pi _j\bar\pi _j\) (Theorem 10.7.1). By unique factorisation, every nonzero \(z\) is uniquely

\[ z = u(1 + i)^{e^{\prime }}\prod _j\pi _j^{s_j}\bar\pi _j^{t_j}\prod _kq_k^{c_k}\cdot w, \]

with \(u\) a unit and \(w\) a product of primes not dividing \(n\), and then \(N(z) = 2^{e^{\prime }}\prod _jp_j^{s_j + t_j}\prod _kq_k^{2c_k}N(w)\). So \(N(z) = n\) if and only if \(w = 1\), \(e^{\prime } = e\), \(s_j + t_j = a_j\) and \(2c_k = b_k\). If some \(b_k\) is odd there is no solution; otherwise there are \(4\) choices of \(u\) and \(a_j + 1\) choices of \((s_j, t_j)\) for each \(j\), so \(r_2(n) = 4\prod _j(a_j + 1)\).

On the other side, \(\chi * \mathbf{1}\), \(n \mapsto \sum _{d \mid n}\chi (d)\), is multiplicative (Proposition 10.6.2(3)), and on prime powers it takes the values \(\sum _{i=0}^e\chi (2^i) = 1\), \(\sum _{i=0}^a\chi (p^i) = a + 1\) for \(p \equiv 1 \pmod4\), and \(\sum _{i=0}^b(-1)^i\), which is \(1\) for even \(b\) and \(0\) for odd \(b\), for \(q \equiv 3 \pmod4\). The product of these values is \(\prod _j(a_j + 1)\) if all \(b_k\) are even and \(0\) otherwise.

0310 Example 10.7.4

\(r_2(5) = 4(2 - 0) = 8\): \((\pm 1, \pm 2)\), \((\pm 2, \pm 1)\). \(r_2(25) = 4 \cdot 3 = 12\): \((0, \pm 5)\), \((\pm 5, 0)\), \((\pm 3, \pm 4)\), \((\pm 4, \pm 3)\). \(r_2(9) = 4(2 - 1) = 4\): only \((\pm 3, 0)\) and \((0, \pm 3)\), as \(9 = 3^2\) with \(3\) inert. \(r_2(3) = 4(1 - 1) = 0\). A pretty consequence, which uses areas and is only sketched here: \(\sum _{n \leq N}r_2(n)\) counts the points of \(\mathbb {Z}^2\) in the disc of radius \(\sqrt N\), about \(\pi N\) of them, while by the theorem it equals \(4\sum _{d \leq N}\chi (d)\lfloor N/d\rfloor \), which is about \(4N(1 - \frac13 + \frac15 - \cdots )\); this is how Gauss’s circle problem yields Leibniz’s series \(1 - \frac13 + \frac15 - \cdots = \frac\pi 4\).

10.7.1 Eisenstein integers0311

Let \(\omega = e^{2\pi i/3} = \frac{-1 + \sqrt{-3}}{2}\), a root of \(x^2 + x + 1\), so that \(\omega ^2 = -1 - \omega = \bar\omega \). The ring of Eisenstein integers is \(\mathbb {Z}[\omega ] = \{ a + b\omega : a, b \in \mathbb {Z}\} \); it is closed under multiplication because \(\omega ^2 = -1 - \omega \). Its elements form the triangular lattice in \(\mathbb {C}\), and the norm is

\[ N(a + b\omega ) = |a + b\omega |^2 = (a + b\omega )(a + b\bar\omega ) = a^2 - ab + b^2, \]

using \(\omega + \bar\omega = -1\) and \(\omega \bar\omega = 1\). It is multiplicative, with values in \(\mathbb {N}\). Note that \(\sqrt{-3} = 1 + 2\omega \in \mathbb {Z}[\omega ]\).

0312 Proposition 10.7.5

  1. \(\mathbb {Z}[\omega ]\) is a Euclidean domain for \(N\), hence a UFD.

  2. Its units are the six elements \(\pm 1\), \(\pm \omega \), \(\pm \omega ^2\) of norm \(1\), the sixth roots of unity.

  3. Up to associates its primes are: \(1 - \omega \), with \(3 = -\omega ^2(1 - \omega )^2\); the rational primes \(q \equiv 2 \pmod3\); and for each rational prime \(p \equiv 1 \pmod3\) two non-associate primes \(\pi \), \(\bar\pi \) with \(p = \pi \bar\pi \).

Proof â–¼

(1) Let \(a, b \in \mathbb {Z}[\omega ]\) with \(b \neq 0\), and write \(a/b = u + v\omega \) with \(u, v \in \mathbb {R}\) (possible since \(1\) and \(\omega \) are a basis of \(\mathbb {C}\) over \(\mathbb {R}\)). Choose integers \(m\), \(n\) with \(|u - m|, |v - n| \leq \frac12\), and put \(q = m + n\omega \), \(r = a - qb\). With \(s = u - m\) and \(t = v - n\), \(|a/b - q|^2 = s^2 - st + t^2 \leq \frac14 + \frac14 + \frac14 {\lt} 1\), so \(N(r) {\lt} N(b)\). A Euclidean domain is a UFD (Theorem 8.5.4).

(2) As for \(\mathbb {Z}[i]\), the units are the elements of norm \(1\). From \(4(a^2 - ab + b^2) = (2a - b)^2 + 3b^2 = 4\) we get \(b \in \{ 0, \pm 1\} \), and the solutions are \(\pm 1\), \(\pm \omega \) and \(\pm (1 + \omega ) = \mp \omega ^2\).

(3) Since \(1 - \omega \) has norm \(1 + 1 + 1 = 3\), it is prime, and \((1 - \omega )^2 = 1 - 2\omega + \omega ^2 = -3\omega \), so \(3 = -\omega ^2(1 - \omega )^2\). The values of the norm modulo \(3\): \(4N(a + b\omega ) = (2a - b)^2 + 3b^2 \equiv (2a - b)^2 \pmod3\), which is \(0\) or \(1\); as \(4 \equiv 1\), no norm is \(\equiv 2 \pmod3\). Hence a rational prime \(q \equiv 2 \pmod3\) is prime in \(\mathbb {Z}[\omega ]\), by the argument used for \(\mathbb {Z}[i]\). Let \(p \equiv 1 \pmod3\). By quadratic reciprocity (Theorem 10.4.9) and Corollary 10.4.3,

\[ \Bigl(\frac{-3}{p}\Bigr) = \Bigl(\frac{-1}{p}\Bigr)\Bigl(\frac{3}{p}\Bigr) = (-1)^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\cdot (-1)^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\Bigl(\frac{p}{3}\Bigr) = \Bigl(\frac{p}{3}\Bigr) = \Bigl(\frac{1}{3}\Bigr) = 1, \]

using that the Legendre symbol is multiplicative (Proposition 10.4.2). So there is \(x\) with \(p \mid x^2 + 3 = (x + \sqrt{-3})(x - \sqrt{-3})\). Now \(p\) divides neither factor: \((x \pm \sqrt{-3})/p = (x \pm 1)/p \pm (2/p)\omega \) does not lie in \(\mathbb {Z}[\omega ]\), as \(p\) is odd. So \(p\) is not prime, \(p = \pi \pi ^{\prime }\) with nonunits, and \(N(\pi ) = p\), so \(\pi \) is prime and \(p = \pi \bar\pi \).

\(\pi = a + b\omega \) and \(\bar\pi \) are not associates. Otherwise \(\pi \) divides \(\bar\pi \), hence the integer \(\pi + \bar\pi = 2a - b\) and the element \(\pi - \bar\pi = b(\omega - \bar\omega ) = b\sqrt{-3}\). If \(\pi \) divides an element \(z\), then \(p = N(\pi )\) divides \(N(z)\); so \(p \mid (2a - b)^2\) and \(p \mid 3b^2\), hence \(p \mid b\) (as \(p \neq 3\)), then \(p \mid 2a\) and \(p \mid a\). But then \(p^2 \mid a^2 - ab + b^2 = p\), which is absurd.

Finally let \(\pi \) be any prime. As in Theorem 10.7.1, \(\pi \) divides a rational prime \(p\), and \(N(\pi ) = p\) or \(p^2\). If \(N(\pi ) = p^2\), then \(\pi \) is associate to \(p\), so \(p\) is prime in \(\mathbb {Z}[\omega ]\); this excludes \(p = 3\) and \(p \equiv 1 \pmod3\), which are products of two nonunits, so \(p \equiv 2 \pmod3\). If \(N(\pi ) = p\), then \(p \not\equiv 2 \pmod3\); for \(p = 3\), \(\pi \) divides \((1 - \omega )^2\), hence is associate to \(1 - \omega \), and for \(p \equiv 1 \pmod3\), \(\pi \) divides one of the two primes of norm \(p\) found above and is associate to it.

0313 Corollary 10.7.6

A prime \(p\) can be written as \(p = x^2 + xy + y^2\) with \(x, y \in \mathbb {Z}\) if and only if \(p = 3\) or \(p \equiv 1 \pmod3\); and as \(p = x^2 + 3y^2\) if and only if \(p = 3\) or \(p \equiv 1 \pmod3\).

Proof â–¼

\(a^2 - ab + b^2 = x^2 + xy + y^2\) with \(x = a\), \(y = -b\), so the primes of the first form are the norms of elements of \(\mathbb {Z}[\omega ]\), which by the proposition are \(3\) and the \(p \equiv 1 \pmod3\). For the second form: \(3 = 0^2 + 3 \cdot 1^2\), and \(x^2 + 3y^2 = N(x + y\sqrt{-3})\) is a norm, so the condition is necessary. Conversely let \(p \equiv 1 \pmod3\) and \(p = N(a + b\omega )\). Among the six associates \(\varepsilon (a + b\omega )\) choose one, \(c + d\omega \), with \(d\) even: multiplying by \(\omega \) sends \(a + b\omega \) to \(a\omega + b\omega ^2 = -b + (a - b)\omega \), so the second coordinates of \(a + b\omega \), \(\omega (a + b\omega )\), \(\omega ^2(a + b\omega )\) are \(b\), \(a - b\), \(-a\), and one of \(b\), \(a - b\), \(a\) is even. Then \(c + d\omega = (c - d/2) + (d/2)\sqrt{-3}\), and \(p = (c - d/2)^2 + 3(d/2)^2\).

For example \(7 = 2^2 + 2 + 1 = 2^2 + 3 \cdot 1^2\), \(13 = 3^2 + 3 + 1 = 1^2 + 3 \cdot 2^2\), \(31 = 5^2 + 5 + 1 = 2^2 + 3 \cdot 3^2\), while \(5\), \(11\), \(17\) are of neither form. The ring \(\mathbb {Z}[\omega ]\) is the setting of Euler’s proof of Fermat’s last theorem for exponent \(3\), and its sixfold symmetry is that of the hexagonal lattice and of the elliptic curve \(\mathbb {C}/\mathbb {Z}[\omega ]\) (Chapter 61).

10.7.2 Failure of unique factorisation0314

0315 Example 10.7.7

In \(\mathbb {Z}[\sqrt{-5}] = \{ a + b\sqrt{-5} : a, b \in \mathbb {Z}\} \), with multiplicative norm \(N(a + b\sqrt{-5}) = a^2 + 5b^2\),

\[ 6 = 2 \cdot 3 = (1 + \sqrt{-5})(1 - \sqrt{-5}) \]

are two factorisations into irreducible elements that do not correspond to each other. The norms of \(2\), \(3\), \(1 \pm \sqrt{-5}\) are \(4\), \(9\), \(6\), \(6\). The units are \(\pm 1\), the only elements of norm \(1\). A proper factor of one of these four elements would have norm \(2\) or \(3\), but \(a^2 + 5b^2\) never equals \(2\) or \(3\) (if \(b \neq 0\) it is at least \(5\)). So all four are irreducible, and \(2\) is not associate to \(1 \pm \sqrt{-5}\), having a different norm. Hence \(\mathbb {Z}[\sqrt{-5}]\) is not a UFD, and \(2\) is irreducible but not prime: it divides \((1 + \sqrt{-5})(1 - \sqrt{-5})\) but neither factor.

Kummer and Dedekind restored unique factorisation by passing from elements to ideals: in \(\mathbb {Z}[\sqrt{-5}]\) the ideals \(\mathfrak {p} = (2, 1 + \sqrt{-5})\), \(\mathfrak {q} = (3, 1 + \sqrt{-5})\) and \(\bar{\mathfrak {q}} = (3, 1 - \sqrt{-5})\) are prime, and \((6) = \mathfrak {p}^2\mathfrak {q}\bar{\mathfrak {q}}\), with \((2) = \mathfrak {p}^2\), \((3) = \mathfrak {q}\bar{\mathfrak {q}}\) and \((1 \pm \sqrt{-5}) = \mathfrak {p}\mathfrak {q}\) or \(\mathfrak {p}\bar{\mathfrak {q}}\); the two factorisations of \(6\) are two groupings of the same four prime ideals. The failure of unique factorisation of elements is measured by the class group, the group of ideals modulo principal ideals, which is finite; its order, the class number, is \(2\) for \(\mathbb {Z}[\sqrt{-5}]\). We do not develop ideal theory here and only state these facts [IR90]; ideals of rings are studied in Chapter 14. The next section computes class numbers in the equivalent language of quadratic forms, which is how Gauss found them.
