18.13 The Stone–Čech compactification03A4

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

The one-point compactification adds as little as possible to a locally compact space. At the other extreme one can ask for a compactification to which every bounded continuous function extends: for \((0, 1]\), the function \(\sin (1/x)\) has no continuous extension to \([0, 1]\), so \([0, 1]\) is not such a compactification, and one has to add many more points. The construction embeds the space in a large cube by all its functions to \([0, 1]\) and takes the closure. It works for exactly the spaces that have enough such functions.

The spaces in question are the completely regular ones (Definition 18.8.10). Normal spaces are completely regular, and so are subspaces and products of completely regular spaces (Proposition 18.8.11). Compact Hausdorff spaces are normal (Proposition 18.8.3), so every subspace of a compact Hausdorff space is completely regular, for instance every locally compact Hausdorff space \(X \subseteq X^+\) (Theorem 18.9.3). The converse is the content of the next theorem.

03A5 Theorem 18.13.1

Let \(X\) be completely regular, \(\mathcal{F} = C(X, [0, 1])\) the set of continuous maps \(X \to [0, 1]\), and \(e \colon X \to [0, 1]^{\mathcal{F}}\) the map \(e(x) = (f(x))_{f \in \mathcal{F}}\). Then \(e\) is an embedding, that is, a homeomorphism onto its image. So the completely regular spaces are exactly the subspaces of compact Hausdorff spaces.

Proof â–¼

The components \(p_f \circ e = f\) are continuous, so \(e\) is continuous (Proposition 18.4.7(2)). It is injective: for \(x \neq y\), the set \(\{ y\} \) is closed, and some \(f\) has \(f(x) = 0 \neq 1 = f(y)\). Let \(U \subseteq X\) be open and \(x \in U\). Choose \(f \in \mathcal{F}\) with \(f(x) = 0\) and \(f = 1\) on \(X \setminus U\), and let \(W = p_f^{-1}([0, 1))\), open in the cube. Then \(e(x) \in W\), and if \(e(y) \in W\), then \(f(y) {\lt} 1\), so \(y \in U\). Hence \(e(U)\) is the union of the sets \(W \cap e(X)\) obtained in this way, so it is open in \(e(X)\). The cube is compact by Tychonoff’s theorem and Hausdorff by Proposition 18.4.7(3), and this proves the last statement.

03A6 Definition 18.13.2

For \(X\) completely regular, the Stone–Čech compactification \(\beta X\) is the closure of \(e(X)\) in \([0, 1]^{\mathcal{F}}\). We identify \(X\) with \(e(X)\), a dense subspace of \(\beta X\).

As a closed subset of a compact Hausdorff space, \(\beta X\) is compact Hausdorff (Proposition 18.6.3(1) and Proposition 18.4.7(3)).

03A7Lean Theorem 18.13.3

Let \(X\) be completely regular. Every continuous map \(g \colon X \to K\) to a compact Hausdorff space extends uniquely to a continuous map \(\beta g \colon \beta X \to K\), \(\beta g \circ e = g\).

Proof â–¼

Uniqueness. Let \(h_1, h_2 \colon \beta X \to K\) be continuous and agree on \(e(X)\). If \(h_1(z) \neq h_2(z)\), choose disjoint open \(V_1 \ni h_1(z)\) and \(V_2 \ni h_2(z)\); the open set \(h_1^{-1}(V_1) \cap h_2^{-1}(V_2)\) contains \(z\), so it meets the dense set \(e(X)\) in a point where \(h_1 = h_2\) takes values in \(V_1 \cap V_2 = \emptyset \), a contradiction.

Existence for cubes. If \(K = [0, 1]\), then \(g \in \mathcal{F}\), and \(\beta g = p_g|_{\beta X}\) works. If \(K = [0, 1]^J\) for a set \(J\), extend each component \(p_j \circ g\) and combine the extensions, which is continuous by Proposition 18.4.7(2).

General \(K\). A compact Hausdorff space is completely regular, so by Theorem 18.13.1 there is an embedding \(e_K \colon K \to Q = [0, 1]^{J}\), with \(J = C(K, [0, 1])\); its image is compact, hence closed in \(Q\). Let \(h \colon \beta X \to Q\) extend \(e_K \circ g\). By continuity \(h(\beta X) = h(\overline{e(X)}) \subseteq \overline{h(e(X))} = \overline{e_K(g(X))} \subseteq e_K(K)\) (Proposition 18.2.13(3)). So \(\beta g = e_K^{-1} \circ h\) is defined, and it is continuous, as \(e_K\) is a homeomorphism onto its image.

03A8 Corollary 18.13.4

Let \(X\) be completely regular, \(Y\) a compact Hausdorff space and \(j \colon X \to Y\) an embedding with dense image such that every continuous map from \(X\) to a compact Hausdorff space extends along \(j\). Then there is a unique homeomorphism \(\beta X \to Y\) that is the identity on \(X\), that is, sends \(e(x)\) to \(j(x)\).

Proof â–¼

Theorem 18.13.3 gives \(\beta j \colon \beta X \to Y\) with \(\beta j \circ e = j\), and the hypothesis gives \(k \colon Y \to \beta X\) with \(k \circ j = e\). Then \(k \circ \beta j\) is the identity on the dense set \(e(X)\), hence everywhere by the uniqueness argument in the proof of the theorem, and likewise \(\beta j \circ k\) is the identity on \(j(X)\), hence on \(Y\). Uniqueness of the homeomorphism is the same argument.

In categorical terms (Chapter 6), \(\beta \) is left adjoint to the inclusion of compact Hausdorff spaces into completely regular spaces. Taking \(K\) a compact interval, the theorem says that restriction is a bijection \(C(\beta X, \mathbb {R}) \to C_b(X, \mathbb {R})\), the bounded continuous functions on \(X\): every bounded continuous function extends uniquely.

03A9 Example 18.13.5

  1. If \(X\) is compact Hausdorff, \(e(X)\) is compact, hence closed, and \(\beta X = X\).

  2. The function \(s(x) = \sin (1/x)\) on \((0, 1]\) extends to \(\beta s \colon \beta (0, 1] \to [-1, 1]\) but not to \([0, 1]\); so \(\beta (0, 1] \neq [0, 1]\), and in fact \(\beta (0, 1] \setminus (0, 1]\) has more than one point. The sequence \(a_n = 1/(\frac\pi 2 + 2\pi n)\) has a cluster point \(z\) in the compact space \(\beta (0, 1]\) (Theorem 18.12.7), and \(z \notin (0, 1]\): otherwise \(z\) would be a cluster point of \((a_n)\) in the subspace \((0, 1]\), which has none, as \(a_n \to 0\). Since \(s(a_n) = 1\) for all \(n\), every neighbourhood of \(\beta s(z)\) contains \(1\) by continuity, so \(\beta s(z) = 1\). In the same way the points \(b_n = 1/(\frac{3\pi }2 + 2\pi n)\) give a point \(z^{\prime } \notin (0, 1]\) with \(\beta s(z^{\prime }) = -1\).

  3. \(\beta \mathbb {N}\) is not sequentially compact. Let \(\mathbb {N}\) be discrete, which is metric and so completely regular. Given \(n_1 {\lt} n_2 {\lt} \cdots \), let \(g \colon \mathbb {N}\to [0, 1]\) be \(1\) at \(n_k\) for odd \(k\) and \(0\) elsewhere. If \(n_k \to z\) in \(\beta \mathbb {N}\), then \(g(n_k) = \beta g(n_k) \to \beta g(z)\), which is impossible, as \(g(n_k)\) alternates between \(1\) and \(0\). So the sequence \((n)\) in the compact space \(\beta \mathbb {N}\) has no convergent subsequence.

03AA Remark 18.13.6

The space \(\beta \mathbb {N}\) is a basic object of set-theoretic topology. Its points correspond to the ultrafilters on \(\mathbb {N}\), the points of \(\mathbb {N}\) to the principal ones, and it has cardinality \(2^{2^{\aleph _0}}\); no sequence of distinct points of \(\beta \mathbb {N}\) converges [Eng89]. Under this correspondence the ultrafilter limits of Example 18.12.12 are the values at the point \(\mathcal{U} \in \beta \mathbb {N}\) of the continuous extensions of bounded sequences. In functional analysis \(\beta X\) appears as the space of characters of the Banach algebra \(C_b(X, \mathbb {C}) \cong C(\beta X, \mathbb {C})\), by Gelfand’s theory of commutative Banach algebras.
