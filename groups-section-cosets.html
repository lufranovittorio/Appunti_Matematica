6.3 Cosets and Lagrange’s theorem00C8

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

00C9 Definition 6.3.1

Let \(H \leq G\). A left coset of \(H\) is a set \(gH = \{ gh : h \in H\} \), and a right coset is a set \(Hg\). The set of left cosets is \(G/H\), and the index of \(H\) is \([G : H] = |G/H|\).

00CA Lemma 6.3.2

The left cosets of \(H\) are the classes of the equivalence relation \(a \sim b \iff a^{-1}b \in H\); hence they partition \(G\). Each left coset is in bijection with \(H\), and \(gH \mapsto Hg^{-1}\) is a bijection from left to right cosets.

Proof â–¼

The relation is an equivalence relation because \(H\) is a subgroup, and the class of \(a\) is \(aH\). The map \(h \mapsto gh\) is a bijection \(H \to gH\). Finally \(aH = bH\) iff \(a^{-1}b \in H\) iff \((a^{-1}b)^{-1} = b^{-1}a \in H\) iff \(Ha^{-1} = Hb^{-1}\).

00CB Theorem 6.3.3 Lagrange

Let \(G\) be finite and \(H \leq G\). Then \(|G| = [G : H] \cdot |H|\). In particular the order of \(H\) divides the order of \(G\), and the order of every element divides \(|G|\), so \(g^{|G|} = e\) for all \(g \in G\).

Proof â–¼

By Lemma 6.3.2, \(G\) is the disjoint union of \([G:H]\) sets of size \(|H|\). The subgroup \(\langle g \rangle \) has order \(\operatorname {ord}(g)\), since \(g^0, \ldots , g^{\operatorname {ord}(g)-1}\) are distinct and every power \(g^n\) equals \(g^r\) with \(r\) the remainder of \(n\).

00CC Corollary 6.3.4

A group of prime order \(p\) is cyclic, generated by any element \(\neq e\).

Proof â–¼

If \(g \neq e\), then \(\operatorname {ord}(g) {\gt} 1\) divides \(p\), so it equals \(p\), and \(\langle g \rangle = G\).

02DA Corollary 6.3.5 Euler, Fermat, Wilson

Let \(n \geq 2\), and write \(a \equiv b \pmod n\) if \(n \mid a - b\). Let \(U_n\) be the set of residue classes modulo \(n\) of the integers \(a\) with \(\gcd (a, n) = 1\), and \(\varphi (n) = |U_n|\) the number of \(0 \leq a {\lt} n\) with \(\gcd (a, n) = 1\).

  1. Multiplication of representatives makes \(U_n\) an abelian group, with identity the class of \(1\).

  2. (Euler) \(a^{\varphi (n)} \equiv 1 \pmod n\) whenever \(\gcd (a, n) = 1\).

  3. (Fermat) If \(p\) is prime and \(p \nmid a\), then \(a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p\); and \(a^p \equiv a \pmod p\) for every integer \(a\).

  4. (Wilson) If \(p\) is prime, then \((p - 1)! \equiv -1 \pmod p\).

Proof â–¼

(1) If \(a \equiv a'\) and \(b \equiv b'\), then \(ab - a'b' = a(b - b') + b'(a - a')\) is divisible by \(n\), so the product of classes is well defined; it is associative and commutative because multiplication of integers is. If \(\gcd (a, n) = \gcd (b, n) = 1\) then \(\gcd (ab, n) = 1\): a prime dividing \(n\) and \(ab\) divides \(a\) or \(b\) (Lemma 4.5.4). By Bézout (Theorem 4.5.3), \(\gcd (a, n) = 1\) gives \(ax + ny = 1\), so the class of \(x\), which is again coprime to \(n\), is an inverse of the class of \(a\).

(2) By Theorem 6.3.3 applied to the group \(U_n\) of order \(\varphi (n)\).

(3) For \(n = p\) prime, every \(a\) with \(0 {\lt} a {\lt} p\) is coprime to \(p\), so \(\varphi (p) = p - 1\) and (2) applies. Multiplying by \(a\) gives \(a^p \equiv a\), which also holds trivially when \(p \mid a\).

(4) An element \(x\) of \(U_p\) is its own inverse exactly when \(p \mid x^2 - 1 = (x - 1)(x + 1)\), that is, by Lemma 4.5.4, when \(x \equiv \pm 1\). The remaining elements of \(U_p\) fall into pairs \(\{ x, x^{-1}\} \) with product \(1\). So the product of all elements of \(U_p\), which is the class of \((p - 1)!\), is the class of \(1 \cdot (-1) = -1\) (for \(p = 2\), \(1 \equiv -1\) and the statement is clear).

02DB Example 6.3.6

\(\varphi (12) = 4\), with \(U_{12} = \{ 1, 5, 7, 11\} \), and every element squares to \(1\); so \(U_{12}\) is not cyclic, although \(U_{12}\) has order \(4\) and \(U_5 = \{ 1, 2, 3, 4\} \), also of order \(4\), is cyclic, generated by \(2\) (\(2^2 = 4\), \(2^3 = 8 \equiv 3\), \(2^4 = 16 \equiv 1\)). Fermat’s little theorem gives, for instance, \(2^{100} = (2^{10})^{10} \equiv 1 \pmod{11}\). Its converse fails: \(561 = 3 \cdot 11 \cdot 17\) is not prime, yet \(a^{560} \equiv 1 \pmod{561}\) for all \(a\) coprime to \(561\), because \(2\), \(10\) and \(16\) divide \(560\), so Fermat’s theorem gives the congruence modulo \(3\), \(11\) and \(17\), hence modulo their product, the three primes being distinct (Theorem 4.5.5). Such numbers are called Carmichael numbers.

00CD Proposition 6.3.7

If \(K \leq H \leq G\) and the indices are finite, then \([G : K] = [G : H][H : K]\).

Proof â–¼

Let \((g_i)\) represent the left cosets of \(H\) in \(G\) and \((h_j)\) those of \(K\) in \(H\). Then the \(g_i h_j K\) are the left cosets of \(K\) in \(G\), and they are distinct: \(G = \bigsqcup _i g_i H = \bigsqcup _i \bigsqcup _j g_i h_j K\).
