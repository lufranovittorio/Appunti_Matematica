64.4 Examples01VU

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

01VV Proposition 64.4.1

Let \(E\) be a complex elliptic curve and \(H = H^1(E; \mathbb {Q})\). If \(E\) has complex multiplication by the imaginary quadratic field \(K\), then \(\mathrm{MT}(H) = \mathrm{Res}_{K/\mathbb {Q}}\mathbb {G}_m\), a torus of dimension \(2\), and \(\mathrm{Hg}(H)\) is the norm-one subtorus. If \(E\) has no complex multiplication, then \(\mathrm{MT}(H) = \mathrm{GL}_2\) and \(\mathrm{Hg}(H) = \mathrm{SL}_2\).

Proof â–¼

By Corollary 64.2.4 and Theorem 63.7.1, the commutant of \(\mathrm{MT}(H)\) in \(\operatorname {End}(H_\mathbb {Q})\) is \(\operatorname {End}(E) \otimes \mathbb {Q}\). In the CM case, \(K\) acts on the \(2\)-dimensional space \(H_\mathbb {Q}\), making it a \(1\)-dimensional \(K\)-vector space, so the commutant of \(K\) in \(\mathrm{GL}(H_\mathbb {Q})\) is the torus \(T_K = \mathrm{Res}_{K/\mathbb {Q}}\mathbb {G}_m\), and \(\mathrm{MT}(H) \subseteq T_K\). It contains the scalars (weight \(1 \neq 0\)) and the Weil operator \(C = h(i)^{-1}\), which acts as \(i\) on \(H^{1,0}\) and \(-i\) on \(H^{0,1}\) and is not a scalar. So \(\dim \mathrm{MT}(H) = 2\) and \(\mathrm{MT}(H) = T_K\). Without CM, the commutant is \(\mathbb {Q}\). The group \(\mathrm{MT}(H)\) is connected and reductive (Theorem 64.3.1). A connected reductive subgroup of \(\mathrm{GL}_2\) is a torus or contains \(\mathrm{SL}_2\). A torus of \(\mathrm{GL}_2\) containing a non-scalar element has as commutant a \(2\)-dimensional commutative algebra, not \(\mathbb {Q}\). So \(\mathrm{MT}(H) \supseteq \mathrm{SL}_2\), and with the scalars, \(\mathrm{MT}(H) = \mathrm{GL}_2\). The Hodge groups follow from Proposition 64.2.2(2) and the fact that \(\mathrm{Hg}\) preserves the polarisation (it fixes the Hodge class \(Q\)), so lies in \(\mathrm{SL}_2 = \mathrm{Sp}_2\).

01VW Proposition 64.4.2

If \(A\) is an abelian variety with complex multiplication by a CM field \(F\) of degree \(2g\), then \(\mathrm{MT}(H^1(A; \mathbb {Q}))\) is a torus, contained in \(\mathrm{Res}_{F/\mathbb {Q}}\mathbb {G}_m\). Conversely, a polarisable weight-one Hodge structure whose Mumford–Tate group is a torus is of CM type.

Proof â–¼

\(F\) acts on \(H_\mathbb {Q}\) by Hodge endomorphisms, and \(\dim _\mathbb {Q}H_\mathbb {Q}= 2g = [F : \mathbb {Q}]\), so \(H_\mathbb {Q}\) is a \(1\)-dimensional \(F\)-vector space. Its commutant in \(\mathrm{GL}(H_\mathbb {Q})\) is \(\mathrm{Res}_{F/\mathbb {Q}}\mathbb {G}_m\), a torus, which contains \(\mathrm{MT}\) by Corollary 64.2.4; subgroups of tori that are connected are tori. Conversely, if \(\mathrm{MT}\) is a torus \(T\), its commutant contains the \(\mathbb {Q}\)-algebra generated by \(T(\mathbb {Q})\), which is commutative semisimple; one shows that it contains a maximal commutative subalgebra of dimension \(2g\) [BL04].

01VX Theorem 64.4.3

For a very general principally polarised abelian variety \(A\) of dimension \(g\) (outside a countable union of proper analytic subsets of the moduli space), \(\mathrm{MT}(H^1(A)) = \mathrm{GSp}_{2g}\) and \(\mathrm{Hg}(H^1(A)) = \mathrm{Sp}_{2g}\). Consequently \(\mathrm{Hdg}^\bullet (A^r)\) is generated by divisor classes for every \(r\), and the Hodge conjecture holds for all powers of \(A\).

Sketch of proof â–¼

For each of the countably many \(\mathbb {Q}\)-subgroups \(G \subsetneq \mathrm{GSp}_{2g}\), the set of points of the Siegel space \(\mathfrak {H}_g\) (Proposition 58.3.4) where \(h\) factors through \(G(\mathbb {R})\) is a proper analytic subset, since \(\mathrm{GSp}_{2g}(\mathbb {R})\) acts transitively on \(\mathfrak {H}_g\) and the \(h\) of a general point generates. Outside these subsets \(\mathrm{MT} = \mathrm{GSp}_{2g}\). By classical invariant theory for \(\mathrm{Sp}_{2g}\), the invariants in \(\Lambda ^\bullet (H^{\oplus r})\) are generated by the symplectic forms pairing the various copies, which are the classes of divisors [BL04]. By Corollary 64.3.3 these are all the Hodge classes. Rational Hodge classes of degree \(2\) are classes of divisors: a multiple lifts to an integral class of type \((1,1)\), which is \(c_1\) of a line bundle (Lemma 48.2.2), algebraic by GAGA (Theorem 55.4.1), whose class is that of a divisor (Example 60.3.2(2)).

01VY Remark 64.4.4

The Mumford–Tate group is not determined by the endomorphism algebra. Mumford constructed abelian fourfolds with \(\operatorname {End}(A) = \mathbb {Z}\) whose Mumford–Tate group is much smaller than \(\mathrm{GSp}_8\): a form of \(\mathbb {G}_m \cdot (\mathrm{SL}_2)^3\) acting through the tensor product of three standard representations [Mum69]. For such \(A\) the ring of Hodge classes is not detected by endomorphisms.

01VZ Remark 64.4.5

For \(A\) defined over a number field \(k\), the Mumford–Tate conjecture predicts that the identity component of the Zariski closure of the image of \(G_k\) in \(\mathrm{GL}(H^1(\bar A, \mathbb {Q}_\ell ))\) equals \(\mathrm{MT}(H^1(A; \mathbb {Q})) \otimes \mathbb {Q}_\ell \), under Artin’s comparison isomorphism (Theorem 62.4.3). It relates the Hodge conjecture to the Tate conjecture (Chapter 73), and it is known for elliptic curves (Serre) and in many cases for abelian varieties.
