39.8 The first and second variation of energy02KA

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

Geodesics are the critical points of the energy of curves, and the second derivative of the energy along a geodesic is a quadratic form, the index form, in which the curvature appears. This section proves both variation formulas and uses the second one to show that geodesics stop minimising after a conjugate point (Jacobi’s theorem) and that positive Ricci curvature bounds the diameter (the Bonnet–Myers theorem).

Let \(\gamma \colon [a, b] \to M\) be a piecewise smooth curve. A variation of \(\gamma \) is a continuous map \(f \colon (-\varepsilon , \varepsilon ) \times [a, b] \to M\) with \(f(0, t) = \gamma (t)\), together with a partition \(a = t_0 {\lt} t_1 {\lt} \cdots {\lt} t_k = b\) such that \(f\) is smooth on each \((-\varepsilon , \varepsilon ) \times [t_{i-1}, t_i]\); it is proper if \(f(s, a) = \gamma (a)\) and \(f(s, b) = \gamma (b)\) for all \(s\). We write \(f_s = f(s, -)\), and \(V(t) = \partial _sf(0, t)\) for the variation field, a continuous vector field along \(\gamma \), smooth on each \([t_{i-1}, t_i]\) (piecewise smooth); it vanishes at \(a\) and \(b\) if \(f\) is proper. The energy of a piecewise smooth curve \(c \colon [a, b] \to M\) is \(E(c) = \frac12\int _a^b|\dot c|^2\, dt\). On each piece we use the covariant derivatives \(D_s\) and \(D_t\) of Lemma 39.4.3.

Every piecewise smooth field \(V\) along \(\gamma \) is the variation field of a variation, for instance of

\[ f(s, t) = \exp _{\gamma (t)}(sV(t)) . \]

Indeed the set of \((s, t)\) with \(sV(t)\) in the domain \(\mathcal{E}\) of \(\exp \) is open and contains \(\{ 0\} \times [a, b]\), so by compactness it contains \((-\varepsilon , \varepsilon ) \times [a, b]\) for some \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\); and \(\partial _sf(0, t) = d_0\exp _{\gamma (t)}(V(t)) = V(t)\). This variation is proper if \(V(a) = V(b) = 0\), and its curves \(s \mapsto f(s, t)\) are geodesics, so \(D_s\partial _sf = 0\).

02KB Lemma 39.8.1

For a piecewise smooth curve \(c \colon [a, b] \to M\), \(L(c)^2 \leq 2(b - a)E(c)\), with equality if and only if \(c\) has constant speed. Consequently, if \(\gamma \) has constant speed and \(E(c) {\lt} E(\gamma )\) for a curve \(c\) with the same interval, then \(L(c) {\lt} L(\gamma )\).

Proof â–¼

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, \(L(c)^2 = \bigl(\int _a^b1 \cdot |\dot c|\, dt\bigr)^2 \leq (b - a)\int _a^b|\dot c|^2\, dt = 2(b - a)E(c)\), with equality if and only if \(|\dot c|\) is constant. If \(\gamma \) has constant speed and \(E(c) {\lt} E(\gamma )\), then \(L(c)^2 \leq 2(b - a)E(c) {\lt} 2(b - a)E(\gamma ) = L(\gamma )^2\).

02KC Proposition 39.8.2 First variation formula

Let \(f\) be a variation of a piecewise smooth curve \(\gamma \), with variation field \(V\), and suppose that \(\gamma \) is smooth on each \([t_{i-1}, t_i]\). Write \(\Delta _i\dot\gamma = \dot\gamma (t_i^+) - \dot\gamma (t_i^-)\) for the jump of the velocity at \(t_i\), \(0 {\lt} i {\lt} k\). Then \(s \mapsto E(f_s)\) is smooth, and

\[ \frac{d}{ds}\Big|_{s=0}E(f_s) = \langle V, \dot\gamma \rangle \Big|_a^b - \sum _{i=1}^{k-1}\langle V(t_i), \Delta _i\dot\gamma \rangle - \int _a^b\langle V, D_t\dot\gamma \rangle \, dt . \]

Consequently \(\gamma \) is a critical point of the energy for all proper variations, that is, \(\frac{d}{ds}|_{s=0}E(f_s) = 0\) for every proper \(f\), if and only if \(\gamma \) is a geodesic.

Proof â–¼

On each piece the integrand of \(E(f_s) = \sum _i\frac12\int _{t_{i-1}}^{t_i}|\partial _tf|^2\, dt\) is smooth on a compact set, so we may differentiate under the integral sign. By Proposition 39.4.1 and Lemma 39.4.3,

\[ \frac{\partial }{\partial s}\frac12|\partial _tf|^2 = \langle D_s\partial _tf, \partial _tf\rangle = \langle D_t\partial _sf, \partial _tf\rangle = \frac{\partial }{\partial t}\langle \partial _sf, \partial _tf\rangle - \langle \partial _sf, D_t\partial _tf\rangle . \]

At \(s = 0\) this is \(\partial _t\langle V, \dot\gamma \rangle - \langle V, D_t\dot\gamma \rangle \). Integrating over \([t_{i-1}, t_i]\) gives \(\langle V(t_i), \dot\gamma (t_i^-)\rangle - \langle V(t_{i-1}), \dot\gamma (t_{i-1}^+)\rangle - \int _{t_{i-1}}^{t_i}\langle V, D_t\dot\gamma \rangle \, dt\), and summing over \(i\) gives the formula, since \(V\) is continuous.

If \(\gamma \) is a geodesic, it is smooth, so there are no jumps, \(D_t\dot\gamma = 0\), and \(V(a) = V(b) = 0\) for proper variations; so the derivative vanishes. Conversely, suppose it vanishes for all proper variations. The function \(\phi (t) = \prod _i(t - t_i)^2\) is smooth, positive except at the \(t_i\), and \(V = \phi \, D_t\dot\gamma \) is a piecewise smooth field vanishing at all the \(t_i\); for the variation \(\exp _{\gamma (t)}(sV(t))\) the formula gives \(0 = -\int _a^b\phi |D_t\dot\gamma |^2\, dt\), so \(D_t\dot\gamma = 0\) on each piece. Next choose a piecewise smooth \(V\) with \(V(a) = V(b) = 0\) and \(V(t_i) = \Delta _i\dot\gamma \), for instance a sum of products of bump functions with parallel fields along \(\gamma \); then \(0 = -\sum _i|\Delta _i\dot\gamma |^2\). So \(\dot\gamma \) is continuous, and \(\gamma \) is a geodesic on each piece whose velocities match at the \(t_i\); by uniqueness of geodesics with given initial velocity, \(\gamma \) is a single geodesic.

In particular a curve of constant speed that minimises length between its endpoints minimises energy (Lemma 39.8.1), hence is a geodesic, which is Lemma 39.5.6 again.

02KD Definition 39.8.3

Let \(\gamma \colon [a, b] \to M\) be a geodesic. The index form of \(\gamma \) is the symmetric bilinear form on piecewise smooth vector fields along \(\gamma \)

\[ I(V, W) = \int _a^b\bigl(\langle D_tV, D_tW\rangle - R(V, \dot\gamma , \dot\gamma , W)\bigr)\, dt . \]

It is symmetric because \(R(V, \dot\gamma , \dot\gamma , W) = R(\dot\gamma , W, V, \dot\gamma ) = R(W, \dot\gamma , \dot\gamma , V)\) by Proposition 39.7.2. If \(V\) is smooth on the pieces \([t_{i-1}, t_i]\), integrating \(\frac{d}{dt}\langle D_tV, W\rangle = \langle D_t^2V, W\rangle + \langle D_tV, D_tW\rangle \) over each piece gives

\[ I(V, W) = \langle D_tV, W\rangle \Big|_a^b - \sum _{i=1}^{k-1}\langle \Delta _iD_tV, W(t_i)\rangle - \int _a^b\langle D_t^2V + R(V, \dot\gamma )\dot\gamma , W\rangle \, dt, \tag {$\ddagger $} \]

with \(\Delta _iD_tV = D_tV(t_i^+) - D_tV(t_i^-)\). In particular \(I(J, W) = 0\) for a Jacobi field \(J\) and every \(W\) vanishing at \(a\) and \(b\).

02KE Proposition 39.8.4 Second variation formula

Let \(\gamma \colon [a, b] \to M\) be a geodesic and \(f\) a variation of \(\gamma \) with variation field \(V\), and let \(A(t) = D_s\partial _sf(0, t)\), the acceleration at \(s = 0\) of the curve \(s \mapsto f(s, t)\). Then

\[ \frac{d^2}{ds^2}\Big|_{s=0}E(f_s) = I(V, V) + \langle A, \dot\gamma \rangle \Big|_a^b = \int _a^b\bigl(|D_tV|^2 - R(V, \dot\gamma , \dot\gamma , V)\bigr)\, dt + \langle A, \dot\gamma \rangle \Big|_a^b . \]

The boundary term vanishes if \(f\) is proper, and also if the curves \(s \mapsto f(s, a)\) and \(s \mapsto f(s, b)\) are geodesics, for instance for \(f(s, t) = \exp _{\gamma (t)}(sV(t))\).

Proof â–¼

As in the proof of Proposition 39.8.2, on each piece \(\frac{d}{ds}E = \int \langle D_t\partial _sf, \partial _tf\rangle \, dt\), and differentiating again,

\[ \frac{d^2}{ds^2}E(f_s) = \sum _i\int _{t_{i-1}}^{t_i}\bigl(\langle D_sD_t\partial _sf, \partial _tf\rangle + \langle D_t\partial _sf, D_s\partial _tf\rangle \bigr)\, dt . \]

At \(s = 0\), \(D_s\partial _tf = D_t\partial _sf = D_tV\), so the second term is \(|D_tV|^2\). By Lemma 39.7.4, \(D_sD_t\partial _sf = D_tD_s\partial _sf + R(\partial _sf, \partial _tf)\partial _sf\), and at \(s = 0\), using \(D_t\dot\gamma = 0\),

\[ \langle D_sD_t\partial _sf, \dot\gamma \rangle = \frac{\partial }{\partial t}\langle D_s\partial _sf, \dot\gamma \rangle + R(V, \dot\gamma , V, \dot\gamma ) = \frac{\partial }{\partial t}\langle D_s\partial _sf, \dot\gamma \rangle - R(V, \dot\gamma , \dot\gamma , V). \]

The field \(A\) at \(t_i\) is the acceleration of the curve \(s \mapsto f(s, t_i)\), which is the same curve for both pieces meeting at \(t_i\); so \(A\) is continuous, and so is \(\dot\gamma \). Hence the integrals of \(\partial _t\langle A, \dot\gamma \rangle \) add up to \(\langle A, \dot\gamma \rangle |_a^b\). If \(f\) is proper, the curves \(s \mapsto f(s, a)\) and \(s \mapsto f(s, b)\) are constant, so \(A(a) = A(b) = 0\); if they are geodesics, \(A(a) = A(b) = 0\) by Proposition 39.4.4.

02KF Theorem 39.8.5 Jacobi

Let \(\gamma \colon [0, L] \to M\) be a geodesic, and suppose that \(\gamma (t_0)\) is conjugate to \(\gamma (0)\) along \(\gamma \) for some \(0 {\lt} t_0 {\lt} L\). Then there are piecewise smooth curves from \(\gamma (0)\) to \(\gamma (L)\), arbitrarily close to \(\gamma \), that are shorter than \(\gamma \). In particular \(\gamma \) is not a minimising geodesic.

Proof â–¼

Let \(J\) be a nonzero Jacobi field on \([0, t_0]\) with \(J(0) = J(t_0) = 0\). Then \(D_tJ(t_0) \neq 0\), as a Jacobi field is determined by its value and derivative at one point. Let \(V = J\) on \([0, t_0]\) and \(V = 0\) on \([t_0, L]\), a piecewise smooth field vanishing at \(0\) and \(L\). In \((\ddagger )\) for \(V\) and a piecewise smooth \(U\) vanishing at \(0\) and \(L\), the integral vanishes, because \(J\) satisfies the Jacobi equation on \([0, t_0]\) and \(V = 0\) on \([t_0, L]\), and the only remaining term comes from the jump \(\Delta D_tV = -D_tJ(t_0)\) at \(t_0\); so \(I(V, U) = \langle D_tJ(t_0), U(t_0)\rangle \). In particular \(I(V, V) = \langle D_tJ(t_0), J(t_0)\rangle = 0\). Let \(P\) be the parallel field along \(\gamma \) with \(P(t_0) = -D_tJ(t_0)\), and \(W = \sin (\pi t/L)P/\sin (\pi t_0/L)\), which vanishes at \(0\) and \(L\) and equals \(-D_tJ(t_0)\) at \(t_0\). Then \(I(V, W) = -|D_tJ(t_0)|^2 {\lt} 0\). Hence for small \(\delta {\gt} 0\),

\[ I(V + \delta W, V + \delta W) = 2\delta I(V, W) + \delta ^2I(W, W) {\lt} 0 . \]

The proper variation \(f(s, t) = \exp _{\gamma (t)}(s(V + \delta W)(t))\) has \(\frac{d}{ds}E(f_s) = 0\) at \(s = 0\) (Proposition 39.8.2) and \(\frac{d^2}{ds^2}E(f_s) {\lt} 0\) at \(s = 0\) (Proposition 39.8.4), so \(E(f_s) {\lt} E(\gamma )\) for all small \(s \neq 0\), and \(L(f_s) {\lt} L(\gamma )\) by Lemma 39.8.1, as the geodesic \(\gamma \) has constant speed.

For example, a great circle on the unit sphere \(S^n\) starting at \(p\) meets the conjugate point \(-p\) at time \(\pi \) (Example 39.7.7), and so it stops minimising after time \(\pi \), in accordance with Remark 39.5.9.

028A Theorem 39.8.6 Bonnet–Myers

Let \((M, g)\) be complete and connected with \(\mathrm{Ric} \geq (n - 1)\kappa g\) for some \(\kappa {\gt} 0\). Then \(M\) has diameter at most \(\pi /\sqrt\kappa \); hence \(M\) is compact, and \(\pi _1(M)\) is finite.

Proof â–¼

Let \(p, q \in M\) and \(L = d(p, q)\), and suppose \(L {\gt} \pi /\sqrt\kappa \). By Theorem 39.5.7 there is a minimising geodesic \(\gamma \colon [0, L] \to M\) of unit speed from \(p\) to \(q\). Let \(E_1, \ldots , E_{n-1}\) be parallel fields along \(\gamma \) that together with \(\dot\gamma \) form an orthonormal basis at one point, hence at every point (Proposition 39.4.4), and put \(V_i(t) = \sin (\pi t/L)E_i(t)\). Then \(D_tV_i = (\pi /L)\cos (\pi t/L)E_i\), and since \(\sum _iR(E_i, \dot\gamma , \dot\gamma , E_i) = \mathrm{Ric}(\dot\gamma , \dot\gamma ) \geq (n - 1)\kappa \) (Definition 39.7.1),

\[ \sum _{i=1}^{n-1}I(V_i, V_i) = \int _0^L\Bigl((n - 1)\frac{\pi ^2}{L^2}\cos ^2\frac{\pi t}{L} - \sin ^2\frac{\pi t}{L}\, \mathrm{Ric}(\dot\gamma , \dot\gamma )\Bigr)dt \leq (n - 1)\frac L2\Bigl(\frac{\pi ^2}{L^2} - \kappa \Bigr) {\lt} 0, \]

using \(\int _0^L\cos ^2(\pi t/L)\, dt = \int _0^L\sin ^2(\pi t/L)\, dt = L/2\). So \(I(V_i, V_i) {\lt} 0\) for some \(i\), and as in the proof of Theorem 39.8.5 the proper variation \(\exp _{\gamma (t)}(sV_i(t))\) gives curves from \(p\) to \(q\) shorter than \(L = d(p, q)\), which is absurd. Hence every two points have distance at most \(\pi /\sqrt\kappa \), and \(M\), being closed and bounded in itself, is compact by Theorem 39.5.7.

For the fundamental group, let \(\pi \colon \widetilde{M} \to M\) be a universal covering (Theorem 18.6.5). It is a manifold (Proposition 19.6.1(4)) with a unique smooth structure making \(\pi \) a local diffeomorphism (Example 32.7.2), and \(\tilde g = \pi ^*g\) makes \(\pi \) a local isometry, so \(\widetilde{M}\) satisfies the same bound on the Ricci curvature. In charts \(y\) of \(M\) and \(y \circ \pi \) of \(\widetilde{M}\) the metrics have the same coefficients, hence the same geodesic equations; so \(\pi \) and its local inverses map geodesics to geodesics. Consequently \(\widetilde{M}\) is complete: if \(\tilde\gamma \) is a geodesic of \(\widetilde{M}\), the geodesic \(\pi \circ \tilde\gamma \) extends to \(\mathbb {R}\) by completeness of \(M\), its lift through \(\tilde\gamma (0)\) (Proposition 18.4.3) is locally a geodesic followed by a local inverse of \(\pi \), hence a geodesic, and it extends \(\tilde\gamma \) to \(\mathbb {R}\). By the first part \(\widetilde{M}\) is compact, so the fibre \(\pi ^{-1}(p)\), which is closed and discrete, is finite. By Corollary 18.4.4(3), \(\pi _1(M, p)\) acts on this fibre transitively with trivial stabiliser, since \(\widetilde{M}\) is simply connected; so \(\pi _1(M, p)\) is finite.

For example the round sphere of radius \(1/\sqrt\kappa \) has \(\mathrm{Ric} = (n - 1)\kappa g\) and diameter exactly \(\pi /\sqrt\kappa \), so the bound is sharp; Cheng’s theorem (Theorem 39.13.15) says that it is the only case of equality. The hypothesis cannot be weakened to \(\mathrm{Ric} {\gt} 0\): the paraboloid \(z = x^2 + y^2\) is complete, being closed in \(\mathbb {R}^3\), and has Gaussian curvature \(4/(1 + 4x^2 + 4y^2)^2 {\gt} 0\) (Section 39.9), but it is not compact; its curvature tends to \(0\) at infinity.

The second variation formula also controls the topology of compact manifolds of positive curvature through their isometries. The key step is a fixed point theorem.

02KR Theorem 39.8.7 Weinstein

Let \(M\) be a compact connected oriented Riemannian manifold of dimension \(n\) with positive sectional curvature, and \(F\) an isometry of \(M\) that preserves the orientation if \(n\) is even and reverses it if \(n\) is odd. Then \(F\) has a fixed point.

Proof â–¼

Suppose not. The function \(x \mapsto d(x, F(x))\) is continuous and positive on the compact \(M\); let \(x_0\) be a minimum point, \(L = d(x_0, F(x_0)) {\gt} 0\), and \(\gamma \colon [0, L] \to M\) a minimising geodesic of unit speed from \(x_0\) to \(F(x_0)\) (Theorem 39.5.7). An isometry maps geodesics to geodesics, since in charts \(y\) and \(y \circ F\) the metric has the same coefficients (as in the proof of Theorem 39.8.6), and it preserves lengths of curves, hence distances.

Step 1: \(d_{x_0}F(\dot\gamma (0)) = \dot\gamma (L)\). Let \(m = \gamma (L/2)\). Then \(d(m, F(m)) \leq d(m, F(x_0)) + d(F(x_0), F(m)) = L/2 + d(x_0, m) = L\), and \(d(m, F(m)) \geq L\) by the choice of \(x_0\). So the curve that follows \(\gamma \) from \(m\) to \(F(x_0)\) and then \(F \circ \gamma \) from \(F(x_0)\) to \(F(m)\) has unit speed and length \(d(m, F(m))\); by Lemma 39.5.6 it is a geodesic, hence smooth at \(F(x_0)\), where its two velocities are \(\dot\gamma (L)\) and \(d_{x_0}F(\dot\gamma (0))\).

Step 2: a closed-up parallel field. Let \(P \colon T_{x_0}M \to T_{F(x_0)}M\) be parallel transport along \(\gamma \). It is a linear isometry (Proposition 39.4.4) and preserves orientations, as in the proof of Proposition 39.6.1(4). So \(B = P^{-1} \circ d_{x_0}F\) is an orthogonal map of \(T_{x_0}M\) with \(\det B = 1\) if \(F\) preserves the orientation and \(\det B = -1\) if it reverses it; in both cases of the theorem \(\det B = (-1)^n\). As \(\dot\gamma \) is parallel, \(P(\dot\gamma (0)) = \dot\gamma (L)\), so \(B\) fixes \(\dot\gamma (0)\) by Step 1, and preserves the orthogonal complement \(E\) of \(\dot\gamma (0)\), of dimension \(m = n - 1\), with \(\det (B|_E) = (-1)^{m+1}\). An orthogonal map \(C\) of an \(m\)-dimensional space with \(\det C = (-1)^{m+1}\) has the eigenvalue \(1\): \(\det (C - I) = \det C\, \det (I - C^T) = \det C\, \det (I - C) = (-1)^{m+1}(-1)^m\det (C - I) = -\det (C - I)\). So there is a unit vector \(v \in E\) with \(Bv = v\). Let \(V\) be the parallel field along \(\gamma \) with \(V(0) = v\); then \(|V| = 1\), \(V \perp \dot\gamma \), and \(V(L) = Pv = d_{x_0}F(v)\).

Step 3: a shorter curve. Let \(f(s, t) = \exp _{\gamma (t)}(sV(t))\) and \(x_s = \exp _{x_0}(sv)\). Then \(f(s, 0) = x_s\) and, since \(F\) maps geodesics to geodesics, \(f(s, L) = \exp _{F(x_0)}(s\, d_{x_0}F(v)) = F(x_s)\). By Proposition 39.8.2 the first derivative of \(E(f_s)\) at \(s = 0\) is \(\langle V, \dot\gamma \rangle |_0^L = 0\). By Proposition 39.8.4, whose boundary term vanishes because the curves \(s \mapsto f(s, t)\) are geodesics, and since \(D_tV = 0\),

\[ \frac{d^2}{ds^2}\Big|_{s=0}E(f_s) = -\int _0^LR(V, \dot\gamma , \dot\gamma , V)\, dt {\lt} 0, \]

the integrand being the sectional curvature of the plane spanned by the orthonormal vectors \(V\) and \(\dot\gamma \). So \(E(f_s) {\lt} E(\gamma )\) for small \(s \neq 0\), and \(L(f_s) {\lt} L\) by Lemma 39.8.1. But \(f_s\) joins \(x_s\) to \(F(x_s)\), so \(L(f_s) \geq d(x_s, F(x_s)) \geq L\), a contradiction.

02KS Corollary 39.8.8 Synge

Let \(M\) be a compact connected Riemannian manifold with positive sectional curvature.

  1. If \(n\) is even and \(M\) is orientable, then \(M\) is simply connected.

  2. If \(n\) is odd, then \(M\) is orientable.

Proof â–¼

(1) The sectional curvature \(K(u, v) = R(u, v, v, u)\) is continuous and positive on the compact set of orthonormal pairs of tangent vectors, so \(K \geq k\) for some \(k {\gt} 0\), and \(\mathrm{Ric} \geq (n - 1)kg\). Let \(\pi \colon \widetilde{M} \to M\) be a universal covering with the metric \(\pi ^*g\). As in the proof of Theorem 39.8.6, \(\widetilde{M}\) is complete, hence compact by that theorem, and it has positive sectional curvature. Orient \(\widetilde{M}\) by pulling back a volume form \(\mu \) of \(M\) by the local diffeomorphism \(\pi \). A deck transformation \(\tau \) satisfies \(\pi \circ \tau = \pi \), so it is an isometry and \(\tau ^*\pi ^*\mu = \pi ^*\mu \): it preserves the orientation. By Theorem 39.8.7, \(\tau \) has a fixed point, so it is the identity, by the uniqueness of lifts (Proposition 18.6.1). Hence \(\mathrm{Deck}(\pi )\) is trivial, and so is \(\pi _1(M) \cong \mathrm{Deck}(\pi )\) (Proposition 18.6.3).

(2) Suppose \(M\) is not orientable, and let \(p \colon \widehat{M} \to M\) be the orientation double covering (Definition 22.3.1), which is connected by Proposition 22.3.2(1). It is compact, a smooth manifold for which \(p\) is a local diffeomorphism (Example 32.7.2), and it has positive sectional curvature for \(p^*g\). It is orientable: at a point \((x, \alpha )\), \(p\) identifies the local homology of \(\widehat{M}\) with \(H_n(M \mid x; \mathbb {Z})\) (a sheet over a coordinate ball is mapped homeomorphically), and \((x, \alpha ) \mapsto \alpha \) is a continuous choice of generators, that is an orientation (Proposition 35.3.2). The deck transformation \(\tau (x, \alpha ) = (x, -\alpha )\) is an isometry of \(p^*g\) without fixed points, and it reverses this orientation, because \(p \circ \tau = p\) carries the generator \(\alpha \) at \((x, \alpha )\) to \(\alpha \) at \((x, -\alpha )\), where the chosen generator is \(-\alpha \). By Theorem 39.8.7 with \(n\) odd, \(\tau \) has a fixed point, a contradiction.

02KT Counterexample 39.8.9

Every hypothesis of Theorem 39.8.7 and Corollary 39.8.8 is needed.

  1. The parity conditions in Weinstein’s theorem. The antipodal map \(x \mapsto -x\) of the unit sphere \(S^n\) is an isometry without fixed points. It is the restriction of \(-I\), of determinant \((-1)^{n+1}\), so it preserves the orientation of \(S^n\) when \(n\) is odd and reverses it when \(n\) is even: exactly the cases excluded by the theorem.

  2. Orientability in (1). \(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^n = S^n/\{ \pm 1\} \) with the round metric has \(K = 1\) and \(\pi _1 = \mathbb {Z}/2\). For \(n\) even it is not orientable (Exercise 35.5.2).

  3. Even dimension in (1). For \(n\) odd, \(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^n\) is orientable with \(K = 1\) and \(\pi _1 = \mathbb {Z}/2\); so are the lens spaces \(S^3/(\mathbb {Z}/p)\) (Example 19.4.5), with \(\pi _1 = \mathbb {Z}/p\), as the group acts by unitary, hence orthogonal, maps.

  4. Odd dimension in (2). \(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^2\) has \(K = 1\) and is not orientable.

  5. Positive curvature, not just \(K \geq 0\). The flat torus \(\mathbb {R}^2/\mathbb {Z}^2\) is compact and orientable of even dimension, with \(\pi _1 = \mathbb {Z}^2\). The product \(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^2 \times S^1\) of the round \(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^2\) and a circle is compact of dimension \(3\) with \(K \geq 0\) (the curvature tensor of a Riemannian product is the sum of those of the factors, and the circle is flat), and it is not orientable: by Exercise 39.14.6 its holonomy contains \(h \times 1\) for \(h \in \mathrm{Hol}(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^2)\) with \(\det h = -1\), which exists by Proposition 39.6.1(4).

  6. Compactness in (1). The sphere minus two antipodal points, \(S^2 \setminus \{ \pm e_3\} \), has \(K = 1\) and is orientable of even dimension, but by stereographic projection it is homeomorphic to \(\mathbb {R}^2 \setminus \{ 0\} \), with \(\pi _1 = \mathbb {Z}\).

  7. Compactness in (2). Let \(A = \mathrm{diag}(-1, -1, -1, 1)\) acting on \(U = S^3 \setminus \{ \pm e_4\} \). Its fixed points on \(S^3\) are \(\pm e_4\), so the group \(\{ 1, A\} \) acts freely, and properly as it is finite, by isometries; \(\det A = -1\), so \(A\) reverses the orientation of \(S^3\) (defined by the outward normal, which \(A\) preserves). The quotient \(U/\{ 1, A\} \) has \(K = 1\) (Proposition 39.2.4) and dimension \(3\), and it is not orientable: an orientation would pull back to an orientation of the connected \(U\) preserved by \(A\).

02KU Example 39.8.10

\(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^2 \times \mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^2\) carries no metric of positive sectional curvature. Its fundamental group \(\mathbb {Z}/2 \times \mathbb {Z}/2\) (Proposition 18.3.5) is nontrivial, so by Corollary 39.8.8(1) it could only be non-orientable. But then its orientation double covering would be a compact orientable \(4\)-manifold of positive curvature whose fundamental group has order \(2\), a subgroup of index \(2\) of \(\mathbb {Z}/2 \times \mathbb {Z}/2\) (Corollary 18.4.4), contradicting (1). Whether \(S^2 \times S^2\) carries a metric of positive sectional curvature is a famous open question of Hopf; Synge’s theorem does not decide it, as \(S^2 \times S^2\) is simply connected.

028B Remark 39.8.11

Theorems 39.7.8 and 39.8.6 are the simplest comparison theorems, which compare a manifold with a space of constant curvature through its Jacobi fields. The Rauch comparison theorem [dC92, Chapter 10] and the Bishop–Gromov volume comparison [Lee18] are their refinements, and they lead to the sphere theorems and to Gromov’s compactness theorem.
