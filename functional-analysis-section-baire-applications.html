30.5 Applications of the Baire theorems032G

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

The three theorems of Theorem 30.4.2 turn qualitative information (a limit exists for every vector, a map is bijective, a graph is closed) into quantitative information (a uniform bound). We give some classical consequences, culminating in the existence of continuous functions whose Fourier series diverge and in the fact that the generic continuous function is nowhere differentiable.

032H Corollary 30.5.1

Let \(X\) be a vector space that is complete for two norms \(\| \cdot \| _1\) and \(\| \cdot \| _2\), with \(\| x\| _1 \leq C\| x\| _2\) for all \(x\). Then the two norms are equivalent: \(\| x\| _2 \leq C^{\prime }\| x\| _1\) for some \(C^{\prime }\).

Proof â–¼

The identity map \((X, \| \cdot \| _2) \to (X, \| \cdot \| _1)\) is bounded and bijective between Banach spaces, so its inverse is bounded by the open mapping theorem (Theorem 30.4.2(2)).

For instance, \(C^1([0, 1])\) is complete both for \(\| f\| _\infty + \| f^{\prime }\| _\infty \) and for \(|f(0)| + \| f^{\prime }\| _\infty \) (Exercise 30.12.10), and the second norm is bounded by the first, so they are equivalent. On the other hand \(C([0, 1])\) is complete for \(\| \cdot \| _\infty \) and not for \(\| \cdot \| _1 \leq \| \cdot \| _\infty \), and these norms are not equivalent (Example 30.2.3(2)): completeness of both norms cannot be dropped.

032I Corollary 30.5.2 Hellinger–Toeplitz

Let \(H\) be a Hilbert space and \(T \colon H \to H\) a linear map, not assumed continuous, with \(\langle Tx, y \rangle = \langle x, Ty \rangle \) for all \(x, y \in H\). Then \(T\) is bounded.

Proof â–¼

We show that the graph of \(T\) is closed and apply the closed graph theorem (Theorem 30.4.2(3)). Let \(x_n \to x\) and \(Tx_n \to z\). For every \(y\), \(\langle z, y \rangle = \lim _n\langle Tx_n, y \rangle = \lim _n\langle x_n, Ty \rangle = \langle x, Ty \rangle = \langle Tx, y \rangle \), so \(z = Tx\).

The operators of quantum mechanics, such as the position operator \(f(x) \mapsto xf(x)\) or the momentum operator \(f \mapsto -i f^{\prime }\) on \(L^2(\mathbb {R})\), are symmetric and unbounded. By the Hellinger–Toeplitz theorem they cannot be defined on all of \(L^2(\mathbb {R})\): they are only defined on dense subspaces, which is the origin of the theory of unbounded operators.

032J Corollary 30.5.3 Banach–Steinhaus

Let \(X\), \(Y\) be Banach spaces and \(T_n \in B(X, Y)\) such that \(Tx = \lim _nT_nx\) exists for every \(x \in X\). Then \(\sup _n\| T_n\| {\lt} \infty \), and \(T\) is a bounded operator with \(\| T\| \leq \liminf _n\| T_n\| \).

Proof â–¼

Convergent sequences are bounded, so \(\sup _n\| T_nx\| {\lt} \infty \) for every \(x\), and the uniform boundedness principle (Theorem 30.4.2(1)) gives \(\sup _n\| T_n\| {\lt} \infty \). The limit \(T\) is clearly linear, and \(\| Tx\| = \lim _n\| T_nx\| \leq \liminf _n\| T_n\| \, \| x\| \).

30.5.1 Divergence of Fourier series032K

The Fourier series of \(f \in L^2(S^1)\) converges to \(f\) in \(L^2\) (Example 30.3.7), but this says nothing about convergence at a given point. For a continuous \(f\) one might hope that the Fourier series converges at every point. The uniform boundedness principle shows that this fails. Throughout, \(S^1 = \mathbb {R}/\mathbb {Z}\), functions on \(S^1\) are \(1\)-periodic functions on \(\mathbb {R}\), and for \(f \in L^1(S^1)\) we write

\[ \hat f(n) = \int _0^1f(t)e^{-2\pi int}\, dt, \qquad S_Nf(x) = \sum _{n=-N}^N\hat f(n)e^{2\pi inx}. \]

Integrals of \(1\)-periodic functions over an interval of length \(1\) do not depend on the interval, and are invariant under \(t \mapsto t + c\) and \(t \mapsto -t\).

032L Lemma 30.5.4 The Dirichlet kernel

For \(f \in L^1(S^1)\), \(S_Nf(x) = \int _0^1f(x - t)D_N(t)\, dt\), where

\[ D_N(t) = \sum _{n=-N}^Ne^{2\pi int} = \frac{\sin ((2N + 1)\pi t)}{\sin (\pi t)} \quad (t \notin \mathbb {Z}), \qquad D_N(t) = 2N + 1 \quad (t \in \mathbb {Z}). \]

The Lebesgue constants \(L_N = \int _0^1|D_N(t)|\, dt\) satisfy \(L_N \geq \frac{4}{\pi ^2}\sum _{k=1}^N\frac1k\); in particular \(L_N \to \infty \).

Proof â–¼

Inserting the definition of \(\hat f(n)\), \(S_Nf(x) = \int _0^1f(s)\sum _{|n| \leq N}e^{2\pi in(x - s)}\, ds = \int _0^1f(s)D_N(x - s)\, ds\), and the substitution \(t = x - s\) gives the first formula. For \(q = e^{2\pi it} \neq 1\), the geometric sum is

\[ \sum _{n=-N}^Nq^n = \frac{q^{N+1} - q^{-N}}{q - 1} = \frac{q^{N + 1/2} - q^{-N - 1/2}}{q^{1/2} - q^{-1/2}} = \frac{\sin ((2N + 1)\pi t)}{\sin (\pi t)}, \]

with \(q^{1/2} = e^{\pi it}\) and \(e^{i\theta } - e^{-i\theta } = 2i\sin \theta \). Since \(D_N\) is even and \(1\)-periodic, \(L_N = 2\int _0^{1/2}|D_N(t)|\, dt\). On \((0, \frac12]\) we have \(0 {\lt} \sin (\pi t) \leq \pi t\), so, substituting \(u = (2N + 1)t\),

\[ L_N \geq 2\int _0^{1/2}\frac{|\sin ((2N + 1)\pi t)|}{\pi t}\, dt = \frac2\pi \int _0^{N + 1/2}\frac{|\sin (\pi u)|}{u}\, du \geq \frac2\pi \sum _{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{k + 1}\int _k^{k+1}|\sin (\pi u)|\, du = \frac{4}{\pi ^2}\sum _{k=1}^N\frac1k, \]

as \(\int _k^{k+1}|\sin (\pi u)|\, du = 2/\pi \). The harmonic series diverges, so \(L_N \to \infty \).

The numbers \(L_N\) grow like \(\frac{4}{\pi ^2}\log N\). The kernel \(D_N\) is large near \(0\) and oscillates with slowly decaying amplitude elsewhere; its integral is \(\int _0^1D_N = 1\), but the integral of its absolute value is unbounded. This is what makes pointwise convergence delicate.

032M Theorem 30.5.5 du Bois-Reymond

There is a continuous function \(f\) on \(S^1\) whose Fourier series diverges at \(0\): \(\sup _N|S_Nf(0)| = \infty \). More precisely, the set of continuous functions with \(\sup _N|S_Nf(0)| = \infty \) contains a countable intersection of dense open subsets of \(C(S^1)\), and in particular it is dense.

Proof â–¼

Consider the Banach space \(C(S^1)\) with the supremum norm and the linear functionals \(\Lambda _N(f) = S_Nf(0) = \int _0^1f(-t)D_N(t)\, dt = \int _0^1f(t)D_N(t)\, dt\), using that \(D_N\) is even. Clearly \(|\Lambda _Nf| \leq L_N\| f\| _\infty \). We claim \(\| \Lambda _N\| = L_N\). The function \(g = \operatorname {sign}D_N\) takes the values \(\pm 1\) except at the finitely many zeros \(j/(2N + 1)\) of \(D_N\) in \([0, 1]\), and is constant between consecutive zeros. Let \(f_k\) be the continuous \(1\)-periodic function that equals \(g\) outside the intervals of length \(1/k\) centred at the zeros, and is linear on these intervals; then \(|f_k| \leq 1\) and \(f_k \to g\) except at the zeros, so \(\Lambda _N(f_k) \to \int _0^1gD_N = L_N\) by dominated convergence (Theorem 28.4.8). Hence \(\| \Lambda _N\| = L_N \to \infty \) by Lemma 30.5.4.

Now let \(F_n = \{ f \in C(S^1) : |\Lambda _Nf| \leq n \text{ for all } N\} \), a closed set. If some \(F_n\) had nonempty interior, the argument in the proof of the uniform boundedness principle (Theorem 30.4.2(1)) would give \(\sup _N\| \Lambda _N\| {\lt} \infty \), which is false. So each \(F_n\) is closed with empty interior, its complement \(U_n\) is dense and open, and \(\bigcap _nU_n\), which is the set of \(f\) with \(\sup _N|S_Nf(0)| = \infty \), is dense by Baire’s theorem (Theorem 18.7.8).

Translating, for every point \(x\) there are continuous functions whose Fourier series diverges at \(x\), and with a little more work one finds continuous functions whose Fourier series diverges on a dense set of points. The proof is not constructive, although explicit examples exist. On the positive side:

  1. If \(f\) is differentiable at \(x\), or merely satisfies \(|f(t) - f(x)| \leq C|t - x|\) near \(x\), then \(S_Nf(x) \to f(x)\) (Exercise 30.12.12).

  2. The averages \(\sigma _Nf = \frac{1}{N + 1}(S_0f + \cdots + S_Nf)\) converge uniformly to \(f\) for every continuous \(f\) (Fejér’s theorem, Exercise 30.12.11): averaging replaces \(D_N\) by a positive kernel.

  3. For \(f \in L^2(S^1)\) the Fourier series converges almost everywhere; this deep theorem is due to Carleson [Car66]. For \(L^1\) it fails: Kolmogorov constructed an integrable function whose Fourier series diverges almost everywhere [Kol23].

032N Proposition 30.5.6 Riemann–Lebesgue lemma

For \(f \in L^1(S^1)\), \(|\hat f(n)| \leq \| f\| _1\) and \(\hat f(n) \to 0\) as \(|n| \to \infty \). The map \(\mathcal{F} \colon L^1(S^1) \to c_0(\mathbb {Z})\), \(f \mapsto (\hat f(n))_{n \in \mathbb {Z}}\), into the Banach space of sequences tending to \(0\) with the supremum norm, is linear, bounded and injective.

Proof â–¼

The bound is clear from \(|e^{-2\pi int}| = 1\). The trigonometric polynomials are dense in \(L^1(S^1)\): continuous functions are dense in \(L^1([0, 1])\), as in Example 30.3.7 (Proposition 28.5.4 with \(p = 1\)), and trigonometric polynomials approximate continuous functions on \(S^1\) uniformly (Corollary 27.15.3), hence in \(L^1\) since \(\| \cdot \| _1 \leq \| \cdot \| _\infty \). Given \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\), choose a trigonometric polynomial \(p\) with \(\| f - p\| _1 {\lt} \varepsilon \); then \(\hat p(n) = 0\) for \(|n|\) large, and for such \(n\), \(|\hat f(n)| \leq |\hat p(n)| + \| f - p\| _1 {\lt} \varepsilon \). So \(\mathcal{F}\) takes values in \(c_0(\mathbb {Z})\), which is a closed subspace of the Banach space \(\ell ^\infty (\mathbb {Z})\), and \(\| \mathcal{F}\| \leq 1\).

For injectivity let \(\hat f = 0\). Then \(\int _0^1fp = 0\) for every trigonometric polynomial \(p\), and by uniform approximation \(\int _0^1f\phi = 0\) for every continuous \(\phi \) on \(S^1\), since \(|\int f(\phi - p)| \leq \| f\| _1\| \phi - p\| _\infty \). For \(0 {\lt} a {\lt} b {\lt} 1\) there are continuous functions \(\phi _k\) with \(0 \leq \phi _k \leq 1\), supported in \((0, 1)\), converging pointwise to \(\mathbf{1}_{[a, b]}\) (piecewise linear), so \(\int _a^bf = 0\) by dominated convergence. Then the averages \(\frac{1}{2r}\int _{x-r}^{x+r}f\) vanish for \(0 {\lt} x - r {\lt} x + r {\lt} 1\), and the Lebesgue differentiation theorem (Theorem 28.9.4) gives \(f = 0\) almost everywhere.

032O Proposition 30.5.7

The map \(\mathcal{F} \colon L^1(S^1) \to c_0(\mathbb {Z})\) is not surjective: there are sequences tending to \(0\) that are not the Fourier coefficients of any integrable function.

Proof â–¼

If \(\mathcal{F}\) were surjective, it would be a bounded bijection between Banach spaces, so by the open mapping theorem there would be a constant \(C\) with \(\| f\| _1 \leq C\sup _n|\hat f(n)|\) for all \(f \in L^1(S^1)\). Apply this to \(f = D_N\): its Fourier coefficients are \(1\) for \(|n| \leq N\) and \(0\) otherwise, so \(L_N = \| D_N\| _1 \leq C\) for all \(N\), contradicting Lemma 30.5.4.

In contrast, on \(L^2\) the Fourier transform is an isometry onto \(\ell ^2(\mathbb {Z})\) (Theorem 30.3.6): on \(L^2\) the coefficients are characterised by \(\sum |c_n|^2 {\lt} \infty \), while no such description of the Fourier coefficients of integrable functions is known.

The same method shows that pathological functions are, in a precise sense, the typical ones. A subset of a complete metric space is called residual (or generic) if it contains a countable intersection of dense open sets, and meagre if its complement is residual, that is, if it is contained in a countable union of closed sets with empty interior. By Baire’s theorem (Theorem 18.7.8) a residual set is dense, a countable intersection of residual sets is residual, and a nonempty complete metric space is not meagre in itself. Residual sets play the role of “almost all points”, in a topological rather than a measure-theoretic sense. In this language Theorem 30.5.5 says that, for the generic continuous function, the Fourier series diverges at \(0\).

Explicit continuous nowhere differentiable functions, such as the Takagi function (Theorem 27.9.8), look like curiosities: the functions one meets in practice are differentiable at most points. The next theorem, due independently to Banach and Mazurkiewicz (1931), shows that the opposite is true in \(C([0, 1])\).

03BE Theorem 30.5.8 Banach–Mazurkiewicz

In the Banach space \(C([0, 1])\) of real continuous functions with the supremum norm, the set of functions that have a finite right derivative at no point of \([0, 1)\) is residual. In particular continuous functions that are differentiable at no point of \((0, 1)\) form a dense subset of \(C([0, 1])\).

Proof â–¼

For \(n \geq 2\) let \(E_n\) be the set of \(f \in C([0, 1])\) for which there is \(x \in [0, 1 - 1/n]\) with

\[ |f(x + h) - f(x)| \leq nh \quad \text{for all } h \in (0, 1/n]. \]

We show that every function with a finite right derivative at some point of \([0, 1)\) lies in \(\bigcup _nE_n\), and that each \(E_n\) is closed with empty interior. Then \(\bigcup _nE_n\) is meagre, and its complement, which is contained in the set of the theorem, is residual, hence dense since \(C([0, 1])\) is complete.

Step 1. Suppose that \(f\) has a finite right derivative \(c\) at \(x \in [0, 1)\). There is \(\delta \in (0, 1 - x)\) with \(|f(x + h) - f(x)| \leq (|c| + 1)h\) for \(0 {\lt} h \leq \delta \), and for \(\delta {\lt} h \leq 1 - x\) we have \(|f(x + h) - f(x)| \leq 2\| f\| _\infty \leq (2\| f\| _\infty /\delta )h\). Choose an integer \(n \geq 2\) with \(n \geq |c| + 1\), \(n \geq 2\| f\| _\infty /\delta \) and \(1/n \leq 1 - x\). Then \(x \leq 1 - 1/n\), and for \(h \in (0, 1/n]\), which ensures \(h \leq 1 - x\), one of the two estimates applies; so \(f \in E_n\).

Step 2: \(E_n\) is closed. Let \(f_k \in E_n\) converge uniformly to \(f\), with points \(x_k \in [0, 1 - 1/n]\) as in the definition. Passing to a subsequence, \(x_k \to x \in [0, 1 - 1/n]\) (Theorem 18.6.5 and Theorem 18.7.4). Fix \(h \in (0, 1/n]\). Then \(x_k + h \leq 1\) and

\[ |f_k(x_k + h) - f(x + h)| \leq \| f_k - f\| _\infty + |f(x_k + h) - f(x + h)| \to 0 \]

by continuity of \(f\), and likewise \(f_k(x_k) \to f(x)\). Passing to the limit in \(|f_k(x_k + h) - f_k(x_k)| \leq nh\) gives \(|f(x + h) - f(x)| \leq nh\). So \(f \in E_n\).

Step 3: \(E_n\) has empty interior. Let \(f \in C([0, 1])\) and \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\); we find \(g \notin E_n\) with \(\| g - f\| _\infty {\lt} \varepsilon \). By the Weierstrass approximation theorem (Corollary 27.15.3) there is a polynomial \(p\) with \(\| f - p\| _\infty {\lt} \varepsilon /2\); let \(M = \sup _{[0, 1]}|p^{\prime }|\), so that \(|p(y) - p(x)| \leq M|y - x|\) by the mean value theorem. Choose \(L {\gt} M + n\) and put \(\delta = \varepsilon /(2L)\), and let \(\varphi (x) = L\operatorname {dist}(x, 2\delta \mathbb {Z})\), a “sawtooth” with values in \([0, L\delta ] = [0, \varepsilon /2]\) that is linear with slope \(\pm L\) on each interval \([m\delta , (m + 1)\delta ]\), \(m \in \mathbb {Z}\). Let \(g = p + \varphi \); then \(\| g - f\| _\infty \leq \| p - f\| _\infty + \| \varphi \| _\infty {\lt} \varepsilon \). For \(x \in [0, 1 - 1/n]\), choose \(m\) with \(m\delta \leq x {\lt} (m + 1)\delta \) and \(h \in (0, 1/n]\) with \(x + h {\lt} (m + 1)\delta \). Then \(|\varphi (x + h) - \varphi (x)| = Lh\), so

\[ |g(x + h) - g(x)| \geq |\varphi (x + h) - \varphi (x)| - |p(x + h) - p(x)| \geq (L - M)h {\gt} nh . \]

So no point \(x\) works for \(g\), and \(g \notin E_n\).

Finally, a function differentiable at a point \(x \in (0, 1)\) has a finite right derivative at \(x\), so the functions differentiable at no point of \((0, 1)\) contain the residual set of the theorem.

The proof shows more than density: the nowhere differentiable functions contain a countable intersection of dense open sets, so they are “topologically almost all” continuous functions, although no single one of them is produced by the proof. The same pattern, “the bad set is a countable union of closed sets with empty interior”, recurs throughout analysis; compare the proof of Theorem 30.5.5. Note that residual sets can be small in the sense of measure: in \(\mathbb {R}\), the complement of a meagre set can have measure zero (Exercise 30.12.18), so “generic” and “almost everywhere” are genuinely different notions.
