39.9 Submanifolds and the second fundamental form02IB

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

Classical differential geometry studies curves and surfaces in \(\mathbb {R}^3\) through two quadratic forms: the first fundamental form, the metric induced from \(\mathbb {R}^3\), and the second fundamental form, which measures how the surface bends in the ambient space. This section develops both for a submanifold of any Riemannian manifold, proves the equations of Gauss and Codazzi that relate them to the curvature, and then specialises to surfaces in \(\mathbb {R}^3\), where Gauss’s equation becomes the Theorema Egregium.

Throughout, \((\overline{M}, \bar{g})\) is a Riemannian manifold with Levi-Civita connection \(\overline{\nabla }\) and curvature \(\overline{R}\), and \(M \subseteq \overline{M}\) is a submanifold with the induced metric \(g = \bar{g}|_{TM}\), the first fundamental form; we write \(\langle -, - \rangle \) for both. For \(p \in M\), \(T_p\overline{M} = T_pM \oplus N_pM\) with \(N_pM = (T_pM)^\perp \), the normal space, and \(v = v^\top + v^\perp \) is the corresponding decomposition. The normal spaces form the normal bundle \(NM\), a vector bundle over \(M\): near each point it has a smooth frame, obtained by applying Gram–Schmidt to a frame of \(T\overline{M}\) extending one of \(TM\). For \(\overline{M} = \mathbb {R}^N\) with the Euclidean metric, \(\overline{\nabla }_XY\) is the directional derivative of the components of \(Y\), and \(\overline{R} = 0\).

02IC Lemma 39.9.1

Every vector field on an open subset of \(M\) extends, near each point, to a vector field on an open subset of \(\overline{M}\). For vector fields \(X\), \(Y\) on \(M\) and extensions \(\overline{X}\), \(\overline{Y}\), the value of \(\overline{\nabla }_{\overline{X}}\overline{Y}\) at \(p \in M\) depends only on \(X\) and \(Y\), and we denote it by \(\overline{\nabla }_XY\). Moreover \([\overline{X}, \overline{Y}]\) restricts on \(M\) to \([X, Y]\).

Proof â–¼

In a slice chart \((x^1, \ldots , x^k, y^1, \ldots , y^m)\) of \(\overline{M}\), in which \(M\) is \(\{ y = 0\} \), a vector field \(\sum _iY^i(x)\partial /\partial x^i\) on \(M\) extends by the same formula, with coefficients independent of \(y\). The value of \(\overline{\nabla }_{\overline{X}}\overline{Y}\) at \(p\) depends only on \(\overline{X}_p = X_p\), since a connection is \(C^\infty \)-linear in its first argument, and it equals \(D_t(\overline{Y} \circ \gamma )(0)\) for any curve \(\gamma \) with \(\dot\gamma (0) = X_p\) (Proposition 39.4.1); taking \(\gamma \) in \(M\), \(\overline{Y} \circ \gamma = Y \circ \gamma \). Finally \(X\) and \(\overline{X}\) are related by the inclusion \(\iota \), and so are \(Y\) and \(\overline{Y}\), so \([X, Y]\) and \([\overline{X}, \overline{Y}]\) are \(\iota \)-related (Lemma 33.2.7).

02ID Theorem 39.9.2 Gauss formula

For vector fields \(X\), \(Y\) tangent to \(M\),

\[ \overline{\nabla }_XY = \nabla _XY + \mathrm{II}(X, Y), \]

where \(\nabla _XY = (\overline{\nabla }_XY)^\top \) is the Levi-Civita connection of \((M, g)\), and \(\mathrm{II}(X, Y) = (\overline{\nabla }_XY)^\perp \), the second fundamental form, is a symmetric \(C^\infty (M)\)-bilinear map with values in the normal bundle.

Proof â–¼

The operator \(\nabla ^\top _XY = (\overline{\nabla }_XY)^\top \) is a connection on \(TM\): it is \(C^\infty \)-linear in \(X\), and \(\overline{\nabla }_X(fY) = (Xf)Y + f\overline{\nabla }_XY\) with \(Y\) tangent gives the Leibniz rule. It is metric: for tangent \(Y\) and \(Z\), \(X\langle Y, Z\rangle = \langle \overline{\nabla }_XY, Z\rangle + \langle Y, \overline{\nabla }_XZ\rangle = \langle \nabla ^\top _XY, Z\rangle + \langle Y, \nabla ^\top _XZ\rangle \), as the normal parts are orthogonal to \(Y\) and \(Z\). By Lemma 39.9.1 and the torsion-freeness of \(\overline{\nabla }\), \(\overline{\nabla }_XY - \overline{\nabla }_YX = [X, Y]\), which is tangent. Its tangent part says that \(\nabla ^\top \) is torsion-free, so \(\nabla ^\top = \nabla \) by the uniqueness in Theorem 39.2.6; its normal part says \(\mathrm{II}(X, Y) = \mathrm{II}(Y, X)\). Finally \(\mathrm{II}\) is \(C^\infty \)-linear in \(X\), and in \(Y\) because \(\mathrm{II}(X, fY) = ((Xf)Y + f\overline{\nabla }_XY)^\perp = f\, \mathrm{II}(X, Y)\).

So the intrinsic connection of \(M\) is the tangential part of the ambient one: for a surface in \(\mathbb {R}^3\), \(\nabla _XY\) is the derivative of \(Y\) in \(\mathbb {R}^3\) projected back onto the tangent plane. A curve in \(M\) is a geodesic of \(M\) exactly when its acceleration in \(\overline{M}\) is normal to \(M\); for instance great circles on the sphere (Exercise 39.14.5).

The Gauss formula does not need the ambient extensions of Lemma 39.9.1, and it holds for immersions that are not injective, such as a curve with self-intersections. Let \(f \colon M \to \overline{M}\) be an immersion with the induced metric \(g = f^*\bar g\), and let \(\overline{\nabla }^f = f^*\overline{\nabla }\) be the pullback connection on \(f^*T\overline{M}\) (Proposition 37.4.5(1)): it differentiates sections of \(f^*T\overline{M}\), such as \(df(Y)\) for a vector field \(Y\) on \(M\), in the direction of tangent vectors of \(M\), and \(\overline{\nabla }^f_X(s \circ f) = \overline{\nabla }_{df(X)}s\) for a vector field \(s\) on \(\overline{M}\). The differential identifies \(TM\) with the subbundle \(df(TM)\) of \(f^*T\overline{M}\), whose orthogonal complement \(N_f\) is the normal bundle of \(f\).

One often writes \(\overline{\nabla }_{df(X)}(df(Y))\) for \(\overline{\nabla }^f_X(df(Y))\). This is justified by Remark 37.5.1: if near \(p\) the section \(df(Y)\) is of the form \(\overline{Y} \circ f\) for a vector field \(\overline{Y}\) on an open set of \(\overline{M}\), then \(\overline{\nabla }^f_X(df(Y)) = \overline{\nabla }_{df(X)}\overline{Y}\), whatever the extension \(\overline{Y}\); for an immersion such extensions exist near every point, since \(f\) is an embedding near each point. But \(df(Y)\) itself is not a vector field on \(\overline{M}\): it is defined only along \(f\), and at a self-intersection \(f(p) = f(q)\) it has two values. For maps that are not immersions the abbreviation is even misleading: for a constant curve \(c\) and a nonconstant field \(V\) along it, \(\dot c = 0\) but \((c^*\overline{\nabla })_{d/dt}V = \dot V \neq 0\).

02LA Proposition 39.9.3 Gauss formula for immersions

For vector fields \(X\), \(Y\) on \(M\),

\[ \overline{\nabla }^f_X\bigl(df(Y)\bigr) = df(\nabla _XY) + \mathrm{II}(X, Y), \]

where \(\nabla \) is the Levi-Civita connection of \(g\) and \(\mathrm{II}(X, Y) = (\overline{\nabla }^f_X(df(Y)))^\perp \) is a symmetric \(C^\infty (M)\)-bilinear map with values in \(N_f\). In particular \(df(\nabla _XY) = (\overline{\nabla }^f_X(df(Y)))^\top \). For an embedding this is Theorem 39.9.2, and for \(\overline{M} = \mathbb {R}^N\), \(\overline{\nabla }^f_X(df(Y))\) is the derivative \(X(df(Y))\) of the \(\mathbb {R}^N\)-valued function \(df(Y)\).

Proof â–¼

\(\overline{\nabla }^f\) is metric: \(X\langle \sigma , \tau \rangle = \langle \overline{\nabla }^f_X\sigma , \tau \rangle + \langle \sigma , \overline{\nabla }^f_X\tau \rangle \) for sections \(\sigma \), \(\tau \) of \(f^*T\overline{M}\). Both sides satisfy the same Leibniz rule when \(\sigma \) or \(\tau \) is multiplied by a function, and locally \(f^*T\overline{M}\) has frames \(s_a \circ f\) pulled back from frames of \(T\overline{M}\); for \(\sigma = s \circ f\) and \(\tau = t \circ f\) the identity is \(df(X)\langle s, t\rangle = \langle \overline{\nabla }_{df(X)}s, t\rangle + \langle s, \overline{\nabla }_{df(X)}t\rangle \) at \(f(p)\).

Symmetry: \(\overline{\nabla }^f_X(df(Y)) - \overline{\nabla }^f_Y(df(X)) = df([X, Y])\). The difference of the two sides is \(C^\infty (M)\)-bilinear in \(X\) and \(Y\) (the terms with derivatives of a function cancel, as for the torsion), so it suffices to take coordinate fields \(X = \partial _i\), \(Y = \partial _j\) of a chart \(u\) of \(M\). With a chart \(y\) of \(\overline{M}\) and \(f^a = y^a \circ f\), \(df(\partial _j) = \sum _a\partial _jf^a\, (\partial _a \circ f)\) and

\[ \overline{\nabla }^f_{\partial _i}\bigl(df(\partial _j)\bigr) = \sum _a\Bigl(\partial _i\partial _jf^a + \sum _{b,c}\overline{\Gamma }^a_{bc}(f)\, \partial _if^b\, \partial _jf^c\Bigr)(\partial _a \circ f), \]

which is symmetric in \(i\) and \(j\) since \(\overline{\Gamma }^a_{bc} = \overline{\Gamma }^a_{cb}\); and \([\partial _i, \partial _j] = 0\).

Conclusion: as in the proof of Theorem 39.9.2, \(\nabla ^{\prime }_XY = df^{-1}\bigl((\overline{\nabla }^f_X(df(Y)))^\top \bigr)\) is a connection on \(TM\); it is metric for \(g\) because \(\overline{\nabla }^f\) is metric and \(N_f \perp df(TM)\), and torsion-free by the tangential part of the symmetry. So \(\nabla ^{\prime } = \nabla \) (Theorem 39.2.6); the normal part of the symmetry gives \(\mathrm{II}(X, Y) = \mathrm{II}(Y, X)\), and \(\mathrm{II}\) is \(C^\infty \)-linear in \(X\) and in \(Y\) as before. For an embedding and an extension \(\overline{Y}\) of \(Y\), \(df(Y) = \overline{Y} \circ f\), so \(\overline{\nabla }^f_X(df(Y)) = \overline{\nabla }_X\overline{Y}\). For \(\overline{M} = \mathbb {R}^N\) the Christoffel symbols vanish.

02IE Definition 39.9.4

For a normal vector field \(\nu \) along \(M\), the shape operator (or Weingarten map) is \(S_\nu (X) = -(\overline{\nabla }_X\nu )^\top \), and the normal connection is \(\nabla ^\perp _X\nu = (\overline{\nabla }_X\nu )^\perp \).

02IF Proposition 39.9.5 Weingarten equation

For tangent \(X\), \(Y\) and normal \(\nu \), \(\langle S_\nu X, Y\rangle = \langle \mathrm{II}(X, Y), \nu \rangle \). In particular \(S_\nu \) is self-adjoint on each tangent space, and \(\overline{\nabla }_X\nu = -S_\nu X + \nabla ^\perp _X\nu \).

Proof â–¼

Differentiate \(\langle Y, \nu \rangle = 0\): \(0 = \langle \overline{\nabla }_XY, \nu \rangle + \langle Y, \overline{\nabla }_X\nu \rangle = \langle \mathrm{II}(X, Y), \nu \rangle - \langle Y, S_\nu X\rangle \). Self-adjointness follows from the symmetry of \(\mathrm{II}\), and the last formula is the decomposition of \(\overline{\nabla }_X\nu \) into its two parts.

02IG Theorem 39.9.6 Gauss and Codazzi equations

For vector fields \(X, Y, Z, W\) tangent to \(M\):

  1. (Gauss) \(R(X, Y, Z, W) = \overline{R}(X, Y, Z, W) + \langle \mathrm{II}(Y, Z), \mathrm{II}(X, W)\rangle - \langle \mathrm{II}(X, Z), \mathrm{II}(Y, W)\rangle \);

  2. (Codazzi) \((\overline{R}(X, Y)Z)^\perp = (\nabla _X\mathrm{II})(Y, Z) - (\nabla _Y\mathrm{II})(X, Z)\), where \((\nabla _X\mathrm{II})(Y, Z) = \nabla ^\perp _X(\mathrm{II}(Y, Z)) - \mathrm{II}(\nabla _XY, Z) - \mathrm{II}(Y, \nabla _XZ)\).

In particular, for orthonormal \(u, v \in T_pM\) the sectional curvatures of the plane they span satisfy \(K(u, v) = \overline{K}(u, v) + \langle \mathrm{II}(u, u), \mathrm{II}(v, v)\rangle - |\mathrm{II}(u, v)|^2\).

Proof â–¼

By the Gauss formula, \(\overline{\nabla }_X\overline{\nabla }_YZ = \overline{\nabla }_X(\nabla _YZ) + \overline{\nabla }_X(\mathrm{II}(Y, Z))\), and applying the Gauss formula again to the tangent field \(\nabla _YZ\) and the Weingarten equation to the normal field \(\mathrm{II}(Y, Z)\),

\[ \overline{\nabla }_X\overline{\nabla }_YZ = \nabla _X\nabla _YZ + \mathrm{II}(X, \nabla _YZ) - S_{\mathrm{II}(Y, Z)}X + \nabla ^\perp _X(\mathrm{II}(Y, Z)). \]

Also \(\overline{\nabla }_{[X,Y]}Z = \nabla _{[X,Y]}Z + \mathrm{II}([X, Y], Z)\). Subtract the same expressions with \(X\) and \(Y\) exchanged, and use \([X, Y] = \nabla _XY - \nabla _YX\). The tangential part gives \((\overline{R}(X, Y)Z)^\top = R(X, Y)Z - S_{\mathrm{II}(Y, Z)}X + S_{\mathrm{II}(X, Z)}Y\); pairing with \(W\) and using the Weingarten equation gives (1). The normal part gives

\[ (\overline{R}(X, Y)Z)^\perp = \nabla ^\perp _X(\mathrm{II}(Y, Z)) + \mathrm{II}(X, \nabla _YZ) - \nabla ^\perp _Y(\mathrm{II}(X, Z)) - \mathrm{II}(Y, \nabla _XZ) - \mathrm{II}(\nabla _XY - \nabla _YX, Z), \]

which is (2) after regrouping. For the last statement take \(X = W = u\) and \(Y = Z = v\) in (1), recalling \(K(u, v) = R(u, v, v, u)\).

02IH Definition 39.9.7

Let \(M\) be a hypersurface (\(\dim \overline{M} = \dim M + 1\)) with a unit normal field \(\nu \), which exists locally, and globally if \(M\) and \(\overline{M}\) are orientable. The scalar second fundamental form is \(h(X, Y) = \langle \mathrm{II}(X, Y), \nu \rangle \), so \(\mathrm{II} = h\, \nu \), and the shape operator \(S = S_\nu \) satisfies \(\langle SX, Y\rangle = h(X, Y)\). The eigenvalues \(\kappa _1, \ldots , \kappa _n\) of the self-adjoint map \(S\) on \(T_pM\) are the principal curvatures, its eigenvectors the principal directions, \(H = \frac1n\operatorname {tr}S\) is the mean curvature, and \(\det S = \kappa _1 \cdots \kappa _n\) the Gauss–Kronecker curvature. Changing \(\nu \) to \(-\nu \) changes the signs of \(h\), \(S\), the \(\kappa _i\) and \(H\).

02II Corollary 39.9.8 Theorema Egregium

Let \(M^n \subseteq \mathbb {R}^{n+1}\) be a hypersurface with unit normal \(\nu \). Then \(R(X, Y, Z, W) = h(Y, Z)h(X, W) - h(X, Z)h(Y, W)\), and for orthonormal principal directions \(e_i\), \(e_j\) the sectional curvature is \(K(e_i, e_j) = \kappa _i\kappa _j\). In particular, for a surface in \(\mathbb {R}^3\) the Gaussian curvature is \(K = \kappa _1\kappa _2 = \det S\): a quantity defined through the embedding is determined by the metric alone, and so is preserved by isometries between surfaces.

Proof â–¼

Apply Theorem 39.9.6(1) with \(\overline{R} = 0\) and \(\mathrm{II} = h\nu \), \(|\nu | = 1\). For principal directions, \(h(e_i, e_i) = \kappa _i\) and \(h(e_i, e_j) = 0\) for \(i \neq j\). For a surface the only plane is the tangent plane, and \(\kappa _1\kappa _2\) is the determinant of \(S\).

Codazzi’s equation for a hypersurface of \(\mathbb {R}^{n+1}\) says that \((\nabla _Xh)(Y, Z)\) is symmetric in all three arguments. Gauss’s and Codazzi’s equations are the integrability conditions for the embedding: by Bonnet’s theorem, a metric and a symmetric form on a simply connected surface satisfying them come from a surface in \(\mathbb {R}^3\), unique up to rigid motions [dC76, Chapter 4].

02IJ Proposition 39.9.9 Surfaces in coordinates

Let \(x \colon \Omega \to \mathbb {R}^3\) be a parametrisation of a surface \(S\) (Remark 32.6.16), with coordinates \((u^1, u^2) = (u, v)\), \(x_i = \partial x/\partial u^i\), and unit normal \(\nu = x_1 \times x_2/|x_1 \times x_2|\). Let

\[ g_{ij} = \langle x_i, x_j\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} E & F \\ F & G \end{pmatrix}, \qquad h_{ij} = \langle \partial _i\partial _jx, \nu \rangle = -\langle x_i, \partial _j\nu \rangle . \]

Then the first and second fundamental forms are \(E\, du^2 + 2F\, du\, dv + G\, dv^2\) and \(h_{11}\, du^2 + 2h_{12}\, du\, dv + h_{22}\, dv^2\), the matrix of \(S\) in the basis \((x_1, x_2)\) is \((g_{ij})^{-1}(h_{ij})\), and

\[ K = \frac{h_{11}h_{22} - h_{12}^2}{EG - F^2}, \qquad H = \frac{Eh_{22} - 2Fh_{12} + Gh_{11}}{2(EG - F^2)}. \]

More generally, for a submanifold of \(\mathbb {R}^N\) parametrised by \(x(u^1, \ldots , u^n)\), the decomposition \(\partial _i\partial _jx = \Gamma ^k_{ij}x_k + \mathrm{II}(x_i, x_j)\) of the second derivatives into tangent and normal parts gives the Christoffel symbols and the second fundamental form, and \(R_{ijkl} = R(x_i, x_j, x_k, x_l) = \langle \mathrm{II}_{jk}, \mathrm{II}_{il}\rangle - \langle \mathrm{II}_{ik}, \mathrm{II}_{jl}\rangle \) with \(\mathrm{II}_{ij} = \mathrm{II}(x_i, x_j)\).

Proof â–¼

In \(\mathbb {R}^N\), \(\overline{\nabla }\) is the directional derivative, and the coordinate field \(\partial /\partial u^j\) is \(x_j\) along \(S\), so \(\overline{\nabla }_{x_i}x_j = \partial _i\partial _jx\). The Gauss formula splits it as \(\nabla _{x_i}x_j + \mathrm{II}(x_i, x_j)\), which gives the general statement, the tangent part being \(\Gamma ^k_{ij}x_k\), and for surfaces \(h_{ij} = \langle \partial _i\partial _jx, \nu \rangle \); differentiating \(\langle x_i, \nu \rangle = 0\) gives the second expression. From \(\langle Sx_i, x_j\rangle = h_{ij}\), the matrix \(A\) of \(S\) satisfies \((g_{ij})A = (h_{ij})\). So \(K = \det S = \det (h_{ij})/\det (g_{ij})\) and \(2H = \operatorname {tr}((g_{ij})^{-1}(h_{ij}))\), which are the formulas stated, using \((g_{ij})^{-1} = \frac{1}{EG - F^2}\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} G & -F \\ -F & E \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\). The formula for \(R_{ijkl}\) is Theorem 39.9.6(1) with \(\overline{R} = 0\).

For a hypersurface of \(\mathbb {R}^{n+1}\) the unit normal is a map \(\nu \colon M \to S^n\), the Gauss map, and since \(T_{\nu (p)}S^n = \nu (p)^\perp = T_pM\) its differential is an endomorphism of \(T_pM\). The shape operator is minus this differential, and the next proposition writes the relation between the matrices of \(d\nu \), of the first fundamental form and of the second in coordinates.

02JM Proposition 39.9.10 Weingarten equations in coordinates

Let \(M \subseteq \mathbb {R}^{n+1}\) be a hypersurface with unit normal \(\nu \) and shape operator \(S = S_\nu \), parametrised by \(x(u^1, \ldots , u^n)\), with \(x_i = \partial x/\partial u^i\), \(g_{ij} = \langle x_i, x_j\rangle \), \(h_{ij} = h(x_i, x_j) = \langle \partial _i\partial _jx, \nu \rangle \), and \((g^{ij})\) the inverse matrix of \((g_{ij})\).

  1. \(d\nu = -S\): the derivative \(\partial _i\nu = \partial (\nu \circ x)/\partial u^i\) is tangent to \(M\) and equals \(-S(x_i)\).

  2. Write \(\partial _i\nu = \sum _ka_{ki}\, x_k\), so that \(A = (a_{ki})\) is the matrix of \(d\nu \) in the basis \((x_1, \ldots , x_n)\). Then

    \[ h_{ij} = -\sum _{k=1}^na_{ki}\, g_{kj}, \qquad \text{that is,}\qquad (h_{ij}) = -A^T(g_{ij}), \qquad A = -(g_{ij})^{-1}(h_{ij}), \qquad a_{ki} = -\sum _lg^{kl}h_{li}, \]

    and the matrix of \(S\) in the same basis is \(-A = (g_{ij})^{-1}(h_{ij})\).

  3. The Gauss–Kronecker curvature is \(\det S = \det (h_{ij})/\det (g_{ij})\), and \(\det (d\nu ) = (-1)^n\det (h_{ij})/\det (g_{ij})\).

  4. For a surface in \(\mathbb {R}^3\), with \(E, F, G\) as in Proposition 39.9.9 and \(\Delta = EG - F^2\),

    \[ a_{11} = \frac{Fh_{12} - Gh_{11}}{\Delta }, \quad a_{12} = \frac{Fh_{22} - Gh_{12}}{\Delta }, \quad a_{21} = \frac{Fh_{11} - Eh_{12}}{\Delta }, \quad a_{22} = \frac{Fh_{12} - Eh_{22}}{\Delta }, \]

    and \(K = \det (d\nu ) = \det S = (h_{11}h_{22} - h_{12}^2)/\Delta \).

Proof â–¼

(1) Differentiating \(\langle \nu , \nu \rangle = 1\) gives \(\langle \partial _i\nu , \nu \rangle = 0\), so \(\partial _i\nu \) is tangent. In \(\mathbb {R}^{n+1}\), \(\overline{\nabla }_{x_i}\nu \) is the derivative \(\partial _i\nu \) of the components of \(\nu \), and by Definition 39.9.4, \(S(x_i) = -(\overline{\nabla }_{x_i}\nu )^\top = -\partial _i\nu \).

(2) Differentiating \(\langle x_j, \nu \rangle = 0\) in \(u^i\) gives \(h_{ij} = \langle \partial _i\partial _jx, \nu \rangle = -\langle x_j, \partial _i\nu \rangle \), and inserting \(\partial _i\nu = \sum _ka_{ki}x_k\) gives \(h_{ij} = -\sum _ka_{ki}g_{kj} = -(A^T(g_{ij}))_{ij}\). Transposing, and using that \((g_{ij})\) and \((h_{ij})\) are symmetric, \((h_{ij}) = -(g_{ij})A\), so \(A = -(g_{ij})^{-1}(h_{ij})\). The matrix of \(S = -d\nu \) is \(-A\).

(3) \(\det S = \det \bigl((g_{ij})^{-1}(h_{ij})\bigr) = \det (h_{ij})/\det (g_{ij})\), and \(\det (d\nu ) = \det (-S) = (-1)^n\det S\).

(4) Here \((g_{ij})^{-1} = \Delta ^{-1}\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} G & -F \\ -F & E \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\), and multiplying out \(-(g_{ij})^{-1}(h_{ij})\) gives the four entries. For \(n = 2\) the sign \((-1)^n\) is \(+1\), and \(K = \det S\) by Corollary 39.9.8.

02JN Remark 39.9.11

With \(e = h_{11}\), \(f = h_{12}\), \(g = h_{22}\) (the letter \(g\) here is not the metric), part (4) is the form in which the Weingarten equations appear in [dC76, Chapter 3], where one works with \(d\nu \) itself and \(\mathrm{II}_p(v) = -\langle d\nu _p(v), v\rangle \). The two conventions differ by the sign \(S = -d\nu \), which is the most common source of sign errors: if \(a_{ki}\) denotes the matrix of \(S\) instead of \(d\nu \), the formula of (2) reads \(h_{ij} = +\sum _ka_{ki}g_{kj}\). For surfaces the sign does not affect \(K\), because \(\det (-S) = \det S\) for \(2 \times 2\) matrices, but it changes the sign of the mean curvature, and in odd dimension \(n\) it changes the sign of the Gauss–Kronecker curvature. For example, for the unit sphere with the outward normal \(\nu (p) = p\), the Gauss map is the identity, \(d\nu = \mathrm{id}\), \(S = -\mathrm{id}\), and \((h_{ij}) = -(g_{ij})\), as in Example 39.9.12(1) below.

02IK Example 39.9.12

  1. Spheres. For the sphere of radius \(r\) in \(\mathbb {R}^{n+1}\) with outward normal \(\nu (p) = p/r\), \(\overline{\nabla }_X\nu = X/r\), so \(S = -\frac1r\mathrm{id}\), \(h = -\frac1rg\), every direction is principal with \(\kappa = -1/r\), and all sectional curvatures are \(\kappa _i\kappa _j = 1/r^2\), as in Example 39.7.3.

  2. Cylinders. For \(\{ x^2 + y^2 = 1\} \subseteq \mathbb {R}^3\) with \(\nu = (x, y, 0)\), \(\overline{\nabla }_X\nu \) is the horizontal part of \(X\), so the principal curvatures are \(-1\) (horizontal circles) and \(0\) (vertical lines), \(K = 0\) and \(H = -\frac12\). The cylinder is locally isometric to the plane (unroll it), in accordance with the Theorema Egregium, although it bends in \(\mathbb {R}^3\).

  3. Graphs. Let \(S\) be the graph of \(z = \phi (u, v)\) with \(\phi (0) = 0\) and \(d\phi (0) = 0\), parametrised by \(x(u, v) = (u, v, \phi (u, v))\). At the origin \(x_1 = e_1\), \(x_2 = e_2\), \(\nu = e_3\), so \(g_{ij} = \delta _{ij}\) and \(h_{ij} = \partial _i\partial _j\phi (0)\): the second fundamental form is the Hessian of \(\phi \), the principal curvatures are its eigenvalues, and \(K(0) = \det \operatorname {Hess}\phi (0)\). The paraboloid \(z = u^2 + v^2\) has \(K(0) = 4\), the saddle \(z = u^2 - v^2\) has \(K(0) = -4\): positive curvature means that the surface lies locally on one side of its tangent plane, negative curvature that it crosses it.
