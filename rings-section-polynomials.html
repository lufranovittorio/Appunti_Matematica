7.6 Polynomial rings00F3

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

00F4 Definition 7.6.1

Let \(R\) be a ring. The polynomial ring \(R[x]\) is the set of sequences \((a_0, a_1, \ldots )\) in \(R\) with only finitely many nonzero terms, with termwise addition and the product \((a_i)(b_j) = (c_k)\), \(c_k = \sum _{i + j = k} a_i b_j\). We identify \(r \in R\) with \((r, 0, 0, \ldots )\) and write \(x = (0, 1, 0, \ldots )\), so that every polynomial is uniquely \(f = \sum _{i=0}^n a_i x^i\). If \(f \neq 0\), its degree \(\deg f\) is the largest \(i\) with \(a_i \neq 0\), and \(a_{\deg f}\) is the leading coefficient; \(f\) is monic if the leading coefficient is \(1\). The ring \(R[x_1, \ldots , x_n]\) is defined inductively as \(R[x_1, \ldots , x_{n-1}][x_n]\), and the power series ring \(R[[x]]\) is defined like \(R[x]\) but with all sequences allowed.

00F5 Proposition 7.6.2

Let \(\varphi \colon R \to S\) be a ring homomorphism and \(s \in S\) an element commuting with all elements of \(\varphi (R)\). There is a unique ring homomorphism \(R[x] \to S\) extending \(\varphi \) and sending \(x\) to \(s\), namely \(\sum a_i x^i \mapsto \sum \varphi (a_i) s^i\). In particular, for \(R\) commutative, \(R[x_1, \ldots , x_n]\) is the free commutative \(R\)-algebra on \(x_1, \ldots , x_n\): homomorphisms to a commutative ring \(S\) extending \(\varphi \) correspond to \(n\)-tuples of elements of \(S\).

Proof â–¼

The formula is forced, and it preserves products because \(s\) commutes with the \(\varphi (a_i)\): \(\bigl(\sum \varphi (a_i)s^i\bigr)\bigl(\sum \varphi (b_j)s^j\bigr) = \sum _k \bigl(\sum _{i+j=k} \varphi (a_i)\varphi (b_j)\bigr)s^k\). The last statement follows by induction on \(n\).

00F6 Notation 7.6.3

For \(R\) commutative, \(f \in R[x]\) and \(a \in R\), we write \(f(a)\) for the image of \(f\) under the homomorphism \(R[x] \to R\) with \(x \mapsto a\). An element \(a\) with \(f(a) = 0\) is a root of \(f\).

00F7 Proposition 7.6.4

If \(R\) is an integral domain, then \(\deg (fg) = \deg f + \deg g\) for nonzero \(f, g\), so \(R[x]\) is an integral domain and \(R[x]^\times = R^\times \).

Proof â–¼

The coefficient of \(x^{\deg f + \deg g}\) in \(fg\) is the product of the leading coefficients, which is nonzero; all higher coefficients vanish. If \(fg = 1\) then \(\deg f = \deg g = 0\).

00F8 Theorem 7.6.5 Division with remainder

Let \(R\) be commutative and \(g \in R[x]\) a polynomial whose leading coefficient is a unit. For every \(f \in R[x]\) there are unique \(q, r \in R[x]\) with \(f = qg + r\) and \(r = 0\) or \(\deg r {\lt} \deg g\). Consequently, if \(k\) is a field then \(k[x]\) is Euclidean with \(N = \deg \), hence a PID and a UFD.

Proof â–¼

Existence by induction on \(\deg f\): if \(f = 0\) or \(\deg f {\lt} \deg g\) take \(q = 0\). Otherwise, with \(a\) and \(b\) the leading coefficients of \(f\) and \(g\), the polynomial \(f - ab^{-1}x^{\deg f - \deg g}g\) has smaller degree or is zero. Uniqueness: if \(qg + r = q'g + r'\) with \(q \neq q'\), then \((q - q')g = r' - r\) has degree \(\geq \deg g\), because the leading coefficient of \(g\) is a unit (so it is not a zero divisor), which is impossible. The last statement follows from Theorem 7.5.4.

00F9 Corollary 7.6.6

Let \(R\) be commutative, \(f \in R[x]\) and \(a \in R\). Then \(f(a) = 0\) if and only if \((x - a) \mid f\). If \(R\) is an integral domain and \(f \neq 0\), then \(f\) has at most \(\deg f\) roots in \(R\).

Proof â–¼

Divide: \(f = q(x - a) + r\) with \(r \in R\), and \(r = f(a)\). If \(R\) is a domain and \(a_1, \ldots , a_m\) are distinct roots, induction shows \((x - a_1) \cdots (x - a_m) \mid f\): if \(f = (x - a_1)g\) then \(0 = f(a_j) = (a_j - a_1)g(a_j)\) gives \(g(a_j) = 0\) for \(j \geq 2\). So \(m \leq \deg f\) by Proposition 7.6.4.

00FA Counterexample 7.6.7

The hypothesis that \(R\) is a domain is needed: in \(\mathbb {Z}/8\mathbb {Z}\) the polynomial \(x^2 - 1\) of degree \(2\) has the four roots \(1, 3, 5, 7\).

00FB Proposition 7.6.8

Let \(R\) be commutative. A power series \(f = \sum a_i x^i \in R[[x]]\) is a unit if and only if \(a_0\) is a unit of \(R\).

Proof â–¼

If \(fg = 1\) then \(a_0 b_0 = 1\). Conversely, if \(a_0\) is a unit define \(b_0 = a_0^{-1}\) and recursively \(b_k = -a_0^{-1}\sum _{i=1}^k a_i b_{k-i}\); then \(\sum _{i+j=k} a_i b_j = 0\) for \(k \geq 1\).

00FC Definition 7.6.9

Let \(R\) be a UFD. A greatest common divisor of elements of \(R\) is a common divisor divisible by every common divisor; it exists (take the product of the common prime factors with their minimal multiplicities) and is unique up to associates. The content \(c(f)\) of a nonzero \(f \in R[x]\) is a greatest common divisor of its coefficients, and \(f\) is primitive if \(c(f)\) is a unit.

00FD Lemma 7.6.10 Gauss

Let \(R\) be a UFD. A product of primitive polynomials is primitive, and \(c(fg) = c(f)c(g)\) up to associates.

Proof â–¼

If \(f\), \(g\) are primitive and a prime \(p\) divides all coefficients of \(fg\), then the images of \(f\) and \(g\) in \((R/(p))[x]\) are nonzero but their product is zero; this is impossible because \(R/(p)\) is a domain, hence so is \((R/(p))[x]\) (Proposition 7.6.4). In general write \(f = c(f)f_0\) and \(g = c(g)g_0\) with \(f_0\), \(g_0\) primitive; then \(fg = c(f)c(g) f_0 g_0\) with \(f_0g_0\) primitive.

00FE Theorem 7.6.11

Let \(R\) be a UFD with fraction field \(K\).

  1. A primitive polynomial \(f \in R[x]\) of positive degree is irreducible in \(R[x]\) if and only if it is irreducible in \(K[x]\).

  2. \(R[x]\) is a UFD, whose prime elements are the prime elements of \(R\) and the primitive polynomials of positive degree that are irreducible in \(K[x]\).

Consequently \(\mathbb {Z}[x_1, \ldots , x_n]\) and \(k[x_1, \ldots , x_n]\) for a field \(k\) are UFDs.

Proof â–¼

Every \(h \in K[x]\), \(h \neq 0\), can be written \(h = a h_0\) with \(a \in K^\times \) and \(h_0 \in R[x]\) primitive (clear denominators, then divide by the content), and if also \(h = a' h_0'\) then \(h_0' = u h_0\) with \(u \in R^\times \): writing \(a/a' = b/b'\) with \(b, b' \in R\), \(b h_0 = b' h_0'\) and comparing contents gives \(b = b'u\).

(1) If \(f = gh\) in \(K[x]\) with \(\deg g, \deg h {\gt} 0\), write \(g = ag_0\), \(h = bh_0\); then \(f = ab\, g_0h_0\) with \(g_0h_0\) primitive (Lemma 7.6.10), so \(ab \in R^\times \) by the uniqueness just shown, and \(f = (ab\, g_0)h_0\) is a factorisation in \(R[x]\) into polynomials of positive degree. Conversely a factorisation in \(R[x]\) of a primitive polynomial into nonunits has both factors of positive degree (a constant factor would divide the content), hence is a factorisation in \(K[x]\).

(2) A prime \(p\) of \(R\) is prime in \(R[x]\) since \(R[x]/(p) \cong (R/(p))[x]\) is a domain. Let \(f\) be primitive of positive degree and irreducible in \(K[x]\), and suppose \(f \mid gh\) in \(R[x]\) with \(g, h \neq 0\). Since \(K[x]\) is a PID, \(f\) is prime in \(K[x]\), so say \(g = fq\) with \(q \in K[x]\). Write \(q = aq_0\) with \(a = c/c'\), \(c, c' \in R\) and \(q_0 \in R[x]\) primitive. Then \(c'g = c\, fq_0\), and comparing contents using Lemma 7.6.10 gives \(c = c'c(g)u\) with \(u \in R^\times \), so \(a \in R\) and \(q \in R[x]\). Hence \(f\) is prime in \(R[x]\).

Every irreducible element of \(R[x]\) is of one of these two kinds: an irreducible of degree \(0\) is irreducible in \(R\), hence prime in \(R\); an irreducible \(f\) of positive degree is primitive (otherwise \(c(f)\) would be a nonunit factor), hence irreducible in \(K[x]\) by (1). Every nonzero nonunit of \(R[x]\) is a product of irreducibles: write \(f = c(f) f_0\), factor \(c(f)\) in \(R\), and factor \(f_0\) in \(K[x]\) into irreducibles, which by the first paragraph and (1) can be taken primitive in \(R[x]\), the remaining constant being a unit of \(R\) by Lemma 7.6.10. By Lemma 7.5.3, \(R[x]\) is a UFD. The last statement follows by induction from \(\mathbb {Z}\) and \(k\) being UFDs.

00FF Proposition 7.6.12 Eisenstein’s criterion

Let \(R\) be a UFD with fraction field \(K\), \(f = a_nx^n + \cdots + a_0 \in R[x]\) with \(n \geq 1\), and \(p\) a prime of \(R\) with \(p \nmid a_n\), \(p \mid a_i\) for \(i {\lt} n\) and \(p^2 \nmid a_0\). Then \(f\) is irreducible in \(K[x]\).

Proof â–¼

By Theorem 7.6.11(1) applied to \(f/c(f)\) (note \(p \nmid c(f)\)) it suffices to exclude \(f = gh\) in \(R[x]\) with \(\deg g, \deg h \geq 1\). Reducing modulo \(p\) gives \(\bar g \bar h = \bar a_n x^n\) in \((R/(p))[x]\), which embeds in \(L[x]\) for \(L = \mathrm{Frac}(R/(p))\). By unique factorisation in \(L[x]\), \(\bar g = bx^k\) and \(\bar h = cx^{n-k}\) with \(k = \deg g\) and \(n - k = \deg h\) both positive. So \(p\) divides the constant terms of \(g\) and \(h\), and \(p^2 \mid a_0\), a contradiction.

00FG Counterexample 7.6.13

A UFD need not be a PID: in \(\mathbb {Z}[x]\) the ideal \((2, x)\) is not principal. If \((2, x) = (f)\), then \(f \mid 2\), so \(f = \pm 1\) or \(f = \pm 2\). But \(2 \nmid x\), and \(1 \notin (2, x)\) since every element of \((2, x)\) has even constant term.
