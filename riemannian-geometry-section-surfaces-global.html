39.10 Global theorems on surfaces02IL

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

This section collects the classical global theorems on surfaces, in the form in which they appear in [dC76, Chapters 4 and 5]: the Gauss–Bonnet theorem for regions with boundary, Minding’s theorem on surfaces of constant curvature, and the theorems of Hadamard, Liebmann and Hilbert on surfaces in \(\mathbb {R}^3\). Throughout, \(M\) is an oriented Riemannian surface and \(J\) is the rotation by \(+\pi /2\) in its tangent planes, as in the proof of Theorem 39.7.9; for a surface in \(\mathbb {R}^3\) oriented by a unit normal \(\nu \), \(Jv = \nu \times v\) (Proposition 35.3.4).

02IM Definition 39.10.1

Let \(\alpha \) be a curve in \(M\) parametrised by arc length, \(|\alpha '| = 1\). Since \(\langle D_s\alpha ', \alpha '\rangle = \frac12\frac{d}{ds}|\alpha '|^2 = 0\), the covariant acceleration is \(D_s\alpha ' = k_g\, J\alpha '\) for a function \(k_g\), the geodesic curvature of \(\alpha \). So \(\alpha \) is a geodesic if and only if \(k_g = 0\). For a surface in \(\mathbb {R}^3\), \(D_s\alpha '\) is the tangential part of the acceleration \(\alpha ''\) (Theorem 39.9.2), and \(k_g = \langle \alpha '', \nu \times \alpha '\rangle \).

For example, a circle of latitude at height \(z_0 \in (-1, 1)\) on the unit sphere has geodesic curvature \(\pm z_0/(1 - z_0^2)^{1/2}\), and the equator, \(z_0 = 0\), is a geodesic.

A simple region is a compact set \(R \subseteq M\) contained in the domain of a positively oriented chart \(x \colon \Omega \to M\), whose preimage is homeomorphic to a closed disc and bounded by a simple closed curve that is piecewise smooth with nonvanishing velocity. Its boundary \(\partial R\) is parametrised by arc length so that \(R\) lies on the left (that is, \(J\alpha '\) points into \(R\)), and at a vertex where the smooth arcs meet, the exterior angle \(\theta _i \in (-\pi , \pi )\) is the oriented angle from the incoming to the outgoing velocity; the interior angle is \(\pi - \theta _i\).

02IN Theorem 39.10.2 Theorem of turning tangents

Let \(R\) be a simple region, \((e_1, e_2)\) a positively oriented orthonormal frame on a neighbourhood of \(R\), and write the unit tangent of \(\partial R\) on each smooth arc as \(\alpha ' = \cos \varphi \, e_1 + \sin \varphi \, e_2\) with \(\varphi \) continuous on the arc. Then the sum over the arcs of the changes of \(\varphi \), plus the sum of the exterior angles, equals \(2\pi \).

This is Hopf’s Umlaufsatz [Hop35]; see [dC76, Chapters 4 and 5]. For a smooth convex curve in the Euclidean plane it says that the tangent turns exactly once.

02IO Theorem 39.10.3 Local Gauss–Bonnet theorem

Let \(R\) be a simple region with smooth boundary arcs \(\alpha _i\) and exterior angles \(\theta _1, \ldots , \theta _k\). Then

\[ \sum _i\int _{\alpha _i}k_g\, ds + \iint _RK\, dA + \sum _{j=1}^k\theta _j = 2\pi . \]
Proof â–¼

Applying Gram–Schmidt to the coordinate fields of the chart gives a positively oriented orthonormal frame \((e_1, e_2)\) near \(R\). Define the \(1\)-form \(\omega \) by \(\nabla _Xe_1 = \omega (X)e_2\); then \(\nabla _Xe_2 = -\omega (X)e_1\), and the computation in the proof of Theorem 39.7.9 gives \(d\omega = -K\, dA\). On a smooth arc, \(\alpha ' = \cos \varphi \, e_1 + \sin \varphi \, e_2\) with \(\varphi \) smooth (lift the map to the circle through the covering \(\mathbb {R}\to S^1\), Proposition 18.4.3), and \(J\alpha ' = -\sin \varphi \, e_1 + \cos \varphi \, e_2\). Differentiating,

\[ D_s\alpha ' = \varphi 'J\alpha ' + \cos \varphi \, \nabla _{\alpha '}e_1 + \sin \varphi \, \nabla _{\alpha '}e_2 = \varphi 'J\alpha ' + \omega (\alpha ')J\alpha ', \]

so \(k_g = \varphi ' + \omega (\alpha ')\). Summing over the arcs, \(\sum _i\int _{\alpha _i}k_g\, ds = \sum _i(\text{change of }\varphi \text{ on }\alpha _i) + \int _{\partial R}\omega \). The first sum is \(2\pi - \sum _j\theta _j\) by Theorem 39.10.2, and \(\int _{\partial R}\omega = \iint _Rd\omega = -\iint _RK\, dA\) by Stokes’ theorem in the chart, that is, Green’s theorem for a plane region bounded by a piecewise smooth simple closed curve [dC76, Chapter 4] (Theorem 35.4.4 in the smooth case).

02IP Corollary 39.10.4

Let \(T\) be a geodesic triangle: a simple region bounded by three geodesic arcs, with interior angles \(\phi _1, \phi _2, \phi _3\). Then

\[ \phi _1 + \phi _2 + \phi _3 - \pi = \iint _TK\, dA. \]

So the angles of a geodesic triangle add up to more than \(\pi \) where \(K {\gt} 0\), to \(\pi \) where \(K = 0\), and to less than \(\pi \) where \(K {\lt} 0\). On the unit sphere the area of a geodesic triangle is its angle excess; for instance the octant bounded by the three coordinate planes has three right angles and area \(\frac{3\pi }{2} - \pi = \frac{\pi }{2}\), one eighth of \(4\pi \).

Proof â–¼

Here \(k_g = 0\) on the sides and \(\theta _j = \pi - \phi _j\), so Theorem 39.10.3 gives \(\iint _TK\, dA + 3\pi - (\phi _1 + \phi _2 + \phi _3) = 2\pi \).

02IQ Theorem 39.10.5 Global Gauss–Bonnet theorem

Let \(R \subseteq M\) be a compact region whose boundary consists of finitely many simple closed piecewise smooth curves \(C_1, \ldots , C_m\), oriented with \(R\) on the left, with exterior angles \(\theta _1, \ldots , \theta _k\) at their vertices. Then

\[ \iint _RK\, dA + \sum _i\int _{C_i}k_g\, ds + \sum _j\theta _j = 2\pi \chi (R). \]
Sketch of proof â–¼

Choose a triangulation of \(R\) fine enough that every triangle is a simple region, with \(F\) triangles, \(E\) edges and \(V\) vertices, so that \(\chi (R) = V - E + F\). Apply Theorem 39.10.3 to each triangle and add. Every interior edge is traversed twice in opposite directions, so the integrals of \(k_g\) over interior edges cancel, and those over boundary edges give the boundary term. The exterior angles of a triangle are \(\pi \) minus its interior angles, and the interior angles of all triangles add up to \(2\pi \) at each of the \(V_{\mathrm{int}}\) interior vertices, to \(\pi - \theta _j\) at a corner of \(\partial R\), and to \(\pi \) at the other boundary vertices. So the sum of all exterior angles of the triangles is \(3\pi F - 2\pi V_{\mathrm{int}} - \pi V_\partial + \sum _j\theta _j\), where \(V_\partial \) is the number of boundary vertices, and adding the local formulas gives \(\iint _RK\, dA + \sum _i\int _{C_i}k_g\, ds + \sum _j\theta _j = -\pi F + 2\pi V_{\mathrm{int}} + \pi V_\partial \). Each triangle has three edges, each interior edge lies in two triangles and each boundary edge in one, so \(3F = 2E_{\mathrm{int}} + E_\partial \), and \(E_\partial = V_\partial \) because the boundary is a union of closed curves; with these relations the right side equals \(2\pi (V - E + F)\). The omitted verification is the existence of such triangulations [dC76, Chapter 4].

For \(R = M\) a closed surface there is no boundary, and the formula is Theorem 39.7.9, \(\iint _MK\, dA = 2\pi \chi (M)\). For a disc in the plane bounded by a smooth convex curve, \(K = 0\) and \(\chi = 1\), and the formula says that the total geodesic curvature of the boundary is \(2\pi \): the tangent turns once. For the northern cap of the unit sphere above the latitude \(z_0\), the area \(2\pi (1 - z_0)\) plus the total geodesic curvature \(2\pi (1 - z_0^2)^{1/2} \cdot z_0/(1 - z_0^2)^{1/2} = 2\pi z_0\) of the boundary circle gives \(2\pi = 2\pi \chi (\text{disc})\).

Many metrics on surfaces are given as a function times the Euclidean metric, and for them the Gaussian curvature has a simple formula.

02KV Proposition 39.10.6 Conformal metrics on surfaces

Let \(\Omega \subseteq \mathbb {R}^2\) be open, \(u \colon \Omega \to \mathbb {R}\) smooth, and \(g = e^{2u}(dx^2 + dy^2)\). Then the Gaussian curvature of \(g\) is

\[ K = -e^{-2u}\Delta u, \qquad \Delta = \partial _x^2 + \partial _y^2 . \]
Proof â–¼

Here \(g_{ij} = e^{2u}\delta _{ij}\), and the formula of Theorem 39.2.6 gives \(\Gamma ^k_{ij} = u_i\delta _{jk} + u_j\delta _{ik} - u_k\delta _{ij}\), where \(u_1 = u_x\) and \(u_2 = u_y\). So \(\nabla _{\partial _x}\partial _x = u_x\partial _x - u_y\partial _y\) and \(\nabla _{\partial _y}\partial _x = u_y\partial _x + u_x\partial _y\). The fields \(e_1 = e^{-u}\partial _x\), \(e_2 = e^{-u}\partial _y\) form a positively oriented orthonormal frame, and

\[ \nabla _{\partial _x}e_1 = -u_xe^{-u}\partial _x + e^{-u}(u_x\partial _x - u_y\partial _y) = -u_y\, e_2, \qquad \nabla _{\partial _y}e_1 = -u_ye^{-u}\partial _x + e^{-u}(u_y\partial _x + u_x\partial _y) = u_x\, e_2 . \]

So the \(1\)-form \(\omega \) with \(\nabla _Xe_1 = \omega (X)e_2\) is \(\omega = -u_y\, dx + u_x\, dy\), and \(d\omega = \Delta u\, dx \wedge dy\). The computation in the proof of Theorem 39.7.9 gives \(d\omega = -K\, dA = -Ke^{2u}\, dx \wedge dy\), whence the formula.

02KW Example 39.10.7

  1. The hyperbolic plane. On \(H = \{ (x, y) : y {\gt} 0\} \) with \((dx^2 + dy^2)/y^2\), \(u = -\log y\) and \(\Delta u = 1/y^2\), so \(K = -1\).

  2. The Poincaré disc. On the unit disc \(\mathbb {D}\) with \(4(dx^2 + dy^2)/(1 - x^2 - y^2)^2\), \(u = \log 2 - \log (1 - r^2)\) with \(r^2 = x^2 + y^2\). For a radial function \(\Delta = \partial _r^2 + r^{-1}\partial _r\), and \(\Delta \log (1 - r^2) = -4/(1 - r^2)^2\), so \(K = -\frac{(1 - r^2)^2}4 \cdot \frac4{(1 - r^2)^2} = -1\). The Cayley map \(w = i(1 + z)/(1 - z)\) is an isometry from the disc onto \(H\): with \(z = x + iy\) and \(w\) written as a complex number, \(|dw/dz| = 2/|1 - z|^2\) and \(\operatorname {Im}w = (1 - |z|^2)/|1 - z|^2\), so \(|dw|/\operatorname {Im}w = 2|dz|/(1 - |z|^2)\).

  3. The sphere. In the chart of stereographic projection (Example 17.9.4) the round metric of \(S^2\) is \(4(dx^2 + dy^2)/(1 + x^2 + y^2)^2\), by a direct computation; here \(\Delta \log (1 + r^2) = 4/(1 + r^2)^2\), and \(K = 1\), as it should.

02IR Theorem 39.10.8 Minding; Cartan

Let \((M, g)\) and \((N, h)\) be Riemannian manifolds of the same dimension \(n\) with the same constant sectional curvature \(\kappa \), and let \(p \in M\), \(q \in N\) and a linear isometry \(I \colon T_pM \to T_qN\) be given. Then there are neighbourhoods \(U \ni p\) and \(V \ni q\) and an isometry \(F \colon U \to V\) with \(F(p) = q\) and \(d_pF = I\). In particular two surfaces with the same constant Gaussian curvature are locally isometric, and every surface of constant curvature \(0\), \(1\) or \(-1\) is locally isometric to the plane, the unit sphere or the hyperbolic plane.

Proof â–¼

Let \(s_\kappa \) be the solution of \(s'' + \kappa s = 0\) with \(s(0) = 0\) and \(s'(0) = 1\) (so \(s_\kappa (t) = t\), \(\sin (\sqrt\kappa t)/\sqrt\kappa \) or \(\sinh (\sqrt{-\kappa }t)/\sqrt{-\kappa }\)). We first compute the differential of \(\exp _p\). Let \(v \in T_pM\) be in the domain of \(\exp _p\), \(v \neq 0\), \(\ell = |v|\), \(u = v/\ell \), and \(\gamma (t) = \exp _p(tv)\); for \(w \in T_pM\) write \(w = w_\parallel + w_\perp \) with \(w_\parallel \) a multiple of \(u\) and \(w_\perp \perp u\), and let \(P_t\) be parallel transport along \(\gamma \). By Proposition 39.7.6(2), \(J(t) = d_{tv}\exp _p(tw)\) is the Jacobi field along \(\gamma \) with \(J(0) = 0\) and \(D_tJ(0) = w\). The fields \(J_1(t) = tP_t(w_\parallel )\) and \(J_2(t) = \ell ^{-1}s_\kappa (\ell t)P_t(w_\perp )\) are Jacobi fields: \(P_t(u) = \dot\gamma (t)/\ell \), so \(J_1\) is a multiple of \(t\dot\gamma \), with \(D_t^2J_1 = 0\) and \(R(J_1, \dot\gamma )\dot\gamma = 0\); and \(P_t(w_\perp ) \perp \dot\gamma \), so by the curvature formula of Example 39.7.3, \(R(J_2, \dot\gamma )\dot\gamma = \kappa |\dot\gamma |^2J_2 = \kappa \ell ^2J_2\), while \(D_t^2J_2 = \ell s_\kappa ''(\ell t)P_t(w_\perp ) = -\kappa \ell ^2J_2\). They have the initial values \(J_1(0) = J_2(0) = 0\), \(D_tJ_1(0) = w_\parallel \) and \(D_tJ_2(0) = w_\perp \), so \(J = J_1 + J_2\) by uniqueness, and since \(P_1\) is an isometry and \(P_1(w_\parallel ) \perp P_1(w_\perp )\),

\[ |d_v\exp _p(w)|^2 = |J(1)|^2 = |w_\parallel |^2 + \Bigl(\frac{s_\kappa (\ell )}{\ell }\Bigr)^2|w_\perp |^2. \]

The same formula holds on \(N\). Now choose \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\) such that \(\exp _p\) and \(\exp _q\) map the balls of radius \(\varepsilon \) diffeomorphically onto open sets \(U\) and \(V\) (Proposition 39.5.1), and let \(F = \exp _q \circ I \circ (\exp _p|_{B_\varepsilon })^{-1} \colon U \to V\), a diffeomorphism with \(F(p) = q\) and \(d_pF = I\). For \(x = \exp _p(v)\) and \(w \in T_pM\), the chain rule gives \(d_xF(d_v\exp _p(w)) = d_{Iv}\exp _q(Iw)\), and since \(I\) preserves lengths and orthogonality, the formula above gives the same length for \(d_v\exp _p(w)\) and \(d_{Iv}\exp _q(Iw)\) (for \(v = 0\) both differentials are the identity). As the \(d_v\exp _p(w)\) fill \(T_xM\), \(d_xF\) preserves lengths, hence inner products, so \(F\) is an isometry.

For instance the cylinder and the cone (without its vertex) are locally isometric to the plane, and the pseudosphere, the surface of revolution of the tractrix, to the hyperbolic plane. Minding’s theorem is local: the flat torus \(\mathbb {R}^2/\mathbb {Z}^2\) is locally but not globally isometric to the plane.

02IS Lemma 39.10.9

A compact surface \(S \subseteq \mathbb {R}^3\) has a point where \(K {\gt} 0\). More precisely, if \(p_0 \in S\) maximises the distance \(R\) to the origin, then \(K(p_0) \geq 1/R^2\).

Proof â–¼

The function \(|x|^2\) on \(S\) has a maximum \(R^2 {\gt} 0\) at some \(p_0\), since \(S\) is compact (and not a point). Its differential \(2\langle p_0, -\rangle \) vanishes on \(T_{p_0}S\), so \(\nu = p_0/R\) is a unit normal at \(p_0\). For a unit-speed curve \(\alpha \) in \(S\) with \(\alpha (0) = p_0\), the function \(|\alpha |^2\) has a maximum at \(0\), so \(0 \geq (|\alpha |^2)''(0) = 2\langle \alpha ''(0), p_0\rangle + 2|\alpha '(0)|^2\), that is, \(\langle \alpha ''(0), \nu \rangle \leq -1/R\). The normal part of \(\alpha ''(0)\) is \(\mathrm{II}(\alpha '(0), \alpha '(0))\) (Theorem 39.9.2, applied to the covariant acceleration of \(\alpha \) in \(\mathbb {R}^3\)), so \(h(w, w) \leq -1/R\) for every unit tangent vector \(w\) at \(p_0\). Both principal curvatures are therefore \(\leq -1/R\), and \(K(p_0) = \kappa _1\kappa _2 \geq 1/R^2\) (Corollary 39.9.8).

So there is no compact surface in \(\mathbb {R}^3\) with \(K \leq 0\) everywhere; in particular the flat torus cannot be embedded isometrically in \(\mathbb {R}^3\) as a smooth surface.

02IT Lemma 39.10.10

Let \(f \colon X \to Y\) be a local homeomorphism from a compact Hausdorff space to a connected Hausdorff space. Then \(f\) is surjective and a covering map with finite fibres.

Proof â–¼

A local homeomorphism is open, and a continuous map from a compact space to a Hausdorff space is closed (Proposition 17.6.2); so \(f(X)\) is open, closed and nonempty, hence equal to \(Y\). A fibre \(f^{-1}(y)\) is discrete, because \(f\) is injective near each point, and closed in the compact \(X\), hence finite, say \(\{ x_1, \ldots , x_k\} \). Choose pairwise disjoint open sets \(U_i \ni x_i\) mapped homeomorphically onto open sets \(V_i \ni y\). The set \(f(X \setminus \bigcup _iU_i)\) is compact, hence closed, and does not contain \(y\), so \(V = \bigcap _iV_i \setminus f(X \setminus \bigcup _iU_i)\) is an open neighbourhood of \(y\) with \(f^{-1}(V) = \bigsqcup _i(U_i \cap f^{-1}(V))\), each piece mapped homeomorphically onto \(V\).

02IU Theorem 39.10.11 Hadamard

Let \(S \subseteq \mathbb {R}^3\) be a compact connected surface with Gaussian curvature \(K {\gt} 0\) everywhere. Then \(S\) is orientable, its Gauss map \(\nu \colon S \to S^2\) (for the unit normal making the second fundamental form negative definite) is a diffeomorphism, \(S\) is diffeomorphic to \(S^2\), and \(\iint _SK\, dA = 4\pi \).

Proof â–¼

At each point \(K = \det S_\nu {\gt} 0\), so the self-adjoint shape operator is definite, and exactly one of the two unit normals makes it negative definite; near each point this normal is \(\pm \) a smooth local normal with a locally constant sign, so it is a unit normal field, and \(S\) is orientable (Proposition 35.3.4). For tangent \(X\), \(\overline{\nabla }_X\nu \) is tangent, because \(\langle \nu , \nu \rangle = 1\), so \(d_p\nu (X) = \overline{\nabla }_X\nu = -S_\nu X\) (Proposition 39.9.5); as \(T_{\nu (p)}S^2 = \nu (p)^\perp = T_pS\) and \(\det (-S_\nu ) = K {\gt} 0\), \(d_p\nu \) is an isomorphism, and \(\nu \) is a local diffeomorphism (Theorem 32.4.14). By Lemma 39.10.10 it is a covering map, and since \(S^2\) is simply connected (Corollary 18.5.2) and \(S\) connected, it has one sheet (Corollary 18.4.4(3)): \(\nu \) is a bijective local diffeomorphism, hence a diffeomorphism. Orient \(S\) by \(\nu \) and \(S^2\) by its outward normal; then \(T_pS\) and \(T_{\nu (p)}S^2\) are the same oriented plane, \(\nu ^*(dA_{S^2}) = \det (d\nu )\, dA_S = K\, dA_S\), and \(\iint _SK\, dA = \int _{S^2}dA = 4\pi \) (Proposition 35.4.2).

The value \(4\pi \) agrees with the Gauss–Bonnet theorem, \(2\pi \chi (S^2)\). Hadamard also proved that such a surface is the boundary of a convex body: it lies on one side of each of its tangent planes [dC76, Chapter 5].

02IV Theorem 39.10.12 Liebmann

A compact connected surface in \(\mathbb {R}^3\) with constant Gaussian curvature \(K\) is a round sphere of radius \(1/\sqrt{K}\).

Sketch of proof â–¼

By Lemma 39.10.9 the constant \(K\) is positive. With the unit normal \(\nu \) of Theorem 39.10.11 the principal curvatures are negative; write them as \(-\lambda _1 \leq -\lambda _2\), so that \(\lambda _1 \geq \lambda _2 {\gt} 0\) and \(\lambda _1\lambda _2 = K\). The continuous function \(\lambda _1\) attains its maximum at some \(p\), where \(\lambda _2 = K/\lambda _1\) attains its minimum. Hilbert’s lemma, a computation with the Codazzi equation in principal coordinates, says that at a non-umbilic point (\(\lambda _1 {\gt} \lambda _2\)) where \(\lambda _1\) has a local maximum and \(\lambda _2\) a local minimum, \(K \leq 0\). So \(p\) is umbilic and \(\lambda _1(p) = \sqrt K\). Hence \(\lambda _1 \leq \sqrt K\) everywhere, while \(\lambda _1^2 \geq \lambda _1\lambda _2 = K\); so \(\lambda _1 = \lambda _2 = \sqrt K\) everywhere, every point is umbilic, and \(S_\nu = -\sqrt K\, \mathrm{id}\). Then \(d\nu = -S_\nu = \sqrt K\, \mathrm{id}\), so \(x - \nu /\sqrt K\) is constant on the connected surface \(S\): every point lies at distance \(1/\sqrt K\) from this fixed point. The omitted verification is Hilbert’s lemma [dC76, Chapter 5].

02IW Theorem 39.10.13 Hilbert

There is no complete surface of constant negative Gaussian curvature isometrically immersed in \(\mathbb {R}^3\). In particular the hyperbolic plane cannot be realised as a complete surface in \(\mathbb {R}^3\).

This is [Hil01]; see [dC76, Chapter 5] for a complete proof. In outline: on such a surface the asymptotic directions (where \(h(w, w) = 0\)) give coordinates in which the metric has the form \(du^2 + 2\cos \theta \, du\, dv + dv^2\), and the Gauss equation becomes the sine-Gordon equation \(\theta _{uv} = \sin \theta \); these coordinates cover the whole hyperbolic plane, whose area is infinite, while the Gauss–Bonnet theorem bounds the area of every coordinate quadrilateral by \(2\pi \), a contradiction. Incomplete surfaces of constant negative curvature do exist, such as the pseudosphere; and by Theorem 39.10.8 each is locally isometric to the hyperbolic plane.
