12.10 Root systems and the classification02R1

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

The properties (R1)–(R4) of Theorem 12.9.7 are purely Euclidean, and they turn out to determine the Lie algebra.

02R2 Definition 12.10.1

A root system in a finite-dimensional real inner product space \(E\) is a subset \(\Phi \) satisfying (R1)–(R4) of Theorem 12.9.7, where for \(\alpha \neq 0\)

\[ \langle \beta , \alpha \rangle = \frac{2(\beta , \alpha )}{(\alpha , \alpha )}, \qquad \sigma _\alpha (\beta ) = \beta - \langle \beta , \alpha \rangle \alpha . \]

Its rank is \(\dim E\). The Weyl group \(W\) is the subgroup of the orthogonal group of \(E\) generated by the reflections \(\sigma _\alpha \), \(\alpha \in \Phi \). An isomorphism of root systems \((E, \Phi ) \to (E^{\prime }, \Phi ^{\prime })\) is a linear isomorphism \(\phi \) with \(\phi (\Phi ) = \Phi ^{\prime }\) and \(\langle \phi \beta , \phi \alpha \rangle = \langle \beta , \alpha \rangle \). \(\Phi \) is irreducible if it is not the union of two nonempty subsets orthogonal to each other.

\(\sigma _\alpha \) is the orthogonal reflection in the hyperplane \(\alpha ^\perp \): it fixes \(\alpha ^\perp \) and sends \(\alpha \) to \(-\alpha \). By (R3) every \(w \in W\) permutes \(\Phi \), and since \(\Phi \) spans \(E\), \(w\) is determined by this permutation; so \(W\) is finite. Note that \(\langle \beta , \alpha \rangle \) is linear in \(\beta \) but not in \(\alpha \). The vectors \(\alpha ^\vee = 2\alpha /(\alpha , \alpha )\) form a root system as well, the dual root system; for the root system of a Lie algebra, \(\alpha ^\vee \) corresponds to the coroot \(h_\alpha \) under the identification of \(\mathfrak {h}\) with \(\mathfrak {h}^*\).

02R3 Lemma 12.10.2

Let \(\alpha , \beta \in \Phi \) with \(\beta \neq \pm \alpha \), and \(\theta \) the angle between them. Then \(\langle \alpha , \beta \rangle \langle \beta , \alpha \rangle = 4\cos ^2\theta \in \{ 0, 1, 2, 3\} \). If \((\beta , \beta ) \geq (\alpha , \alpha )\) and \((\alpha , \beta ) \neq 0\), then \(\langle \alpha , \beta \rangle = \pm 1\) and \((\beta , \beta )/(\alpha , \alpha ) = |\langle \beta , \alpha \rangle | \in \{ 1, 2, 3\} \); so the possible pairs are

\[ \theta = \tfrac {\pi }{2},\ \tfrac {\pi }{3},\ \tfrac {2\pi }{3},\ \tfrac {\pi }{4},\ \tfrac {3\pi }{4},\ \tfrac {\pi }{6},\ \tfrac {5\pi }{6}, \qquad \frac{(\beta , \beta )}{(\alpha , \alpha )} = \text{any},\ 1,\ 1,\ 2,\ 2,\ 3,\ 3 . \]

Moreover, if \((\alpha , \beta ) {\gt} 0\) then \(\alpha - \beta \in \Phi \), and if \((\alpha , \beta ) {\lt} 0\) then \(\alpha + \beta \in \Phi \).

Proof â–¼

\(\langle \alpha , \beta \rangle \langle \beta , \alpha \rangle = 4(\alpha , \beta )^2/((\alpha , \alpha )(\beta , \beta )) = 4\cos ^2\theta \) is an integer by (R4), and it is less than \(4\) because \(\alpha \) and \(\beta \) are not proportional (by (R2)), so the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality is strict. The two integers \(\langle \alpha , \beta \rangle \) and \(\langle \beta , \alpha \rangle \) have the sign of \((\alpha , \beta )\), and \(|\langle \alpha , \beta \rangle | \leq |\langle \beta , \alpha \rangle |\) when \((\beta , \beta ) \geq (\alpha , \alpha )\); if their product is \(1\), \(2\) or \(3\), the smaller one is \(\pm 1\), and \(\langle \beta , \alpha \rangle /\langle \alpha , \beta \rangle = (\beta , \beta )/(\alpha , \alpha )\). If \((\alpha , \beta ) {\gt} 0\), one of \(\langle \alpha , \beta \rangle \), \(\langle \beta , \alpha \rangle \) is \(1\): in the first case \(\sigma _\beta (\alpha ) = \alpha - \beta \in \Phi \), and in the second \(\sigma _\alpha (\beta ) = \beta - \alpha \in \Phi \), so \(\alpha - \beta = \sigma _{\alpha - \beta }(\beta - \alpha ) \in \Phi \). If \((\alpha , \beta ) {\lt} 0\), apply this to \(\alpha \) and \(-\beta = \sigma _\beta (\beta )\).

02R4 Example 12.10.3

In rank \(1\) the only root system is \(A_1 = \{ \pm \alpha \} \), by (R2). In rank \(2\) there are four, up to isomorphism [Hum72, Section 9.3]; one checks (R1)–(R4) directly.

  1. \(A_1 \times A_1 = \{ \pm e_1, \pm e_2\} \) in \(\mathbb {R}^2\), reducible; it is the root system of \(\mathfrak {sl}_2 \times \mathfrak {sl}_2\).

  2. \(A_2 = \{ \pm (\varepsilon _1 - \varepsilon _2), \pm (\varepsilon _2 - \varepsilon _3), \pm (\varepsilon _1 - \varepsilon _3)\} \) in the plane \(x_1 + x_2 + x_3 = 0\) of \(\mathbb {R}^3\): six roots of equal length at angles \(\pi /3\), the root system of \(\mathfrak {sl}_3\) (Example 12.9.8).

  3. \(B_2 = \{ \pm e_1, \pm e_2, \pm e_1 \pm e_2\} \): four short and four long roots, at angles \(\pi /4\); it is also called \(C_2\), the root system of \(\mathfrak {sp}_4\) (Exercise 12.12.11).

  4. \(G_2\): in the plane \(x_1 + x_2 + x_3 = 0\), the six short roots of \(A_2\) together with the six long roots \(\pm (2\varepsilon _1 - \varepsilon _2 - \varepsilon _3)\), \(\pm (2\varepsilon _2 - \varepsilon _1 - \varepsilon _3)\), \(\pm (2\varepsilon _3 - \varepsilon _1 - \varepsilon _2)\); consecutive roots make angles \(\pi /6\), and long roots are \(\sqrt3\) times longer than short ones.

02R5 Definition 12.10.4

A base of a root system \(\Phi \) is a subset \(\Delta \) that is a basis of \(E\) such that every root is \(\beta = \sum _{\alpha \in \Delta }k_\alpha \alpha \) with integer coefficients \(k_\alpha \) that are either all \(\geq 0\) or all \(\leq 0\). The elements of \(\Delta \) are the simple roots, the roots with all \(k_\alpha \geq 0\) are positive (\(\beta {\gt} 0\)), and they form a set \(\Phi ^+\) with \(\Phi = \Phi ^+ \sqcup -\Phi ^+\). The height of \(\beta \) is \(\sum _\alpha k_\alpha \).

02R6 Theorem 12.10.5

Let \(\gamma \in E\) be regular, that is, \((\gamma , \alpha ) \neq 0\) for all \(\alpha \in \Phi \) (such \(\gamma \) exist, see the end of the proof). Let \(\Phi ^+(\gamma ) = \{ \alpha \in \Phi : (\gamma , \alpha ) {\gt} 0\} \), and let \(\Delta (\gamma )\) be the set of \(\alpha \in \Phi ^+(\gamma )\) that are not the sum of two elements of \(\Phi ^+(\gamma )\). Then \(\Delta (\gamma )\) is a base, with positive roots \(\Phi ^+(\gamma )\), and \((\alpha , \beta ) \leq 0\) for distinct \(\alpha , \beta \in \Delta (\gamma )\).

Proof â–¼

Every \(\beta \in \Phi ^+(\gamma )\) is a non-negative integer combination of \(\Delta (\gamma )\). Otherwise choose such a \(\beta \) with \((\gamma , \beta )\) minimal. It is not in \(\Delta (\gamma )\), so \(\beta = \beta _1 + \beta _2\) with \(\beta _i \in \Phi ^+(\gamma )\); as \((\gamma , \beta ) = (\gamma , \beta _1) + (\gamma , \beta _2)\) with both terms positive, each \((\gamma , \beta _i) {\lt} (\gamma , \beta )\), so each \(\beta _i\) is such a combination, and so is \(\beta \).

\((\alpha , \beta ) \leq 0\) for distinct \(\alpha , \beta \in \Delta (\gamma )\). Otherwise \(\alpha - \beta \in \Phi \) by Lemma 12.10.2, and \(\alpha - \beta \) or \(\beta - \alpha \) lies in \(\Phi ^+(\gamma )\); then \(\alpha = \beta + (\alpha - \beta )\) or \(\beta = \alpha + (\beta - \alpha )\) is decomposable.

\(\Delta (\gamma )\) is linearly independent. Suppose \(\sum _\alpha r_\alpha \alpha = 0\), and separate the terms: \(\varepsilon = \sum _{\alpha \in A}s_\alpha \alpha = \sum _{\beta \in B}t_\beta \beta \) with disjoint \(A\), \(B \subseteq \Delta (\gamma )\) and \(s_\alpha , t_\beta {\gt} 0\). Then \((\varepsilon , \varepsilon ) = \sum s_\alpha t_\beta (\alpha , \beta ) \leq 0\), so \(\varepsilon = 0\); and \(0 = (\gamma , \varepsilon ) = \sum s_\alpha (\gamma , \alpha )\) with all \((\gamma , \alpha ) {\gt} 0\) forces \(A = \emptyset \), and similarly \(B = \emptyset \).

Since \(\Phi = \Phi ^+(\gamma ) \sqcup -\Phi ^+(\gamma )\) spans \(E\), so does \(\Delta (\gamma )\), and it is a base. Regular elements exist: in coordinates, \(\gamma \mapsto \prod _{\alpha \in \Phi }(\gamma , \alpha )\) is a product of nonzero linear polynomials, hence a nonzero polynomial, and a nonzero real polynomial does not vanish everywhere on \(\mathbb {R}^l\).

02R7 Lemma 12.10.6

Let \(\Delta \) be a base and \(\alpha \in \Delta \). Then \(\sigma _\alpha \) permutes \(\Phi ^+ \setminus \{ \alpha \} \). Consequently \(\rho = \frac12\sum _{\beta \in \Phi ^+}\beta \) satisfies \(\sigma _\alpha (\rho ) = \rho - \alpha \) and \(\langle \rho , \alpha \rangle = 1\) for every simple root \(\alpha \).

Proof â–¼

Let \(\beta = \sum _{\delta \in \Delta }k_\delta \delta \in \Phi ^+\), \(\beta \neq \alpha \). By (R2), \(\beta \) is not a multiple of \(\alpha \), so \(k_\delta {\gt} 0\) for some \(\delta \neq \alpha \). The coefficient of \(\delta \) in \(\sigma _\alpha (\beta ) = \beta - \langle \beta , \alpha \rangle \alpha \) is still \(k_\delta {\gt} 0\), so \(\sigma _\alpha (\beta )\) is a positive root, different from \(\alpha \). As \(\sigma _\alpha \) is injective and \(\sigma _\alpha (\alpha ) = -\alpha \), \(\sigma _\alpha (2\rho ) = 2\rho - 2\alpha \). Comparing with \(\sigma _\alpha (\rho ) = \rho - \langle \rho , \alpha \rangle \alpha \) gives \(\langle \rho , \alpha \rangle = 1\).

Further facts about bases, which we quote [Hum72, Sections 10.1 and 10.3]: every base is \(\Delta (\gamma )\) for some regular \(\gamma \); the Weyl group acts simply transitively on the set of bases and is generated by the simple reflections \(\sigma _\alpha \), \(\alpha \in \Delta \); and every root is \(W\)-conjugate to a simple root.

02R8 Definition 12.10.7

Let \(\Delta = \{ \alpha _1, \ldots , \alpha _l\} \) be a base. The Cartan matrix is the integer matrix \((\langle \alpha _i, \alpha _j\rangle )_{i,j}\). Its diagonal entries are \(2\), and by Theorem 12.10.5 and Lemma 12.10.2 its other entries lie in \(\{ 0, -1, -2, -3\} \). The Dynkin diagram has a vertex for each simple root, and \(\alpha _i\) and \(\alpha _j\) (\(i \neq j\)) are joined by \(\langle \alpha _i, \alpha _j\rangle \langle \alpha _j, \alpha _i\rangle \in \{ 0, 1, 2, 3\} \) edges; when two joined roots have different lengths, the edges carry an arrow pointing to the shorter one.

By Lemma 12.10.2 the Dynkin diagram determines the Cartan matrix, and the Cartan matrix determines the root system up to isomorphism, because \(\Phi \) is the set of images of the simple roots under the group generated by the simple reflections, and these act by \(\sigma _{\alpha _j}(\alpha _i) = \alpha _i - \langle \alpha _i, \alpha _j\rangle \alpha _j\) [Hum72, Section 11.1]. The root system is irreducible if and only if its Dynkin diagram is connected, and in general it is the orthogonal union of the irreducible root systems belonging to the connected components.

02R9 Theorem 12.10.8 Classification of root systems

The connected Dynkin diagrams are those of the following list, and each belongs to exactly one irreducible root system up to isomorphism [Hum72, Sections 11.4 and 12.1].

  1. \(A_l\) (\(l \geq 1\)), a chain of \(l\) vertices joined by single edges:

  2. \(B_l\) (\(l \geq 2\)), a chain whose last edge is double, with the arrow pointing to the last (short) root:

  3. \(C_l\) (\(l \geq 3\)), the same chain with the arrow reversed (the last root is long):

  4. \(D_l\) (\(l \geq 4\)), a chain of \(l - 2\) vertices whose last vertex is joined to two further vertices:

  5. \(E_6\), \(E_7\), \(E_8\), a chain of \(5\), \(6\), \(7\) vertices with one more vertex joined to the third one; for \(E_8\):

  6. \(F_4\), a chain of four vertices whose middle edge is double:

  7. \(G_2\), two vertices joined by a triple edge, with the arrow pointing to the short root.

The restrictions on \(l\) avoid repetitions: \(B_1 = C_1 = A_1\), \(C_2 = B_2\), \(D_2 = A_1 \times A_1\) and \(D_3 = A_3\). The proof is an exercise in Euclidean geometry: the simple roots, normalised to unit vectors \(u_i\), are linearly independent with \((u_i, u_j) \in \{ 0, -\frac12, -\frac{1}{\sqrt2}, -\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\} \) for \(i \neq j\), and positivity of \((\sum c_iu_i, \sum c_iu_i)\) for well-chosen coefficients excludes all other graphs.

02RA Theorem 12.10.9 Classification of semisimple Lie algebras

Let \(\mathfrak {g}\) be a semisimple Lie algebra over \(\mathbb {C}\) with Cartan subalgebra \(\mathfrak {h}\) and root system \(\Phi \).

  1. \(\mathfrak {g}\) is simple if and only if \(\Phi \) is irreducible; the simple ideals of \(\mathfrak {g}\) correspond to the irreducible components of \(\Phi \).

  2. Two semisimple Lie algebras are isomorphic if and only if their root systems are isomorphic.

  3. Every root system is the root system of a semisimple Lie algebra.

Consequently the simple Lie algebras over \(\mathbb {C}\) are, up to isomorphism, exactly one for each diagram of Theorem 12.10.8: the classical algebras \(\mathfrak {sl}_{l+1}\) (\(A_l\)), \(\mathfrak {so}_{2l+1}\) (\(B_l\)), \(\mathfrak {sp}_{2l}\) (\(C_l\)), \(\mathfrak {so}_{2l}\) (\(D_l\)), of dimensions \(l(l + 2)\), \(l(2l + 1)\), \(l(2l + 1)\), \(l(2l - 1)\), and five exceptional algebras \(\mathfrak {e}_6\), \(\mathfrak {e}_7\), \(\mathfrak {e}_8\), \(\mathfrak {f}_4\), \(\mathfrak {g}_2\) of dimensions \(78\), \(133\), \(248\), \(52\) and \(14\).

For (1) see [Hum72, Section 14.1], for (2) [Hum72, Sections 14 and 16], and for (3) [Hum72, Section 18], where \(\mathfrak {g}\) is constructed by generators \(x_i\), \(y_i\), \(h_i\) (\(1 \leq i \leq l\)) and relations read off from the Cartan matrix (Serre’s theorem). The dimension of \(\mathfrak {g}\) is \(|\Phi | + l\) (Theorem 12.9.4 and Theorem 12.9.6(1)); for instance \(A_l\) has \(l(l + 1)\) roots, by Example 12.9.8.
