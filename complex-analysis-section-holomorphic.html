29.2 Holomorphic functions0147

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

0148 Definition 29.2.1

Let \(U \subseteq \mathbb {C}\) be open. A function \(f \colon U \to \mathbb {C}\) is holomorphic if the limit \(f'(a) = \lim _{h \to 0}(f(a + h) - f(a))/h\), \(h \in \mathbb {C}^\times \), exists for every \(a \in U\). An entire function is one holomorphic on \(\mathbb {C}\).

The definition looks exactly like the one for real functions, but \(h\) now tends to \(0\) through all complex numbers, from every direction at once, and the difference quotient must have the same limit along all of them. This single requirement turns out to be extraordinarily strong: it implies that \(f\) has derivatives of all orders and is given by its Taylor series (Theorem 29.4.2 and Corollary 29.4.3). Before seeing why, let us compute a few derivatives directly from the definition.

036E Example 29.2.2

Let \(a \in \mathbb {C}\) and \(h \in \mathbb {C}^\times \) small.

  1. \(f(z) = z^n\), \(n \geq 1\). By the binomial theorem \((a + h)^n - a^n = na^{n-1}h + \sum _{k = 2}^n\binom nka^{n-k}h^k\), so the difference quotient is \(na^{n-1} + \sum _{k = 2}^n\binom nka^{n-k}h^{k-1} \to na^{n-1}\). So \((z^n)^{\prime } = nz^{n-1}\), as over \(\mathbb {R}\).

  2. \(f(z) = 1/z\) on \(\mathbb {C}^\times \). For \(a \neq 0\) and \(|h| {\lt} |a|\), \(\frac1h\bigl(\frac1{a + h} - \frac1a\bigr) = \frac{-1}{a(a + h)} \to -\frac1{a^2}\).

  3. \(f(z) = \bar z\). The difference quotient is \(\bar h/h\), which equals \(1\) for real \(h\) and \(-1\) for purely imaginary \(h\). So the limit exists at no point: \(\bar z\) is a perfectly smooth map of the plane, an isometry even, but it is nowhere complex differentiable. The same happens for \(\operatorname {Re}z\), whose quotient \(\operatorname {Re}h/h\) is \(1\) on the real axis and \(0\) on the imaginary axis.

  4. \(f(z) = |z|^2 = z\bar z\). Here \(|a + h|^2 - |a|^2 = \bar ah + a\bar h + |h|^2\), so the quotient is \(\bar a + a\, \bar h/h + \bar h\). For \(a = 0\) it is \(\bar h \to 0\); for \(a \neq 0\) the middle term \(a\, \bar h/h\) has no limit, by (3). So \(|z|^2\) is complex differentiable at \(0\) and nowhere else. A function can be complex differentiable at a single point; “holomorphic” asks for it at every point of an open set, and all the theory below depends on this.

0149 Proposition 29.2.3 Cauchy–Riemann

Identify \(\mathbb {C}\) with \(\mathbb {R}^2\) and write \(f = u + iv\). Then \(f\) is holomorphic at \(a\) if and only if \(f\) is differentiable at \(a\) as a map of real variables (Definition 27.10.2) and its derivative is complex linear, which holds if and only if

\[ \partial _xu = \partial _yv, \qquad \partial _yu = -\partial _xv \quad \text{at } a. \]

Then \(f'(a) = \partial _xu + i\partial _xv\). Sums, products, quotients and composites of holomorphic functions are holomorphic, with the usual rules.

Proof â–¼

Complex differentiability at \(a\) says \(f(a + h) = f(a) + ch + o(|h|)\) with \(c = f'(a) \in \mathbb {C}\), which is real differentiability with \(Df(a)\) the real linear map \(h \mapsto ch\), that is, with matrix \(\left(\begin{smallmatrix} \alpha & -\beta \\ \beta & \alpha \end{smallmatrix}\right)\) for \(c = \alpha + i\beta \). Comparing with the Jacobian matrix \(\left(\begin{smallmatrix} \partial _xu & \partial _yu \\ \partial _xv & \partial _yv \end{smallmatrix}\right)\) gives the equations. The rules follow as in Proposition 27.6.2, whose proofs are valid over \(\mathbb {C}\).

014A Example 29.2.4

Polynomials are entire; \(1/z\) is holomorphic on \(\mathbb {C}^\times \); power series are holomorphic in their disc of convergence, with the termwise derivative (Theorem 27.6.20); \(\exp \) is entire with \(\exp ' = \exp \). The function \(\bar z\) is nowhere holomorphic, since \(\partial _xu = 1 \neq -1 = \partial _yv\).

The Cauchy–Riemann equations have a geometric meaning. A real linear map \(\mathbb {R}^2 \to \mathbb {R}^2\) is complex linear exactly when its matrix is \(\left(\begin{smallmatrix} \alpha & -\beta \\ \beta & \alpha \end{smallmatrix}\right)\), that is, when it is multiplication by \(c = \alpha + i\beta = |c|e^{i\varphi }\): a rotation by \(\varphi \) followed by a dilation by \(|c|\). So near a point where \(f^{\prime }(a) \neq 0\), a holomorphic map looks, to first order, like a rotation and a dilation, which preserve angles and orientation. Maps with this property are called conformal, and the converse also holds.

036F Proposition 29.2.5

  1. Let \(f\) be holomorphic near \(a\) with \(f^{\prime }(a) \neq 0\), and let \(\gamma _1, \gamma _2\) be \(C^1\) curves with \(\gamma _1(0) = \gamma _2(0) = a\) and nonzero tangent vectors \(\gamma _1^{\prime }(0), \gamma _2^{\prime }(0)\). Then \((f \circ \gamma _j)^{\prime }(0) = f^{\prime }(a)\gamma _j^{\prime }(0)\). Hence the image curves have tangent vectors rotated by the same angle \(\arg f^{\prime }(a)\) and stretched by the same factor \(|f^{\prime }(a)|\), and the oriented angle between them equals that between \(\gamma _1\) and \(\gamma _2\).

  2. Conversely, let \(f\) be real differentiable at \(a\) with \(A = Df(a)\) invertible, and suppose that \(A\) preserves oriented angles, that is, \(Aw/Av\) and \(w/v\) have the same argument for all nonzero \(v, w \in \mathbb {C}\). Then \(f\) is complex differentiable at \(a\).

Proof â–¼

(1) By the chain rule (Proposition 27.6.2) and Proposition 29.2.3, \((f \circ \gamma _j)^{\prime }(0) = Df(a)\gamma _j^{\prime }(0) = f^{\prime }(a)\gamma _j^{\prime }(0)\). The quotient of the two image tangent vectors is \(\gamma _2^{\prime }(0)/\gamma _1^{\prime }(0)\), the same as for the original ones, and the oriented angle from one vector to another is the argument of this quotient.

(2) Let \(p = A(1) \neq 0\) and \(q = A(i)\). The oriented angle from \(1\) to \(i\) is \(\frac\pi 2\), so \(q/p\) has argument \(\frac\pi 2\): \(q = itp\) for some \(t {\gt} 0\). The oriented angle from \(1\) to \(1 + i\) is \(\frac\pi 4\), so \(A(1 + i)/A(1) = (p + q)/p = 1 + it\) has argument \(\frac\pi 4\), which forces \(t = 1\). Hence \(A(x + iy) = xp + yip = (x + iy)p\): \(A\) is multiplication by \(p\), so it is complex linear, and \(f\) is complex differentiable at \(a\) with \(f^{\prime }(a) = p\) by Proposition 29.2.3.

036G Example 29.2.6

  1. Critical points. For \(f(z) = z^2\) and \(a = 0\), where \(f^{\prime }(0) = 0\), angles are not preserved but doubled: the ray \(\{ re^{i\theta } : r {\gt} 0\} \) is mapped to the ray \(\{ r^2e^{2i\theta }\} \). In general, near a zero of order \(m\) of \(f^{\prime }\) the map multiplies angles by \(m + 1\); this follows from the local form of holomorphic maps (Theorem 29.5.12).

  2. The exponential. \(e^{x + iy} = e^xe^{iy}\) maps the vertical line \(x = x_0\) onto the circle of radius \(e^{x_0}\), and the horizontal line \(y = y_0\) onto the ray of argument \(y_0\). The lines of the grid meet at right angles, and so do their images, circles and rays from the origin, as Proposition 29.2.5 predicts, since \(\exp ^{\prime } = \exp \) never vanishes.

  3. Reflection. \(z \mapsto \bar z\) preserves the size of angles but reverses their orientation, and it is not holomorphic (Example 29.2.2(3)). The argument in (2) adapts: an invertible real linear map that preserves unoriented angles is either complex linear or of the form \(z \mapsto p\bar z\), so the maps that preserve unoriented angles, at points where the derivative is invertible, are the holomorphic and the antiholomorphic ones (those \(f\) with \(\bar f\) holomorphic).

014B Definition 29.2.7

A path is a piecewise continuously differentiable map \(\gamma \colon [a, b] \to \mathbb {C}\); it is closed if \(\gamma (a) = \gamma (b)\). Its image is \(\gamma ^*\) and its length is \(L(\gamma ) = \int _a^b|\gamma '(t)|\, dt\). For continuous \(f\) on \(\gamma ^*\),

\[ \int _\gamma f(z)\, dz = \int _a^bf(\gamma (t))\gamma '(t)\, dt. \]
014C Lemma 29.2.8

The integral is unchanged by increasing \(C^1\) reparametrisations, changes sign under reversal, and \(|\int _\gamma f| \leq L(\gamma )\sup _{\gamma ^*}|f|\). If \(f = F'\) for a holomorphic \(F\) on an open set containing \(\gamma ^*\), then \(\int _\gamma f = F(\gamma (b)) - F(\gamma (a))\); in particular \(\int _\gamma f = 0\) for closed \(\gamma \). For the circle \(\gamma (t) = a + re^{2\pi it}\), \(t \in [0, 1]\), one has \(\int _\gamma (z - a)^n\, dz = 0\) for \(n \neq -1\) and \(= 2\pi i\) for \(n = -1\).

Proof â–¼

Reparametrisation and reversal follow from the substitution rule (Corollary 27.8.4), the estimate from Proposition 27.8.2. If \(f = F'\), then \((F \circ \gamma )' = f(\gamma )\gamma '\) on each \(C^1\) piece, so the integral telescopes by the fundamental theorem of calculus. For the circle and \(n \neq -1\), \((z - a)^n\) has the primitive \((z - a)^{n+1}/(n + 1)\); for \(n = -1\) the integral is \(\int _0^12\pi i\, dt\).

In the definition, \(f(\gamma (t))\gamma ^{\prime }(t)\) is a complex-valued function of the real variable \(t\), and its integral is taken separately on the real and the imaginary part (Definition 27.8.1): \(\int _a^b(g + ih) = \int _a^bg + i\int _a^bh\). So a complex line integral is a pair of real line integrals, and the formal rule \(f\, dz = (u + iv)(dx + i\, dy)\) says which ones.

03E6 Proposition 29.2.9

Let \(\gamma = x + iy\) be a path and \(f = u + iv\) continuous on \(\gamma ^*\), with \(x, y, u, v\) real. Then

\[ \int _\gamma f(z)\, dz = \int _\gamma (u\, dx - v\, dy) + i\int _\gamma (v\, dx + u\, dy), \]

where the two integrals on the right are real line integrals of the second kind (Definition 27.11.12) of the vector fields \((u, -v)\) and \((v, u)\) in \(\mathbb {R}^2 = \mathbb {C}\). Moreover \(\bigl|\int _\gamma f(z)\, dz\bigr| \leq \int _\gamma |f|\, ds\), where \(\int _\gamma |f|\, ds = \int _a^b|f(\gamma (t))|\, |\gamma ^{\prime }(t)|\, dt\) is a line integral of the first kind (Definition 27.11.9), also written \(\int _\gamma |f|\, |dz|\).

Proof â–¼

On each \(C^1\) piece, \(f(\gamma (t))\gamma ^{\prime }(t) = (u + iv)(x^{\prime } + iy^{\prime }) = (ux^{\prime } - vy^{\prime }) + i(vx^{\prime } + uy^{\prime })\), with \(u\) and \(v\) evaluated at \(\gamma (t)\). The real part is \(\langle (u, -v), (x^{\prime }, y^{\prime })\rangle \) and the imaginary part \(\langle (v, u), (x^{\prime }, y^{\prime })\rangle \), and integrating them gives the two line integrals. The estimate is the norm inequality \(|\int g| \leq \int |g|\) of Proposition 27.8.2(3), since \(|f(\gamma (t))\gamma ^{\prime }(t)| = |f(\gamma (t))|\, |\gamma ^{\prime }(t)|\).

For instance, for \(f(z) = \bar z = x - iy\) the two real fields are \((x, y)\) and \((-y, x)\). The first is the gradient of \(\frac12(x^2 + y^2)\), so its integral over a closed path vanishes; the second is the rotation field, whose integral over the unit circle is \(2\pi \) (Example 27.11.14(1)). This gives \(\int _\gamma \bar z\, dz = 0 + 2\pi i\), as in Example 29.2.11(1) below.

03E7 Remark 29.2.10

The two real forms are closed exactly when \(f\) satisfies the Cauchy–Riemann equations: for \(u, v\) of class \(C^1\), \(d(u\, dx - v\, dy) = -(u_y + v_x)\, dx \wedge dy\) and \(d(v\, dx + u\, dy) = (u_x - v_y)\, dx \wedge dy\) (Proposition 29.2.3). So, if \(f\) is holomorphic with continuous derivative and \(\gamma \) bounds a region \(D\) in its domain, Green’s theorem (Chapter 40) gives \(\int _\gamma f\, dz = \iint _D0 = 0\): this is Cauchy’s original proof of his theorem. The proof below, through Goursat’s theorem, does not assume that \(f^{\prime }\) is continuous, and does not need Green’s theorem. In the language of Chapter 36, \(f\, dz\) is a complex-valued \(1\)-form, and \(d(f\, dz) = \frac{\partial f}{\partial \bar z}\, d\bar z \wedge dz\), which vanishes for holomorphic \(f\).

036H Example 29.2.11

  1. A function without primitive. Let \(\gamma (t) = e^{2\pi it}\), \(t \in [0, 1]\), be the unit circle. On it \(\bar z = e^{-2\pi it}\) and \(\gamma ^{\prime }(t) = 2\pi ie^{2\pi it}\), so \(\int _\gamma \bar z\, dz = \int _0^12\pi i\, dt = 2\pi i \neq 0\). By Lemma 29.2.8, \(\bar z\) is not the derivative of a holomorphic function on any open set containing the circle.

  2. Dependence on the path. Integrate \(\bar z\) from \(0\) to \(1 + i\) in two ways. Along the segment \(\gamma (t) = t(1 + i)\): \(\int _0^1t(1 - i)(1 + i)\, dt = \int _0^12t\, dt = 1\). Along the segment from \(0\) to \(1\) followed by the segment from \(1\) to \(1 + i\), parametrised by \(t\) and \(1 + it\): \(\int _0^1t\, dt + \int _0^1(1 - it)\, i\, dt = \frac12 + \bigl(i + \frac12\bigr) = 1 + i\). The results differ. For a function with a primitive, such as \(z^2 = (z^3/3)^{\prime }\), every path from \(0\) to \(1 + i\) gives \((1 + i)^3/3\).

  3. A holomorphic function without primitive. \(1/z\) is holomorphic on \(\mathbb {C}^\times \), yet \(\int _\gamma dz/z = 2\pi i\) over the unit circle (Lemma 29.2.8); so \(1/z\) has no primitive on \(\mathbb {C}^\times \), that is, there is no holomorphic logarithm on \(\mathbb {C}^\times \). What goes wrong is the hole at \(0\): Cauchy’s theorem below says that integrals of holomorphic functions over closed paths vanish when the path can be shrunk to a point inside the domain.
