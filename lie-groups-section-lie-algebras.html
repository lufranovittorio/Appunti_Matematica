33.2 Lie groups and their Lie algebras018H

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

018I Definition 33.2.1

A Lie group is a group \(G\) with a smooth structure for which multiplication \(m \colon G \times G \to G\), \(m(g, h) = gh\), and inversion \(i \colon G \to G\), \(i(g) = g^{-1}\), are smooth. A homomorphism of Lie groups is a smooth group homomorphism. For \(g \in G\), left translation, right translation and conjugation are \(L_g(x) = gx\), \(R_g(x) = xg\) and \(c_g(x) = gxg^{-1}\).

The maps \(L_g\), \(R_g\) and \(c_g\) are diffeomorphisms: \(L_g\) is the composite of \(x \mapsto (g, x)\) and \(m\), so it is smooth (Proposition 32.3.3), and its inverse is \(L_{g^{-1}}\); similarly for \(R_g\), and \(c_g = L_g \circ R_{g^{-1}}\). In particular \(d_e(L_g) \colon T_eG \to T_gG\) is an isomorphism, so the whole tangent bundle is determined by the single space \(T_eG\).

02EX Lemma 33.2.2

Let \(G\) be a Lie group. Under the identification \(T_{(g,h)}(G \times G) = T_gG \oplus T_hG\) of Proposition 32.4.13,

\[ d_{(g,h)}m(v, w) = d_g(R_h)(v) + d_h(L_g)(w). \]

In particular \(d_{(e,e)}m(X, Y) = X + Y\) and \(d_ei(X) = -X\) for \(X, Y \in T_eG\).

Proof â–¼

By Proposition 32.4.13, \((v, w)\) corresponds to \(d\iota _h(v) + d\iota _g(w)\) with \(\iota _h(x) = (x, h)\) and \(\iota _g(y) = (g, y)\). Since \(m \circ \iota _h = R_h\) and \(m \circ \iota _g = L_g\), the chain rule gives the formula. For the inverse, the map \(g \mapsto m(g, i(g))\) is constant, so differentiating at \(e\) gives \(0 = X + d_ei(X)\).

018J Example 33.2.3

  1. \((\mathbb {R}^n, +)\), where multiplication \((x, y) \mapsto x + y\) and inversion \(x \mapsto -x\) are linear, hence smooth. Any finite-dimensional real vector space is a Lie group in the same way.

  2. The circle \(S^1 = \{ z \in \mathbb {C}: |z| = 1\} \) under multiplication. Complex multiplication \(\mathbb {C}\times \mathbb {C}\to \mathbb {C}\) is a polynomial map of the real coordinates, hence smooth; its restriction to the submanifold \(S^1 \times S^1\) is smooth and takes values in \(S^1\), so it is smooth as a map to \(S^1\) (Proposition 32.6.5). Inversion is \(z \mapsto \overline{z}\), the restriction of a linear map.

  3. The general linear groups \(\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb {R})\) and \(\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb {C})\), open subsets of \(M_n(\mathbb {R})\) and \(M_n(\mathbb {C})\), with multiplication polynomial in the entries and inversion given by the adjugate divided by the determinant (Example 32.3.4(4)). They have dimensions \(n^2\) and \(2n^2\).

  4. Products \(G \times H\) of Lie groups, with componentwise operations (Proposition 32.3.3(5)). So the torus \(T^n = S^1 \times \cdots \times S^1\) is a Lie group. The torus \(\mathbb {R}^n/\mathbb {Z}^n\) is also a Lie group (use Theorem 32.7.1: the operations lift to the smooth operations of \(\mathbb {R}^n\)), isomorphic to \(T^n\) by Exercise 32.10.6.

  5. The identity component \(G^0\) of a Lie group is an open normal subgroup (Propositions 19.5.6(1) and 19.2.3(4)), hence a Lie group as an open submanifold. The quotient \(G/G^0\) is discrete.

Most Lie groups arise as subgroups of the groups in (3). The following proposition makes them Lie groups.

02EY Proposition 33.2.4

Let \(G\) be a Lie group and \(H \subseteq G\) a subgroup that is a submanifold. Then \(H\), with its submanifold structure, is a Lie group, the inclusion \(H \to G\) is a homomorphism of Lie groups, and \(H\) is closed in \(G\).

Proof â–¼

The product \(H \times H\) is a submanifold of \(G \times G\) (products of slice charts are slice charts). The multiplication of \(G\), restricted to \(H \times H\), is smooth, and it takes values in \(H\), so it is smooth as a map \(H \times H \to H\) (Proposition 32.6.5); similarly for inversion. The inclusion is smooth by Lemma 32.6.3.

For closedness, let \(\overline{H}\) be the closure of \(H\), which is a subgroup, because multiplication and inversion are continuous. We show that \(H\) is open in \(\overline{H}\). Let \(h \in H\) and let \((U, \varphi )\) be a slice chart at \(h\); then \(U \cap H\) is closed in \(U\), being the preimage of the closed set \(\mathbb {R}^k \times \{ 0\} \). If \(x \in U \cap \overline{H}\), every neighbourhood of \(x\) inside \(U\) meets \(H\), hence meets \(U \cap H\), so \(x\) lies in the closure of \(U \cap H\) in \(U\), which is \(U \cap H\). So \(U \cap \overline{H} = U \cap H\), and \(H\) is open in \(\overline{H}\). An open subgroup of a topological group is closed (Proposition 19.2.3(2)), so \(H\) is closed in \(\overline{H}\), that is, \(H = \overline{H}\).

02EZ Example 33.2.5

The classical groups are the following subgroups of \(\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb {R})\) and \(\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb {C})\). Each is a submanifold, by the regular value theorem (Theorem 32.6.8), hence a Lie group by Proposition 33.2.4; we compute its dimension and its tangent space at \(I\).

  1. The special linear group \(\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb {R}) = \{ \det A = 1\} \) has dimension \(n^2 - 1\) and \(T_I\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb {R}) = \{ X : \operatorname {tr}X = 0\} \) (Example 32.6.9(3)). The same computation over \(\mathbb {C}\), with \(\det \colon M_n(\mathbb {C}) \to \mathbb {C}\), shows that \(\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb {C})\) has real dimension \(2n^2 - 2\): at \(A \in \mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb {C})\), \(d_A\det (A) = n\) and \(d_A\det (iA) = in\), so \(d_A\det \) is onto \(\mathbb {C}\).

  2. The orthogonal group \(\mathrm{O}(n) = \{ A \in M_n(\mathbb {R}) : A^TA = I\} \) has dimension \(n(n - 1)/2\), and \(T_I\mathrm{O}(n) = \{ X : X^T = -X\} \) (Example 32.6.9(2)). It is compact: it is closed, and bounded because its columns are unit vectors. For \(A \in \mathrm{O}(n)\), \(\det (A)^2 = \det (A^TA) = 1\), so \(\det A = \pm 1\); the special orthogonal group \(\mathrm{SO}(n) = \{ A \in \mathrm{O}(n) : \det A = 1\} \) is open and closed in \(\mathrm{O}(n)\), so it is a compact Lie group of the same dimension, with the same tangent space at \(I\).

  3. The unitary group \(\mathrm{U}(n) = \{ A \in M_n(\mathbb {C}) : A^*A = I\} \). Let \(\mathrm{Herm}_n\) be the real vector space of Hermitian matrices (\(S^* = S\)), of real dimension \(n^2\) (\(n\) real diagonal entries and \(n(n - 1)/2\) complex entries above the diagonal), and \(F(A) = A^*A\), a smooth map \(M_n(\mathbb {C}) \to \mathrm{Herm}_n\). Since \(F(A + tX) = A^*A + t(A^*X + X^*A) + t^2X^*X\), \(d_AF(X) = A^*X + X^*A\). For \(A \in \mathrm{U}(n)\) and \(S\) Hermitian, \(X = AS/2\) gives \(d_AF(X) = S\), so \(I\) is a regular value. Hence \(\mathrm{U}(n)\) is a submanifold of dimension \(2n^2 - n^2 = n^2\), with \(T_I\mathrm{U}(n) = \{ X : X^* = -X\} \), the skew-Hermitian matrices. It is compact, like \(\mathrm{O}(n)\). For \(n = 1\), \(\mathrm{U}(1) = S^1\).

  4. The special unitary group \(\mathrm{SU}(n) = \{ A \in \mathrm{U}(n) : \det A = 1\} \). The determinant is a homomorphism of Lie groups \(\mathrm{U}(n) \to \mathrm{U}(1) = S^1\) (for \(A\) unitary, \(|\det A|^2 = \det (A^*A) = 1\)). Its differential at \(I\) is \(X \mapsto \operatorname {tr}X\), because \(\det (I + tX) = 1 + t\operatorname {tr}X + O(t^2)\); on skew-Hermitian matrices it takes values in \(i\mathbb {R}= T_1S^1\), and it is onto \(i\mathbb {R}\) since \(\operatorname {tr}(i\, E_{11}) = i\), where \(E_{11}\) is the matrix unit. At any \(A \in \mathrm{SU}(n)\) we have \(\det \circ L_A = \det \) on \(\mathrm{U}(n)\), so \(d_A\det \circ d_I(L_A) = d_I\det \) is surjective as well. So \(1\) is a regular value of \(\det \colon \mathrm{U}(n) \to S^1\), and \(\mathrm{SU}(n)\) is a compact submanifold of \(\mathrm{U}(n)\) of dimension \(n^2 - 1\), with \(T_I\mathrm{SU}(n) = \{ X : X^* = -X, \operatorname {tr}X = 0\} \).

In the smallest cases, \(\mathrm{SO}(2)\) is the group of rotations \(\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} \cos t & -\sin t \\ \sin t & \cos t \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\), isomorphic to \(S^1\), and \(\mathrm{O}(2)\) is the union of \(\mathrm{SO}(2)\) and the reflections; \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\) is diffeomorphic to \(S^3\) (Example 33.2.11).

We now attach a Lie algebra to a Lie group. The idea is to use the translations to spread a tangent vector at \(e\) over the whole group, obtaining a vector field, and to take the bracket of vector fields (Definition 32.9.4).

018K Definition 33.2.6

A vector field \(X\) on \(G\) is left-invariant if \(d(L_g)(X_h) = X_{gh}\) for all \(g, h \in G\). The Lie algebra of \(G\) is \(\mathfrak {g} = T_eG\), with the bracket transported from the left-invariant vector fields: for \(X, Y \in \mathfrak {g}\), \([X, Y] = [\widetilde{X}, \widetilde{Y}]_e\), where \(\widetilde{X}\) is the left-invariant vector field with value \(X\) at \(e\) (Proposition 33.2.8).

For a diffeomorphism \(F \colon M \to N\) and a vector field \(X\) on \(M\), the push-forward \(F_*X\) is the vector field \((F_*X)_{F(p)} = d_pF(X_p)\) on \(N\); so \(X\) is left-invariant exactly when \((L_g)_*X = X\) for all \(g\). More generally, for a smooth map \(\varphi \colon M \to N\), vector fields \(X\) on \(M\) and \(Y\) on \(N\) are \(\varphi \)-related if \(d_p\varphi (X_p) = Y_{\varphi (p)}\) for all \(p\).

02F0 Lemma 33.2.7

Let \(\varphi \colon M \to N\) be smooth. Then \(X\) and \(Y\) are \(\varphi \)-related if and only if \(X(f \circ \varphi ) = (Yf) \circ \varphi \) for all \(f \in C^\infty (N)\). If \(X_1, X_2\) are \(\varphi \)-related to \(Y_1, Y_2\), then \([X_1, X_2]\) is \(\varphi \)-related to \([Y_1, Y_2]\). In particular, for a diffeomorphism \(F\), \(F_*[X_1, X_2] = [F_*X_1, F_*X_2]\).

Proof â–¼

At \(p\), \(X_p(f \circ \varphi ) = d_p\varphi (X_p)(f)\) and \((Yf)(\varphi (p)) = Y_{\varphi (p)}(f)\); two tangent vectors at \(\varphi (p)\) are equal if they agree on all \(f\), which proves the first statement. Then \([X_1, X_2](f \circ \varphi ) = X_1((Y_2f) \circ \varphi ) - X_2((Y_1f) \circ \varphi ) = (Y_1Y_2f - Y_2Y_1f) \circ \varphi \). For a diffeomorphism \(F\), \(X\) and \(F_*X\) are \(F\)-related, and \(F_*X\) is the only vector field \(F\)-related to \(X\).

018L Proposition 33.2.8

The map \(X \mapsto X_e\) is a bijection from left-invariant vector fields to \(T_eG\), its inverse sending \(v\) to the field \(g \mapsto d(L_g)_ev\); left-invariant fields are smooth and closed under the Lie bracket. Hence \(\mathfrak {g}\) is a Lie algebra of dimension \(\dim G\) (Definition 12.4.1), and a homomorphism \(\varphi \colon G \to H\) induces a homomorphism of Lie algebras \(d_e\varphi \colon \mathfrak {g} \to \mathfrak {h}\).

Proof â–¼

The bijection. A left-invariant field satisfies \(X_g = d(L_g)(X_e)\), so it is determined by \(X_e\). Conversely, for \(v \in T_eG\) put \(\widetilde{v}_g = d_e(L_g)(v)\). It is left-invariant by the chain rule, since \(L_h \circ L_g = L_{hg}\). It is smooth: let \(\gamma \) be a smooth curve with \(\gamma (0) = e\) and \(\gamma '(0) = v\) (Proposition 32.4.9). For \(f \in C^\infty (G)\), \((\widetilde{v}f)(g) = v(f \circ L_g) = \frac{\partial }{\partial t}\big|_{t=0}f(g\gamma (t))\), the partial derivative in \(t\) at \(t = 0\) of the smooth function \((g, t) \mapsto f(g\gamma (t))\); computed in charts, it is a smooth function of \(g\). So \(f \mapsto \widetilde{v}f\) is a derivation of \(C^\infty (G)\), and \(\widetilde{v}\) is the corresponding vector field (Proposition 32.5.6).

Brackets. If \(X\) and \(Y\) are left-invariant, then \((L_g)_*[X, Y] = [(L_g)_*X, (L_g)_*Y] = [X, Y]\) by Lemma 33.2.7, so \([X, Y]\) is left-invariant. Thus the left-invariant fields form a Lie subalgebra of \(\mathfrak {X}(G)\) (Proposition 32.9.6), and transporting the bracket along the linear bijection \(X \mapsto X_e\) makes \(\mathfrak {g}\) a Lie algebra of dimension \(\dim T_eG = \dim G\).

Homomorphisms. Let \(v \in \mathfrak {g}\) and \(w = d_e\varphi (v)\). Since \(\varphi \circ L_g = L_{\varphi (g)} \circ \varphi \), \(d\varphi (\widetilde{v}_g) = d\varphi \circ d(L_g)(v) = d(L_{\varphi (g)})(w) = \widetilde{w}_{\varphi (g)}\): the fields \(\widetilde{v}\) and \(\widetilde{w}\) are \(\varphi \)-related. By Lemma 33.2.7, \([\widetilde{v}_1, \widetilde{v}_2]\) is \(\varphi \)-related to \([\widetilde{w}_1, \widetilde{w}_2]\), and evaluating at \(e\) gives \(d_e\varphi ([v_1, v_2]) = [d_e\varphi (v_1), d_e\varphi (v_2)]\).

018M Example 33.2.9

For \(G = \mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb {K})\) the Lie algebra is \(\mathfrak {gl}_n(\mathbb {K}) = M_n(\mathbb {K})\) with the commutator \([X, Y] = XY - YX\). Indeed \(L_A\) is the restriction of the linear map \(B \mapsto AB\), so its differential is again \(B \mapsto AB\), and the left-invariant field with value \(X\) at \(I\) is \(\widetilde{X}_A = AX\). In the linear coordinates of \(M_n(\mathbb {K})\), the coordinate formula of Proposition 32.9.6 reads \([\widetilde{X}, \widetilde{Y}](A) = D\widetilde{Y}(A)[\widetilde{X}(A)] - D\widetilde{X}(A)[\widetilde{Y}(A)]\); since \(\widetilde{Y}(A) = AY\) is linear in \(A\), \(D\widetilde{Y}(A)[B] = BY\), so \([\widetilde{X}, \widetilde{Y}](A) = AXY - AYX\), and at \(A = I\) this is \(XY - YX\).

If \(G \subseteq \mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb {K})\) is a subgroup and a submanifold, the inclusion is a homomorphism of Lie groups (Proposition 33.2.4) with injective differential, so by Proposition 33.2.8 its Lie algebra is the subspace \(T_IG \subseteq M_n(\mathbb {K})\) with the commutator. By Example 33.2.5:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathfrak {sl}_n(\mathbb {K}) & = \{ X : \operatorname {tr}X = 0\} , & \mathfrak {o}(n) = \mathfrak {so}(n) & = \{ X \in M_n(\mathbb {R}) : X^T = -X\} ,\\ \mathfrak {u}(n) & = \{ X \in M_n(\mathbb {C}) : X^* = -X\} , & \mathfrak {su}(n) & = \{ X \in \mathfrak {u}(n) : \operatorname {tr}X = 0\} . \end{aligned} \]

One can check directly that these subspaces are closed under the commutator: \(\operatorname {tr}(XY - YX) = 0\) always, and if \(X^T = -X\) and \(Y^T = -Y\), then \((XY - YX)^T = Y^TX^T - X^TY^T = YX - XY\).

02F1 Example 33.2.10

The Lie algebra \(\mathfrak {so}(3)\) of skew-symmetric \(3 \times 3\) matrices is \(\mathbb {R}^3\) with the cross product. For \(a \in \mathbb {R}^3\) let \(L_a\) be the skew-symmetric matrix with \(L_av = a \times v\) for all \(v \in \mathbb {R}^3\):

\[ L_a = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -a_3 & a_2 \\ a_3 & 0 & -a_1 \\ -a_2 & a_1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Every skew-symmetric matrix has this form, so \(a \mapsto L_a\) is a linear isomorphism \(\mathbb {R}^3 \to \mathfrak {so}(3)\). By the Jacobi identity for the cross product, \(a \times (b \times v) - b \times (a \times v) = (a \times b) \times v\), so \([L_a, L_b] = L_{a \times b}\). In the basis \(L_1 = L_{e_1}\), \(L_2 = L_{e_2}\), \(L_3 = L_{e_3}\): \([L_1, L_2] = L_3\), \([L_2, L_3] = L_1\), \([L_3, L_1] = L_2\).

02F2 Example 33.2.11

The group \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\) is diffeomorphic to \(S^3\), and its Lie algebra is isomorphic to \(\mathfrak {so}(3)\). A matrix \(A \in M_2(\mathbb {C})\) lies in \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\) if and only if its columns form an orthonormal basis of \(\mathbb {C}^2\) and \(\det A = 1\). If the first column is \((a, b)\) with \(|a|^2 + |b|^2 = 1\), the second is a unit vector orthogonal to it, hence \(\lambda (-\overline{b}, \overline{a})\) with \(|\lambda | = 1\), and then \(\det A = \lambda \). So

\[ \mathrm{SU}(2) = \Bigl\{ \begin{pmatrix} a & -\overline{b} \\ b & \overline{a} \end{pmatrix} : |a|^2 + |b|^2 = 1\Bigr\} , \]

and \((a, b) \mapsto A\) is a bijection \(S^3 \to \mathrm{SU}(2)\), where \(S^3 \subseteq \mathbb {C}^2 = \mathbb {R}^4\). It is smooth (the restriction of a real-linear map, with values in \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\)), and so is its inverse, \(A \mapsto \) first column. The Lie algebra \(\mathfrak {su}(2)\) has the basis

\[ I_1 = \begin{pmatrix} i & 0 \\ 0 & -i \end{pmatrix}, \qquad I_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad I_3 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & i \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \]

since every traceless skew-Hermitian matrix is \(\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} ix & y + iz \\ -y + iz & -ix \end{smallmatrix}\bigr) = xI_1 + yI_2 + zI_3\). A direct computation gives \(I_1^2 = I_2^2 = I_3^2 = -I\) and \(I_1I_2 = -I_2I_1 = I_3\), \(I_2I_3 = -I_3I_2 = I_1\), \(I_3I_1 = -I_1I_3 = I_2\) (the quaternion relations). So \([I_1, I_2] = 2I_3\) and cyclically, and the basis \(e_k = I_k/2\) satisfies \([e_1, e_2] = e_3\), \([e_2, e_3] = e_1\), \([e_3, e_1] = e_2\). Comparing with Example 33.2.10, \(e_k \mapsto L_k\) is an isomorphism of Lie algebras \(\mathfrak {su}(2) \to \mathfrak {so}(3)\). It comes from a homomorphism of groups \(\mathrm{SU}(2) \to \mathrm{SO}(3)\), which is a double covering (Exercise 33.6.2).
