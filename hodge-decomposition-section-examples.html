46.7 Examples01H4

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

01H5 Example 46.7.1

Let \(X\) be a compact Riemann surface. It is Kähler (Example 45.4.3), so \(b_1\) is even, and its Hodge diamond has \(h^{0,0} = h^{1,1} = 1\) and \(h^{1,0} = h^{0,1} = g\), where \(g = b_1/2\). So the genus \(g\), a topological invariant, equals both the dimension of the space of holomorphic \(1\)-forms \(H^0(X, K_X)\) and \(\dim H^1(X, \mathcal{O}_X)\). For the Riemann sphere \(g = 0\); for an elliptic curve \(\mathbb {C}/\Lambda \), \(g = 1\) and \(H^0(X, K_X)\) is spanned by \(dz\).

01H6 Example 46.7.2

For \(\mathbb {C}\mathbb {P}^n\), \(b_{2k} = 1\) for \(0 \leq k \leq n\) and all other Betti numbers vanish (Theorem 21.5.1). By Corollary 46.5.3(4), \(h^{k,k} \geq 1\), so \(H^{2k}(\mathbb {C}\mathbb {P}^n; \mathbb {C}) = H^{k,k}\) is spanned by \([\omega _{FS}]^k\), and \(h^{p,q} = 0\) for \(p \neq q\). In particular \(\mathbb {C}\mathbb {P}^n\) has no nonzero holomorphic forms of positive degree, and \(H^q(\mathbb {C}\mathbb {P}^n, \mathcal{O}) = 0\) for \(q {\gt} 0\).

01H7 Example 46.7.3

Let \(E_\tau = \mathbb {C}/(\mathbb {Z}+ \tau \mathbb {Z})\) with \(\operatorname {Im}\tau {\gt} 0\), and let \(a\), \(b\) be the classes in \(H_1(E_\tau ; \mathbb {Z})\) of the images of the segments \([0, 1]\) and \([0, \tau ]\). Integration identifies \(H^1(E_\tau ; \mathbb {C})\) with \(\mathbb {C}^2\) through \([\alpha ] \mapsto (\int _a\alpha , \int _b\alpha )\). Then \(H^{1,0}\) is spanned by \([dz] \mapsto (1, \tau )\) and \(H^{0,1}\) by \([d\bar z] \mapsto (1, \bar\tau )\). The lattice \(H^1(E_\tau ; \mathbb {Z})\) and the vector space \(H^1(E_\tau ; \mathbb {C})\) do not depend on \(\tau \), but the line \(H^{1,0} = [1 : \tau ] \in \mathbb {P}^1\) does, and it recovers \(\tau \). So the Hodge decomposition captures the complex structure, which is not a topological invariant. This is the first example of a period map (Chapter 67).

01H8 Example 46.7.4

For a smooth quartic surface \(X \subseteq \mathbb {C}\mathbb {P}^3\) (a K3 surface) the Hodge diamond is

\[ \begin{array}{ccccc}& & 1 & & \\ & 0 & & 0 & \\ 1 & & 20 & & 1 \\ & 0 & & 0 & \\ & & 1 & & \end{array} \]

Here \(b_1 = 0\) by the Lefschetz hyperplane theorem (Chapter 48), \(K_X \cong \mathcal{O}_X\) by adjunction (Proposition 43.5.1) so that \(p_g = 1\), and \(b_2 = 22\) comes from the Euler characteristic \(\chi = c_2(X) = 24\) [BHPVdV04, Chapter VIII]. The subspace \(H^{1,1} \cap H^2(X; \mathbb {Q})\) is where the Hodge conjecture for divisors, the Lefschetz \((1,1)\)-theorem, lives.

02DC Example 46.7.5

Let \(C\) and \(C'\) be compact Riemann surfaces of genera \(g\) and \(g'\), so \(h^{0,0} = h^{1,1} = 1\) and \(h^{1,0} = h^{0,1} = g\) for \(C\) (Example 46.7.1). By Exercise 46.8.1, the surface \(S = C \times C'\) has

\[ h^{1,0}(S) = g + g', \qquad h^{2,0}(S) = gg', \qquad h^{1,1}(S) = 2 + 2gg', \]

since \(H^{1,1}(S)\) receives \(H^{1,1}(C) \otimes H^{0,0}(C')\), \(H^{0,0}(C) \otimes H^{1,1}(C')\), \(H^{1,0}(C) \otimes H^{0,1}(C')\) and \(H^{0,1}(C) \otimes H^{1,0}(C')\). As a check, \(b_2(S) = 2h^{2,0} + h^{1,1} = 2 + 4gg'\), which is \(1 + (2g)(2g') + 1\) by the Künneth formula. For two elliptic curves, \(g = g' = 1\), the Hodge diamond is

\[ \begin{array}{ccccc}& & 1 & & \\ & 2 & & 2 & \\ 1 & & 4 & & 1 \\ & 2 & & 2 & \\ & & 1 & & \end{array} \]

which is also that of every two-dimensional complex torus (Example 44.4.2). The \(H^{1,1}\) of \(S\) always contains the classes of the two fibres, and the remaining \(2gg'\) dimensions come from \(H^1(C) \otimes H^1(C')\); which rational classes lie there is governed by the homomorphisms between the Jacobians of \(C\) and \(C'\) (for elliptic curves this is worked out in Chapter 65).

01H9 Counterexample 46.7.6

The Hopf surface (Exercise 42.5.3) has \(b_1 = 1\), which is odd, so by Corollary 46.5.3(3) it is not Kähler, which gives a second proof of Counterexample 45.4.8. It also shows how the theorem fails: \(h^{1,0} = 0 \neq 1 = h^{0,1}\) (Example 44.4.2).

01HA Counterexample 46.7.7

Let \(G\) be the complex Heisenberg group of matrices \(\left(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & z_1 & z_3 \\ 0 & 1 & z_2 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right)\), \(z \in \mathbb {C}^3\), and \(\Gamma \subseteq G\) the subgroup with entries in \(\mathbb {Z}[i]\). The Iwasawa manifold \(X = \Gamma \backslash G\) is a compact complex manifold of dimension \(3\) carrying a holomorphic \(1\)-form that is not closed. So \(X\) is not Kähler, by Proposition 46.5.6.

Proof â–¼

Left multiplication by an element with entries \((a_1, a_2, a_3)\) is \((z_1, z_2, z_3) \mapsto (z_1 + a_1, z_2 + a_2, z_3 + a_1z_2 + a_3)\). This map is holomorphic, and \(\Gamma \) acts freely and properly discontinuously, being a discrete subgroup acting by translations of the group. So \(X\) is a complex manifold. It is compact: using \(a_1, a_2 \in \mathbb {Z}[i]\) one moves \(z_1, z_2\) into the unit square, and then using \(a_3\) one moves \(z_3\) there, so the image of a compact set surjects onto \(X\). The form \(\theta = dz_3 - z_1\, dz_2\) is invariant under left multiplication: \(d(z_3 + a_1z_2 + a_3) - (z_1 + a_1)\, d(z_2 + a_2) = dz_3 - z_1\, dz_2\). So it descends to a holomorphic \(1\)-form on \(X\), and \(d\theta = -dz_1 \wedge dz_2 \neq 0\).
