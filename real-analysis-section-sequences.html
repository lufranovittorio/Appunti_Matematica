26.2 Limits of sequences023R

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

We recall that a real sequence \((a_n)_{n \in \mathbb {N}}\) converges to \(a \in \mathbb {R}\) if for every \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\) there is \(N\) with \(|a_n - a| {\lt} \varepsilon \) for all \(n \geq N\) (Definition 4.7.5). This is convergence in the metric space \(\mathbb {R}\) (Chapter 17). In this section we develop the theory of limits of sequences directly from this definition.

023S Definition 26.2.1

Let \((a_n)\) be a real sequence.

  1. If \((a_n)\) converges to \(a\) we write \(a_n \to a\) or \(\lim _na_n = a\), and call \(a\) the limit.

  2. \((a_n)\) diverges to \(+\infty \), written \(a_n \to +\infty \), if for every \(M \in \mathbb {R}\) there is \(N\) with \(a_n {\gt} M\) for all \(n \geq N\); similarly \(a_n \to -\infty \) if for every \(M\) eventually \(a_n {\lt} M\).

  3. \((a_n)\) is regular if it converges or diverges to \(\pm \infty \), and oscillating otherwise.

  4. \((a_n)\) is bounded if there is \(C\) with \(|a_n| \leq C\) for all \(n\); bounded above or below are defined similarly.

  5. \((a_n)\) is increasing if \(a_n \leq a_{n+1}\) for all \(n\), strictly increasing if \(a_n {\lt} a_{n+1}\), and similarly decreasing; it is monotone if it is increasing or decreasing.

  6. A subsequence of \((a_n)\) is a sequence \((a_{n_k})_{k \in \mathbb {N}}\) with \(n_0 {\lt} n_1 {\lt} n_2 {\lt} \cdots \).

A property holds eventually, or for large \(n\), if there is \(N\) such that it holds for all \(n \geq N\).

023T Proposition 26.2.2

Let \((a_n)\) be a real sequence.

  1. A sequence has at most one limit.

  2. A convergent sequence is bounded.

  3. (Permanence of sign) If \(a_n \to a\) and \(a {\gt} 0\), then \(a_n {\gt} a/2 {\gt} 0\) for large \(n\).

  4. Changing, adding or removing finitely many terms does not affect convergence or the limit.

  5. If \(a_n \to a\), every subsequence converges to \(a\); the same holds for divergence to \(\pm \infty \).

Proof â–¼

(1) Suppose \(a_n \to a\) and \(a_n \to b\) with \(a \neq b\), and let \(\varepsilon = |a - b|/2\). For \(n\) larger than both thresholds, \(|a - b| \leq |a - a_n| + |a_n - b| {\lt} 2\varepsilon = |a - b|\), a contradiction. (2) Take \(\varepsilon = 1\): for \(n \geq N\), \(|a_n| \leq |a_n - a| + |a| {\lt} |a| + 1\), so \(|a_n| \leq \max (|a_0|, \ldots , |a_{N-1}|, |a| + 1)\) for all \(n\). (3) Take \(\varepsilon = a/2\): then \(a_n {\gt} a - a/2 = a/2\) for large \(n\). (4) The definitions only involve the terms with \(n \geq N\), and \(N\) may be enlarged. (5) By induction \(n_k \geq k\), so \(k \geq N\) implies \(n_k \geq N\).

023U Lemma 26.2.3 Bernoulli’s inequality

For \(h \geq -1\) and \(n \in \mathbb {N}\), \((1 + h)^n \geq 1 + nh\).

Proof â–¼

For \(n = 0\) both sides are \(1\). If \((1 + h)^n \geq 1 + nh\), then, as \(1 + h \geq 0\), \((1 + h)^{n+1} \geq (1 + nh)(1 + h) = 1 + (n + 1)h + nh^2 \geq 1 + (n + 1)h\).

023V Proposition 26.2.4 Algebra of limits

Let \(a_n \to a\) and \(b_n \to b\) be real sequences and \(c \in \mathbb {R}\). Then \(a_n + b_n \to a + b\), \(ca_n \to ca\), \(a_nb_n \to ab\) and \(|a_n| \to |a|\). If \(b \neq 0\), then \(b_n \neq 0\) for large \(n\) and \(a_n/b_n \to a/b\).

Proof â–¼

Given \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\), for large \(n\) we have \(|a_n - a| {\lt} \varepsilon /2\) and \(|b_n - b| {\lt} \varepsilon /2\), so \(|(a_n + b_n) - (a + b)| {\lt} \varepsilon \). The reverse triangle inequality \(||a_n| - |a|| \leq |a_n - a|\) gives \(|a_n| \to |a|\), and \(|ca_n - ca| = |c|\, |a_n - a|\) gives \(ca_n \to ca\). For the product, let \(C\) bound \(|a_n|\) (Proposition 26.2.2(2)); then

\[ |a_nb_n - ab| \leq |a_n|\, |b_n - b| + |b|\, |a_n - a| \leq C|b_n - b| + |b|\, |a_n - a|, \]

which is less than \(\varepsilon \) for large \(n\). For the quotient it suffices to show \(1/b_n \to 1/b\). As \(|b_n| \to |b| {\gt} 0\), permanence of sign gives \(|b_n| {\gt} |b|/2\) for large \(n\), and then \(|1/b_n - 1/b| = |b - b_n|/(|b_n|\, |b|) \leq 2|b_n - b|/|b|^2\), which tends to \(0\).

023W Proposition 26.2.5 Comparison and squeeze

Let \((a_n)\), \((b_n)\), \((c_n)\) be real sequences.

  1. If \(a_n \leq b_n\) for large \(n\), \(a_n \to a\) and \(b_n \to b\), then \(a \leq b\).

  2. (Squeeze theorem) If \(a_n \leq c_n \leq b_n\) for large \(n\) and \(a_n \to L\), \(b_n \to L\), then \(c_n \to L\).

  3. If \(a_n \leq b_n\) for large \(n\) and \(a_n \to +\infty \), then \(b_n \to +\infty \).

  4. If \(|a_n| \leq b_n\) for large \(n\) and \(b_n \to 0\), then \(a_n \to 0\). In particular, if \((a_n)\) is bounded and \(b_n \to 0\), then \(a_nb_n \to 0\).

Proof â–¼

(1) If \(a {\gt} b\), then \(a_n - b_n \to a - b {\gt} 0\) by Proposition 26.2.4, so \(a_n - b_n {\gt} 0\) for large \(n\) by permanence of sign, a contradiction. (2) Given \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\), for large \(n\) we have \(L - \varepsilon {\lt} a_n \leq c_n \leq b_n {\lt} L + \varepsilon \). (3) Eventually \(b_n \geq a_n {\gt} M\). (4) Apply (2) to \(-b_n \leq a_n \leq b_n\); for the last statement, \(|a_nb_n| \leq C|b_n|\).

023X Counterexample 26.2.6

Strict inequalities do not pass to the limit: \(1/(n + 1) {\gt} 0\) for all \(n\), but the limit is \(0\). The convergence \(|a_n| \to |a|\) does not imply \(a_n \to a\) unless \(a = 0\): the sequence \(a_n = (-1)^n\) oscillates although \(|a_n| \to 1\).

023Y Proposition 26.2.7 Infinite limits

Let \(a_n \to +\infty \).

  1. If \((b_n)\) is bounded below, then \(a_n + b_n \to +\infty \).

  2. If \(b_n \geq c\) for large \(n\), for some \(c {\gt} 0\), then \(a_nb_n \to +\infty \).

  3. \(a_n {\gt} 0\) for large \(n\), and \(1/a_n \to 0\). Conversely, if \(b_n {\gt} 0\) and \(b_n \to 0\), then \(1/b_n \to +\infty \).

Proof â–¼

(1) If \(b_n \geq -C\), then for any \(M\), eventually \(a_n {\gt} M + C\), so \(a_n + b_n {\gt} M\). (2) Eventually \(a_n {\gt} M/c\) with \(M {\gt} 0\), so \(a_nb_n {\gt} M\). (3) Eventually \(a_n {\gt} 1/\varepsilon {\gt} 0\), so \(0 {\lt} 1/a_n {\lt} \varepsilon \). Conversely, eventually \(0 {\lt} b_n {\lt} 1/M\) for \(M {\gt} 0\), so \(1/b_n {\gt} M\).

023Z Counterexample 26.2.8 Indeterminate forms

If \(a_n \to +\infty \) and \(b_n \to -\infty \), the limits alone do not determine the behaviour of \(a_n + b_n\): with \(a_n = n\), the choices \(b_n = -n + c\), \(b_n = -n/2\), \(b_n = -2n\) and \(b_n = -n + (-1)^n\) give the limit \(c\), the limit \(+\infty \), the limit \(-\infty \), and an oscillating sequence. Similarly, if \(a_n \to 0\) and \(b_n \to +\infty \), then with \(a_n = 1/(n+1)\) the choices \(b_n = c(n + 1)\), \((n + 1)^2\) and \((-1)^n(n + 1)\) show that \(a_nb_n\) can converge to any \(c\), diverge, or oscillate. The same happens for the forms \(\infty /\infty \) and \(0/0\).

0240 Proposition 26.2.9 Fundamental limits

  1. For \(q \in \mathbb {R}\): \(q^n \to 0\) if \(|q| {\lt} 1\), \(q^n \to 1\) if \(q = 1\), \(q^n \to +\infty \) if \(q {\gt} 1\), and \((q^n)\) oscillates if \(q \leq -1\).

  2. For \(a {\gt} 0\), \(a^{1/n} \to 1\), and \(n^{1/n} \to 1\).

  3. For \(k \in \mathbb {N}\) and \(a {\gt} 1\), \(n^k/a^n \to 0\): exponentials beat powers.

  4. For every \(a \in \mathbb {R}\), \(a^n/n! \to 0\), and \(n!/n^n \to 0\).

Proof â–¼

(1) If \(q {\gt} 1\), write \(q = 1 + h\) with \(h {\gt} 0\); by Lemma 26.2.3, \(q^n \geq 1 + nh \to +\infty \). If \(0 {\lt} |q| {\lt} 1\), then \(|q|^{-n} \to +\infty \), so \(|q^n| \to 0\) by Proposition 26.2.7(3), and \(q^n \to 0\) by Proposition 26.2.5(4). If \(q = -1\) the terms alternate between \(\pm 1\); if \(q {\lt} -1\), \(|q^n| \to +\infty \) and the signs alternate, so the sequence is unbounded above and below and has no limit. (2) Let \(a \geq 1\) and \(a^{1/n} = 1 + h_n\) with \(h_n \geq 0\) (the \(n\)-th root exists by Proposition 4.7.4). By Bernoulli, \(a \geq 1 + nh_n\), so \(0 \leq h_n \leq (a - 1)/n \to 0\). For \(a {\lt} 1\) apply this to \(1/a\) and use Proposition 26.2.4. For \(n^{1/n} = 1 + h_n\) with \(n \geq 2\), the binomial formula gives \(n \geq \binom {n}{2}h_n^2\), so \(0 \leq h_n \leq \sqrt{2/(n - 1)} \to 0\). (3) Write \(a = 1 + h\). For \(n \geq 2k + 2\) the binomial formula gives \(a^n \geq \binom {n}{k+1}h^{k+1} \geq \frac{(n/2)^{k+1}}{(k+1)!}h^{k+1}\), since each of the \(k + 1\) factors \(n, n - 1, \ldots , n - k\) is at least \(n/2\). Hence \(0 \leq n^k/a^n \leq 2^{k+1}(k + 1)!/(h^{k+1}n) \to 0\). (4) Choose an integer \(N {\gt} 2|a|\). For \(n \geq N\), \(|a|^n/n! \leq \frac{|a|^N}{N!}2^{N-n}\), which tends to \(0\) by (1). Finally \(n!/n^n = \frac1n\cdot \frac2n\cdots \frac nn \leq \frac1n\).

0241 Proposition 26.2.10

Every monotone sequence is regular. An increasing sequence converges to its supremum if it is bounded above, and diverges to \(+\infty \) otherwise; a decreasing sequence converges to its infimum if it is bounded below, and diverges to \(-\infty \) otherwise.

Proof â–¼

Let \((a_n)\) be increasing. If it is bounded above, let \(s = \sup _na_n\) (Definition 4.7.1). For \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\), \(s - \varepsilon \) is not an upper bound, so \(a_N {\gt} s - \varepsilon \) for some \(N\), and then \(s - \varepsilon {\lt} a_N \leq a_n \leq s\) for \(n \geq N\). If it is not bounded above, for every \(M\) some \(a_N {\gt} M\), and then \(a_n \geq a_N {\gt} M\) for \(n \geq N\). The decreasing case follows by passing to \((-a_n)\).

0242 Lemma 26.2.11

Every real sequence has a monotone subsequence.

Proof â–¼

Call \(n\) a peak if \(a_n \geq a_m\) for all \(m {\gt} n\). If there are infinitely many peaks \(n_0 {\lt} n_1 {\lt} \cdots \), then \(a_{n_0} \geq a_{n_1} \geq \cdots \) is a decreasing subsequence. Otherwise let \(n_0\) be larger than all peaks. As \(n_0\) is not a peak, there is \(n_1 {\gt} n_0\) with \(a_{n_1} {\gt} a_{n_0}\); as \(n_1\) is not a peak, there is \(n_2 {\gt} n_1\) with \(a_{n_2} {\gt} a_{n_1}\); continuing (by recursion), we obtain a strictly increasing subsequence.

0243 Theorem 26.2.12 Bolzano–Weierstrass

Every bounded real sequence has a convergent subsequence. Every real sequence has a regular subsequence: a sequence that is unbounded above has a subsequence diverging to \(+\infty \).

Proof â–¼

By Lemma 26.2.11 there is a monotone subsequence, which is regular by Proposition 26.2.10, and convergent if the sequence is bounded. If \((a_n)\) is unbounded above, choose recursively \(n_{k+1} {\gt} n_k\) with \(a_{n_{k+1}} {\gt} k + 1\), which is possible because only finitely many terms have index \(\leq n_k\).

0244 Theorem 26.2.13 Cauchy criterion

A real sequence converges if and only if it is a Cauchy sequence.

Proof â–¼

If \(a_n \to a\), then \(|a_n - a_m| \leq |a_n - a| + |a - a_m| {\lt} \varepsilon \) when both are within \(\varepsilon /2\) of \(a\). Conversely, let \((a_n)\) be Cauchy. Taking \(\varepsilon = 1\) shows that \((a_n)\) is bounded, so by Theorem 26.2.12 a subsequence converges to some \(a\). Given \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\), choose \(N\) with \(|a_n - a_m| {\lt} \varepsilon /2\) for \(m, n \geq N\), and \(k\) with \(n_k \geq N\) and \(|a_{n_k} - a| {\lt} \varepsilon /2\). Then \(|a_n - a| \leq |a_n - a_{n_k}| + |a_{n_k} - a| {\lt} \varepsilon \) for \(n \geq N\). (This is a second proof of Proposition 4.7.6 for \(\mathbb {R}\).)

0245 Counterexample 26.2.14

It is not enough that consecutive terms get close. The partial sums \(H_n = \sum _{k=1}^n1/k\) of the harmonic series satisfy \(H_{n+1} - H_n = 1/(n + 1) \to 0\), but \(H_{2n} - H_n = \sum _{k=n+1}^{2n}1/k \geq n \cdot \frac{1}{2n} = \frac12\), so \((H_n)\) is not Cauchy; being increasing, it diverges to \(+\infty \). Likewise \(\sqrt{n + 1} - \sqrt n = 1/(\sqrt{n + 1} + \sqrt n) \to 0\) while \(\sqrt n \to +\infty \).

0246 Proposition 26.2.15 Subsequence criterion

  1. \(a_n \to a\) if and only if every subsequence of \((a_n)\) has a further subsequence converging to \(a\).

  2. A bounded sequence all of whose convergent subsequences have the same limit \(a\) converges to \(a\).

Proof â–¼

(1) One direction is Proposition 26.2.2(5). Conversely, if \(a_n \not\to a\), there is \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\) such that \(|a_n - a| \geq \varepsilon \) for infinitely many \(n\); these terms form a subsequence none of whose subsequences converges to \(a\). (2) By Theorem 26.2.12, every subsequence is bounded and has a convergent subsequence, whose limit is \(a\) by hypothesis; apply (1).

0247 Proposition 26.2.16 Stolz–Cesàro

Let \((b_n)\) be strictly increasing with \(b_n \to +\infty \), and let \((a_n)\) be a real sequence with \(\frac{a_{n+1} - a_n}{b_{n+1} - b_n} \to L \in \mathbb {R}\). Then \(a_n/b_n \to L\). In particular (Cesàro), if \(a_n \to L\), then the averages \(\frac{1}{n}(a_1 + \cdots + a_n) \to L\).

Proof â–¼

Let \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\) and choose \(N\) such that \(b_N {\gt} 0\) and \(|(a_{k+1} - a_k) - L(b_{k+1} - b_k)| \leq \varepsilon (b_{k+1} - b_k)\) for \(k \geq N\). Summing over \(N \leq k {\lt} n\) gives \(|a_n - a_N - L(b_n - b_N)| \leq \varepsilon (b_n - b_N) \leq \varepsilon b_n\). Dividing by \(b_n {\gt} 0\),

\[ \Big|\frac{a_n}{b_n} - L\Big| \leq \varepsilon + \frac{|a_N - Lb_N|}{b_n}, \]

and the last term is less than \(\varepsilon \) for large \(n\), as \(b_n \to +\infty \). For Cesàro, apply this to \(a'_n = a_1 + \cdots + a_n\) and \(b_n = n\).

0248 Counterexample 26.2.17

The converse of Cesàro’s theorem fails: \(a_n = (-1)^n\) does not converge, but its averages tend to \(0\), since the partial sums are \(0\) or \(-1\).
