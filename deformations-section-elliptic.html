51.3 The moduli space of elliptic curves031T

Draft. This chapter is being written; its proofs have not been checked.

A lattice in \(\mathbb {C}\) is a subgroup \(\Lambda = \mathbb {Z}\omega _1 + \mathbb {Z}\omega _2\) generated by two \(\mathbb {R}\)-linearly independent numbers; the quotient \(\mathbb {C}/\Lambda \) is a compact Riemann surface, an elliptic curve, diffeomorphic to \(S^1 \times S^1\). Every compact Riemann surface of genus \(1\) is of this form, by the uniformisation theorem (Theorem 44.4.2), but we shall not need this. We determine when two such tori are isomorphic.

031U Proposition 51.3.1

Let \(\Lambda \), \(\Lambda ^{\prime } \subseteq \mathbb {C}\) be lattices. Every holomorphic map \(f \colon \mathbb {C}/\Lambda \to \mathbb {C}/\Lambda ^{\prime }\) is induced by an affine map \(z \mapsto \alpha z + \beta \) with \(\alpha \Lambda \subseteq \Lambda ^{\prime }\). Consequently \(\mathbb {C}/\Lambda \cong \mathbb {C}/\Lambda ^{\prime }\) if and only if \(\Lambda ^{\prime } = \alpha \Lambda \) for some \(\alpha \in \mathbb {C}^*\).

Proof â–¼

The projection \(\mathbb {C}\to \mathbb {C}/\Lambda ^{\prime }\) is a covering map and \(\mathbb {C}\) is simply connected, so \(f \circ p \colon \mathbb {C}\to \mathbb {C}/\Lambda ^{\prime }\), with \(p \colon \mathbb {C}\to \mathbb {C}/\Lambda \), lifts to a continuous map \(F \colon \mathbb {C}\to \mathbb {C}\) (Proposition 19.6.1), which is holomorphic because the projections are local biholomorphisms. For \(\lambda \in \Lambda \), \(F(z + \lambda ) - F(z)\) lies in the discrete set \(\Lambda ^{\prime }\) and depends continuously on \(z\), so it is a constant \(c(\lambda )\). Hence \(F^{\prime }(z + \lambda ) = F^{\prime }(z)\): the entire function \(F^{\prime }\) is \(\Lambda \)-periodic, hence bounded (its values are those on a compact fundamental parallelogram), hence constant by Liouville’s theorem (Corollary 29.4.7). So \(F(z) = \alpha z + \beta \), and \(\alpha \lambda = c(\lambda ) \in \Lambda ^{\prime }\). If \(f\) is an isomorphism, the same applied to \(f^{-1}\) gives \(\alpha ^{-1}\Lambda ^{\prime } \subseteq \Lambda \), so \(\alpha \Lambda = \Lambda ^{\prime }\) with \(\alpha \neq 0\). Conversely, \(z \mapsto \alpha z\) induces an isomorphism \(\mathbb {C}/\Lambda \to \mathbb {C}/\alpha \Lambda \).

Every lattice has a basis \(\omega _1, \omega _2\) with \(\operatorname {Im}(\omega _2/\omega _1) {\gt} 0\) (exchange the two vectors if necessary), and then \(\Lambda = \omega _1(\mathbb {Z}+ \mathbb {Z}\tau )\) with \(\tau = \omega _2/\omega _1 \in \mathbb {H}\). So every elliptic curve is isomorphic to some \(E_\tau = \mathbb {C}/(\mathbb {Z}+ \mathbb {Z}\tau )\). The group \(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb {Z})\) acts on \(\mathbb {H}\) by Möbius transformations,

\[ \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}\cdot \tau = \frac{a\tau + b}{c\tau + d}, \qquad \operatorname {Im}\frac{a\tau + b}{c\tau + d} = \frac{\operatorname {Im}\tau }{|c\tau + d|^2}, \]

the formula for the imaginary part following from \((a\tau + b)(c\bar\tau + d) = ac|\tau |^2 + ad\tau + bc\bar\tau + bd\) and \(ad - bc = 1\).

031V Theorem 51.3.2

\(E_\tau \cong E_{\tau ^{\prime }}\) if and only if \(\tau ^{\prime } = \gamma \cdot \tau \) for some \(\gamma \in \mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb {Z})\). So the isomorphism classes of elliptic curves correspond bijectively to the points of \(\mathbb {H}/\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb {Z})\). Every orbit meets the set

\[ \mathcal{F} = \{ \tau \in \mathbb {H} : |\operatorname {Re}\tau | \leq \tfrac 12, \ |\tau | \geq 1\} , \]

and two distinct points of \(\mathcal{F}\) are in the same orbit only if they lie on the boundary and are exchanged by \(\tau \mapsto \tau + 1\) or by \(\tau \mapsto -1/\tau \).

Proof â–¼

By Proposition 51.3.1, \(E_\tau \cong E_{\tau ^{\prime }}\) if and only if \(\mathbb {Z}+ \mathbb {Z}\tau ^{\prime } = \alpha (\mathbb {Z}+ \mathbb {Z}\tau )\) for some \(\alpha \). Then \(\alpha \tau \) and \(\alpha \) form a basis of \(\mathbb {Z}+ \mathbb {Z}\tau ^{\prime }\), as do \(\tau ^{\prime }\) and \(1\); so \(\tau ^{\prime } = \alpha (a\tau + b)\) and \(1 = \alpha (c\tau + d)\) with \(\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{smallmatrix}\bigr) \in \mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb {Z})\), and dividing, \(\tau ^{\prime } = (a\tau + b)/(c\tau + d)\). Since both \(\tau \) and \(\tau ^{\prime }\) lie in \(\mathbb {H}\), the formula for the imaginary part, valid for \(\det = \pm 1\) with the factor \(\det \), forces \(ad - bc = 1\). Conversely, if \(\tau ^{\prime } = \gamma \cdot \tau \), then \(\alpha = (c\tau + d)^{-1}\) satisfies \(\alpha (\mathbb {Z}+ \mathbb {Z}\tau ) = \mathbb {Z}+ \mathbb {Z}\tau ^{\prime }\), as \(\gamma \) is invertible over \(\mathbb {Z}\).

Every orbit meets \(\mathcal{F}\). Recall that \(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb {Z})\) contains \(T = \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\), acting by \(\tau \mapsto \tau + 1\), and \(S = \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\), acting by \(\tau \mapsto -1/\tau \). Let \(\tau \in \mathbb {H}\). The numbers \(|c\tau + d|\) for \((c, d) \in \mathbb {Z}^2 \setminus \{ 0\} \) are bounded below by a positive constant and take only finitely many values below any bound (the lattice \(\mathbb {Z}+ \mathbb {Z}\tau \) is discrete), so \(\operatorname {Im}(\gamma \cdot \tau ) = \operatorname {Im}\tau /|c\tau + d|^2\) attains a maximum over \(\gamma \in \mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb {Z})\); choose \(\gamma \) attaining it and a power \(T^k \colon \tau \mapsto \tau + k\) with \(|\operatorname {Re}(T^k\gamma \cdot \tau )| \leq \frac12\). The point \(\sigma = T^k\gamma \cdot \tau \) still has maximal imaginary part. If \(|\sigma | {\lt} 1\), then \(S \colon \sigma \mapsto -1/\sigma \) would give \(\operatorname {Im}(-1/\sigma ) = \operatorname {Im}\sigma /|\sigma |^2 {\gt} \operatorname {Im}\sigma \), a contradiction. So \(\sigma \in \mathcal{F}\).

Points of \(\mathcal{F}\) in the same orbit. Let \(\tau = x + iy\) and \(\gamma \cdot \tau \) both lie in \(\mathcal{F}\), with \(\gamma = \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{smallmatrix}\bigr) \neq \pm 1\). Exchanging the roles of \(\tau \) and \(\gamma \cdot \tau \) (and replacing \(\gamma \) by \(\gamma ^{-1}\)) we may assume \(\operatorname {Im}(\gamma \cdot \tau ) \geq \operatorname {Im}\tau \), that is, \(|c\tau + d| \leq 1\). Write \(\rho = e^{2\pi i/3} = -\frac12 + \frac{\sqrt3}{2}i\) and \(\rho + 1 = -\bar\rho = e^{\pi i/3}\) for the two corners of \(\mathcal{F}\). For \(\tau \in \mathcal{F}\) we have \(y^2 = |\tau |^2 - x^2 \geq 1 - \frac14\), so \(y \geq \sqrt3/2\), and \(|c|\sqrt3/2 \leq |c|y = |\operatorname {Im}(c\tau + d)| \leq 1\) forces \(c \in \{ 0, \pm 1\} \).

  • If \(c = 0\), then \(ad = 1\), so \(a = d = \pm 1\) and \(\gamma \cdot \tau = \tau \pm b\) with \(b \neq 0\). Both \(\tau \) and \(\tau \pm b\) have real part in \([-\frac12, \frac12]\), so \(|b| = 1\) and \(x = \pm \frac12\): the two points lie on the vertical sides and are exchanged by \(\tau \mapsto \tau \pm 1\).

  • If \(c = \pm 1\), replacing \(\gamma \) by \(-\gamma \) (which acts in the same way) we may take \(c = 1\), so \(|\tau + d| \leq 1\). Now \(|\tau + d|^2 = (x + d)^2 + y^2\). If \(|d| \geq 2\) this is at least \((\frac32)^2 {\gt} 1\). If \(d = \pm 1\), it is \(|\tau |^2 \pm 2x + 1 \geq 1 \pm 2x + 1 \geq 1\), with equality only if \(|\tau | = 1\) and \(x = \mp \frac12\), that is, \(\tau = \rho \) for \(d = 1\) and \(\tau = \rho + 1\) for \(d = -1\). If \(d = 0\), it is \(|\tau |^2 \geq 1\), with equality only if \(|\tau | = 1\).

    Case \(d = 0\), \(|\tau | = 1\). Then \(-b = ad - bc = 1\) and \(\gamma \cdot \tau = (a\tau - 1)/\tau = a - 1/\tau = a - \bar\tau \), of real part \(a - x\). This lies in \([-\frac12, \frac12]\) only if \(a = 0\), or \(a = \pm 1\) and \(x = \pm \frac12\). If \(a = 0\), then \(\gamma \cdot \tau = -1/\tau \), and \(\tau \), \(-1/\tau = -\bar\tau \) are points of the arc \(|\tau | = 1\) exchanged by \(\tau \mapsto -1/\tau \). If \(a = \pm 1\), then \(x = a/2\), so \(\tau \) is a corner, and \(\gamma \cdot \tau = a - \bar\tau = (a - x) + iy = \tau \).

    Case \(d = \pm 1\), \(\tau \) a corner. Then \(b = ad - 1\) and \(\gamma \cdot \tau = a - (\tau + d)^{-1}\). For \(\tau = \rho \), \(d = 1\): \(\rho + 1 = -\bar\rho \) and \((\rho + 1)^{-1} = -\rho \), so \(\gamma \cdot \rho = a + \rho \), which is in \(\mathcal{F}\) only for \(a \in \{ 0, 1\} \), giving \(\rho \) itself or \(\rho + 1\). For \(\tau = \rho + 1\), \(d = -1\), similarly \(\gamma \cdot \tau = a - \rho ^{-1} = a - \bar\rho = a + \rho + 1\), giving \(\rho + 1\) or \(\rho \).

In all cases either \(\gamma \cdot \tau = \tau \) or the two points lie on the boundary of \(\mathcal{F}\) and are exchanged by \(\tau \mapsto \tau \pm 1\) or by \(\tau \mapsto -1/\tau \).

So the moduli space of elliptic curves is \(\mathbb {H}/\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb {Z})\), which is \(\mathcal{F}\) with its boundary glued: the vertical sides are identified by \(\tau \mapsto \tau + 1\) and the two halves of the arc \(|\tau | = 1\) by \(\tau \mapsto -1/\tau \). Topologically it is a plane. The case analysis in the proof also shows that the stabiliser of a point of \(\mathcal{F}\) in \(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb {Z})\) is \(\{ \pm 1\} \), except at \(\tau = i\) (stabiliser of order \(4\), generated by \(S\)) and at the corners \(\rho \), \(\rho + 1\) (order \(6\); for \(\rho \) it is generated by \(ST\), as \(ST\cdot \tau = -1/(\tau + 1)\)); correspondingly every elliptic curve has the automorphism \(z \mapsto -z\), \(E_i\) has in addition \(z \mapsto iz\), and \(E_\rho \) has \(z \mapsto -\rho z\), a sixth root of unity. These two curves have complex multiplication by \(\mathbb {Z}[i]\) and \(\mathbb {Z}[\rho ]\), the Gaussian and Eisenstein integers.

031W Remark 51.3.3

  1. The \(j\)-invariant. There is a holomorphic function \(j \colon \mathbb {H} \to \mathbb {C}\), invariant under \(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb {Z})\), that induces a bijection \(\mathbb {H}/\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb {Z}) \to \mathbb {C}\); with the Eisenstein series \(g_2(\tau ) = 60\sum {}^{\prime }(m + n\tau )^{-4}\) and \(g_3(\tau ) = 140\sum {}^{\prime }(m + n\tau )^{-6}\) it is \(j = 1728g_2^3/(g_2^3 - 27g_3^2)\), with \(j(i) = 1728\) and \(j(\rho ) = 0\) [Ser73, Chapter VII]. So the moduli space of elliptic curves is the affine line, the \(j\)-line.

  2. Quadratic forms. The reduction of \(\tau \) into \(\mathcal{F}\) is the reduction theory of positive definite binary quadratic forms (Theorem 10.8.3) in another guise: the form \(|x + y\tau |^2 = x^2 + 2\operatorname {Re}(\tau )xy + |\tau |^2y^2\) satisfies \(|2\operatorname {Re}\tau | \leq 1 \leq |\tau |^2\), the reduction inequalities \(|b| \leq a \leq c\), exactly when \(\tau \in \mathcal{F}\). For a positive definite form \(ax^2 + bxy + cy^2\) of discriminant \(D {\lt} 0\), the root \(\tau = \frac{-b + i\sqrt{|D|}}{2a}\) of \(a\tau ^2 + b\tau + c\) in \(\mathbb {H}\) gives an elliptic curve with complex multiplication, and \(j(\tau )\) is an algebraic integer of degree the class number \(h(D)\); for class number one it is a rational integer, for instance \(j\bigl(\frac{1 + i\sqrt{163}}{2}\bigr) = -640320^3\), which explains why \(e^{\pi \sqrt{163}}\) is so close to an integer (Remark 10.8.9) [Cox89].

  3. Not a fine moduli space. There is no family over \(\mathbb {H}/\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb {Z})\) whose fibre over each point is the corresponding curve and which induces all families: the isotrivial family of Example 51.2.2(4) has constant “moduli point” \([i]\) without being trivial. The automorphisms of the curves are the obstruction. Such a space, which classifies the objects but does not carry a universal family, is a coarse moduli space.
