12.7 Semisimple Lie algebras02Q6

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

By Lie’s theorem a solvable Lie algebra over \(\mathbb {C}\) is, in a suitable basis of any representation, an algebra of upper triangular matrices: it is as close to being abelian as a nonabelian Lie algebra can be. Semisimple Lie algebras are the opposite extreme: they have no nonzero solvable ideals at all. Every Lie algebra \(\mathfrak {g}\) is an extension of the semisimple Lie algebra \(\mathfrak {g}/\operatorname {rad}\mathfrak {g}\) by its largest solvable ideal \(\operatorname {rad}\mathfrak {g}\) (Lemma 12.7.2), so the two classes together describe all Lie algebras. This section and the next four develop the structure of semisimple Lie algebras:

  1. the Killing form and Cartan’s criteria for solvability and semisimplicity; the decomposition into simple ideals; derivations; the abstract Jordan decomposition (this section);

  2. Weyl’s theorem on complete reducibility (Section 12.8);

  3. the root space decomposition with respect to a Cartan subalgebra, built from copies of \(\mathfrak {sl}_2\) (Section 12.9);

  4. root systems, Weyl groups, Dynkin diagrams and the classification (Section 12.10);

  5. weights and highest weights of representations (Section 12.11).

We follow [Hum72, Chapters II–III and VI]. Throughout these sections, \(k\) is a field of characteristic \(0\), so that \(\mathbb {Q}\subseteq k\), and Lie algebras and representations are finite-dimensional.

02Q7 Definition 12.7.1

Let \(\mathfrak {g}\) be a Lie algebra.

  1. The radical \(\operatorname {rad}\mathfrak {g}\) is the largest solvable ideal of \(\mathfrak {g}\) (it exists by Lemma 12.7.2 below). \(\mathfrak {g}\) is semisimple if \(\operatorname {rad}\mathfrak {g} = 0\), and simple if it is not abelian and its only ideals are \(0\) and \(\mathfrak {g}\).

  2. For a representation \(\varphi \colon \mathfrak {g} \to \mathfrak {gl}(V)\), the trace form of \(\varphi \) is the symmetric bilinear form \(\beta _\varphi (x, y) = \operatorname {tr}(\varphi (x)\varphi (y))\) on \(\mathfrak {g}\). The trace form of the adjoint representation is the Killing form

    \[ \kappa (x, y) = \kappa _{\mathfrak {g}}(x, y) = \operatorname {tr}(\operatorname {ad}x \circ \operatorname {ad}y). \]
02Q8 Lemma 12.7.2

Let \(\mathfrak {g}\) be a Lie algebra.

  1. The sum of two solvable ideals is a solvable ideal. Hence \(\mathfrak {g}\) has a largest solvable ideal \(\operatorname {rad}\mathfrak {g}\), and \(\mathfrak {g}/\operatorname {rad}\mathfrak {g}\) is semisimple.

  2. \(\mathfrak {g}\) is semisimple if and only if it has no nonzero abelian ideal. In particular a semisimple Lie algebra has centre \(0\). A simple Lie algebra \(\mathfrak {g}\) satisfies \([\mathfrak {g}, \mathfrak {g}] = \mathfrak {g}\) and is semisimple.

  3. Every trace form is invariant: \(\beta _\varphi ([x, y], z) = \beta _\varphi (x, [y, z])\). Consequently the orthogonal \(\mathfrak {a}^\perp = \{ x : \beta _\varphi (x, \mathfrak {a}) = 0\} \) of an ideal \(\mathfrak {a}\) is an ideal.

  4. If \(\mathfrak {a}\) is an ideal of \(\mathfrak {g}\), the Killing form of the Lie algebra \(\mathfrak {a}\) is the restriction of \(\kappa _{\mathfrak {g}}\) to \(\mathfrak {a} \times \mathfrak {a}\).

Proof â–¼

(1) Let \(\mathfrak {a}\), \(\mathfrak {b}\) be solvable ideals. Then \(\mathfrak {a} + \mathfrak {b}\) is an ideal, and \((\mathfrak {a} + \mathfrak {b})/\mathfrak {a} \cong \mathfrak {b}/(\mathfrak {a} \cap \mathfrak {b})\) is a homomorphic image of \(\mathfrak {b}\), hence solvable (Lemma 12.5.2(2)); so \(\mathfrak {a} + \mathfrak {b}\) is solvable by Lemma 12.5.2(3). A solvable ideal of maximal dimension therefore contains every solvable ideal. If \(\bar{\mathfrak {a}}\) is a solvable ideal of \(\mathfrak {g}/\operatorname {rad}\mathfrak {g}\), its preimage \(\mathfrak {a}\) is an ideal of \(\mathfrak {g}\) with \(\mathfrak {a}/\operatorname {rad}\mathfrak {g}\) solvable, hence solvable, so \(\mathfrak {a} \subseteq \operatorname {rad}\mathfrak {g}\) and \(\bar{\mathfrak {a}} = 0\).

(2) Abelian ideals are solvable. Conversely, if \(\mathfrak {r} = \operatorname {rad}\mathfrak {g} \neq 0\), let \(m\) be largest with \(D^m\mathfrak {r} \neq 0\). By Lemma 12.5.2(1), applied repeatedly, each \(D^i\mathfrak {r}\) is an ideal of \(\mathfrak {g}\), and \([D^m\mathfrak {r}, D^m\mathfrak {r}] = D^{m+1}\mathfrak {r} = 0\); so \(D^m\mathfrak {r}\) is a nonzero abelian ideal. The centre is an abelian ideal. If \(\mathfrak {g}\) is simple, \([\mathfrak {g}, \mathfrak {g}]\) is an ideal, nonzero as \(\mathfrak {g}\) is not abelian, so \([\mathfrak {g}, \mathfrak {g}] = \mathfrak {g}\); an abelian ideal is then \(0\), since \(\mathfrak {g}\) itself is not abelian.

(3) For endomorphisms \(X\), \(Y\), \(Z\), \(\operatorname {tr}([X, Y]Z) = \operatorname {tr}(XYZ) - \operatorname {tr}(YXZ) = \operatorname {tr}(XYZ) - \operatorname {tr}(XZY) = \operatorname {tr}(X[Y, Z])\), using \(\operatorname {tr}(AB) = \operatorname {tr}(BA)\). Apply this to \(X = \varphi (x)\), \(Y = \varphi (y)\), \(Z = \varphi (z)\), and use \(\varphi ([x, y]) = [\varphi (x), \varphi (y)]\). If \(z \in \mathfrak {a}^\perp \), \(x \in \mathfrak {g}\) and \(a \in \mathfrak {a}\), then \(\beta _\varphi ([x, z], a) = -\beta _\varphi ([z, x], a) = -\beta _\varphi (z, [x, a]) = 0\), since \([x, a] \in \mathfrak {a}\).

(4) If an endomorphism \(\phi \) of a finite-dimensional space \(U\) maps \(U\) into a subspace \(W\), then \(\operatorname {tr}\phi = \operatorname {tr}(\phi |_W)\): in a basis of \(U\) extending one of \(W\), the rows of the matrix of \(\phi \) belonging to the added vectors are zero. For \(x, y \in \mathfrak {a}\), \(\operatorname {ad}x \circ \operatorname {ad}y\) maps \(\mathfrak {g}\) into \(\mathfrak {a}\), and its restriction to \(\mathfrak {a}\) is \(\operatorname {ad}_{\mathfrak {a}}x \circ \operatorname {ad}_{\mathfrak {a}}y\).

02Q9 Remark 12.7.3

Levi’s theorem says that the extension \(0 \to \operatorname {rad}\mathfrak {g} \to \mathfrak {g} \to \mathfrak {g}/\operatorname {rad}\mathfrak {g} \to 0\) splits: \(\mathfrak {g}\) contains a semisimple subalgebra \(\mathfrak {s}\) mapping isomorphically onto \(\mathfrak {g}/\operatorname {rad}\mathfrak {g}\), so that \(\mathfrak {g} = \operatorname {rad}\mathfrak {g} \oplus \mathfrak {s}\) as vector spaces, with \(\mathfrak {s}\) acting on the ideal \(\operatorname {rad}\mathfrak {g}\); see [FH91, Appendix E]. For example \(\mathfrak {gl}_n(k) = k\cdot I \oplus \mathfrak {sl}_n(k)\), where the radical \(k \cdot I\) is the centre (Example 12.7.9). We do not use Levi’s theorem.

The key tool is the following refinement of the Jordan–Chevalley decomposition, and its consequence for traces.

02QA Lemma 12.7.4

Let \(k\) be algebraically closed, \(V\) finite-dimensional, \(x \in \mathfrak {gl}(V)\), and \(x = s + n\) its Jordan–Chevalley decomposition (Theorem 10.7.10).

  1. There are polynomials \(p, q \in k[T]\) without constant term such that \(s = p(x)\) and \(n = q(x)\). Hence if \(A \subseteq B \subseteq V\) are subspaces with \(x(B) \subseteq A\), then \(s(B) \subseteq A\) and \(n(B) \subseteq A\).

  2. If \(v_1, \ldots , v_m\) is a basis of \(V\) with \(sv_i = a_iv_i\) and \(E_{ij}\) are the corresponding matrix units (\(E_{ij}v_l = \delta _{jl}v_i\)), then \(\operatorname {ad}s(E_{ij}) = (a_i - a_j)E_{ij}\).

  3. \(\operatorname {ad}x = \operatorname {ad}s + \operatorname {ad}n\) is the Jordan–Chevalley decomposition of the endomorphism \(\operatorname {ad}x\) of \(\mathfrak {gl}(V)\).

Proof â–¼

(1) In Step 1 of the proof of Theorem 10.7.10, \(s = p(x)\) for any polynomial \(p\) with \(p \equiv \lambda _i \bmod (T - \lambda _i)^{e_i}\) for all the distinct eigenvalues \(\lambda _i\); by uniqueness (Step 2) this \(s\) is the semisimple part. If some \(\lambda _i\) is \(0\), this congruence already gives \(p(0) = 0\). Otherwise \(T\) is coprime to all \((T - \lambda _i)^{e_i}\), and the Chinese remainder theorem (Theorem 8.3.3) gives a \(p\) satisfying in addition \(p \equiv 0 \bmod T\). Put \(q = T - p\). If \(x(B) \subseteq A \subseteq B\), then \(x(A) \subseteq x(B) \subseteq A\), so \(x^j(B) \subseteq A\) for all \(j \geq 1\), and the same holds for the polynomials \(p(x)\) and \(q(x)\) without constant term.

(2) \((sE_{ij} - E_{ij}s)v_l = \delta _{jl}(a_i - a_j)v_i\).

(3) Over an algebraically closed field semisimple endomorphisms are diagonalisable (Lemma 10.7.9), so a basis as in (2) exists, and by (2) \(\operatorname {ad}s\) is diagonalisable in the basis \((E_{ij})\). The endomorphism \(\operatorname {ad}n\) is nilpotent by Lemma 12.5.4, and \([\operatorname {ad}s, \operatorname {ad}n] = \operatorname {ad}[s, n] = 0\) by the Jacobi identity. By the uniqueness in Theorem 10.7.10, \(\operatorname {ad}s\) and \(\operatorname {ad}n\) are the semisimple and nilpotent parts of \(\operatorname {ad}x = \operatorname {ad}s + \operatorname {ad}n\).

02QB Lemma 12.7.5

Let \(k\) be algebraically closed, \(V\) finite-dimensional, \(A \subseteq B\) subspaces of \(\mathfrak {gl}(V)\), and \(M = \{ x \in \mathfrak {gl}(V) : [x, B] \subseteq A\} \). If \(x \in M\) satisfies \(\operatorname {tr}(xy) = 0\) for all \(y \in M\), then \(x\) is nilpotent.

Proof â–¼

Let \(x = s + n\) be the Jordan–Chevalley decomposition and \(v_1, \ldots , v_m\) a basis with \(sv_i = a_iv_i\). Let \(E \subseteq k\) be the \(\mathbb {Q}\)-vector space spanned by \(a_1, \ldots , a_m\). We show \(E = 0\); then all \(a_i\) vanish, \(s = 0\), and \(x = n\) is nilpotent. As \(E\) is finite-dimensional over \(\mathbb {Q}\), it suffices to show that every \(\mathbb {Q}\)-linear map \(f \colon E \to \mathbb {Q}\) is zero.

Given \(f\), let \(y \in \mathfrak {gl}(V)\) be defined by \(yv_i = f(a_i)v_i\). By Lemma 12.7.4(2), \(\operatorname {ad}y(E_{ij}) = (f(a_i) - f(a_j))E_{ij}\) and \(\operatorname {ad}s(E_{ij}) = (a_i - a_j)E_{ij}\). The finitely many numbers \(a_i - a_j\) and \(0\) can be assigned the values \(f(a_i) - f(a_j)\) and \(0\) consistently: if \(a_i - a_j = a_{i^{\prime }} - a_{j^{\prime }}\), then \(f(a_i) - f(a_j) = f(a_i - a_j) = f(a_{i^{\prime }}) - f(a_{j^{\prime }})\) by \(\mathbb {Q}\)-linearity, and \(a_i - a_j = 0\) gives the value \(0\). By Lagrange interpolation there is \(r \in k[T]\) with \(r(0) = 0\) and \(r(a_i - a_j) = f(a_i) - f(a_j)\) for all \(i\), \(j\); then \(\operatorname {ad}y = r(\operatorname {ad}s)\), since both are diagonal in the basis \((E_{ij})\) with the same entries. By Lemma 12.7.4(3) and (1), applied to \(\operatorname {ad}x\), \(\operatorname {ad}s = p(\operatorname {ad}x)\) for a polynomial \(p\) without constant term; so \(\operatorname {ad}y = r(p(\operatorname {ad}x))\) is a polynomial in \(\operatorname {ad}x\) without constant term. As \(\operatorname {ad}x\) maps \(B\) into \(A\), so does \(\operatorname {ad}y\) (Lemma 12.7.4(1)), that is, \(y \in M\), and the hypothesis gives \(\operatorname {tr}(xy) = 0\).

Now \(\operatorname {tr}(xy) = \operatorname {tr}(sy) + \operatorname {tr}(ny)\). In the basis \((v_i)\), \(sy\) is diagonal with entries \(a_if(a_i)\). Interpolating again, \(y = g(s)\) for a polynomial \(g\) with \(g(a_i) = f(a_i)\), so \(y\) commutes with \(n\), which commutes with \(s\); hence \(ny\) is nilpotent (\((ny)^N = n^Ny^N\)) and has trace \(0\). Therefore \(\sum _ia_if(a_i) = 0\). The left side is an element of \(E\), since \(f(a_i) \in \mathbb {Q}\); applying \(f\) gives \(\sum _if(a_i)^2 = 0\), a sum of squares of rational numbers. So \(f(a_i) = 0\) for all \(i\), and \(f = 0\).

02QC Theorem 12.7.6 Cartan’s criterion

Let \(V\) be a finite-dimensional vector space and \(\mathfrak {g} \subseteq \mathfrak {gl}(V)\) a Lie subalgebra such that \(\operatorname {tr}(xy) = 0\) for all \(x \in [\mathfrak {g}, \mathfrak {g}]\) and \(y \in \mathfrak {g}\). Then \(\mathfrak {g}\) is solvable.

Proof â–¼

Reduction to \(k\) algebraically closed. Choose a basis, so that \(\mathfrak {g} \subseteq \mathfrak {gl}_d(k) \subseteq \mathfrak {gl}_d(\bar k)\) for an algebraic closure \(\bar k\) (Theorem 9.3.5), and let \(\bar{\mathfrak {g}}\) be the \(\bar k\)-span of \(\mathfrak {g}\). It is a Lie subalgebra, as the bracket is bilinear; \([\bar{\mathfrak {g}}, \bar{\mathfrak {g}}]\) is the \(\bar k\)-span of \([\mathfrak {g}, \mathfrak {g}]\), so the hypothesis holds for \(\bar{\mathfrak {g}}\) by bilinearity of \(\operatorname {tr}(xy)\). If \(\bar{\mathfrak {g}}\) is solvable, so is its subalgebra \(\mathfrak {g}\). So we may assume \(k\) algebraically closed.

The case \(k\) algebraically closed. It suffices to show that every \(x \in [\mathfrak {g}, \mathfrak {g}]\) is a nilpotent endomorphism of \(V\). Indeed, then \(\operatorname {ad}x\) is nilpotent on \(\mathfrak {gl}(V)\) (Lemma 12.5.4), hence on its invariant subspace \([\mathfrak {g}, \mathfrak {g}]\), so \([\mathfrak {g}, \mathfrak {g}]\) is nilpotent by Engel’s theorem (Corollary 12.5.6), and \(\mathfrak {g}\) is solvable by Corollary 12.5.10(3). Apply Lemma 12.7.5 with \(A = [\mathfrak {g}, \mathfrak {g}]\) and \(B = \mathfrak {g}\): then \(M = \{ y : [y, \mathfrak {g}] \subseteq [\mathfrak {g}, \mathfrak {g}]\} \) contains \(\mathfrak {g}\). Let \(x \in [\mathfrak {g}, \mathfrak {g}]\), a sum of brackets \([u, v]\) with \(u, v \in \mathfrak {g}\), and \(y \in M\). By the identity \(\operatorname {tr}([u, v]y) = \operatorname {tr}(u[v, y])\) of Lemma 12.7.2(3), and since \([v, y] = -[y, v] \in [\mathfrak {g}, \mathfrak {g}]\), the hypothesis gives \(\operatorname {tr}([u, v]y) = 0\). So \(\operatorname {tr}(xy) = 0\) for all \(y \in M\), and \(x\) is nilpotent by the lemma.

02QD Corollary 12.7.7

A Lie algebra \(\mathfrak {g}\) is solvable if and only if \(\kappa (x, y) = 0\) for all \(x \in [\mathfrak {g}, \mathfrak {g}]\) and \(y \in \mathfrak {g}\).

Proof â–¼

Suppose the Killing form vanishes on \([\mathfrak {g}, \mathfrak {g}] \times \mathfrak {g}\). Since \([\operatorname {ad}\mathfrak {g}, \operatorname {ad}\mathfrak {g}] = \operatorname {ad}[\mathfrak {g}, \mathfrak {g}]\), Cartan’s criterion applied to \(\operatorname {ad}\mathfrak {g} \subseteq \mathfrak {gl}(\mathfrak {g})\) shows that \(\operatorname {ad}\mathfrak {g}\) is solvable. The kernel of \(\operatorname {ad}\) is the centre, which is abelian, and \(\mathfrak {g}/\mathfrak {z}(\mathfrak {g}) \cong \operatorname {ad}\mathfrak {g}\); so \(\mathfrak {g}\) is solvable by Lemma 12.5.2(3).

Conversely, let \(\mathfrak {g}\) be solvable, and let \(L\) be the \(\bar k\)-span of \(\operatorname {ad}\mathfrak {g}\) in \(\mathfrak {gl}_d(\bar k)\), using a basis of \(\mathfrak {g}\) and an algebraic closure \(\bar k\). Then \(D^iL\) is the \(\bar k\)-span of \(D^i(\operatorname {ad}\mathfrak {g}) = \operatorname {ad}(D^i\mathfrak {g})\), so \(L\) is solvable. By Lie’s theorem (Theorem 12.5.9) there is a basis of \(\bar k^d\) in which all elements of \(L\) are upper triangular; then the elements of \([L, L]\) are strictly upper triangular, and so is the product of an element of \([L, L]\) with an element of \(L\). For \(x \in [\mathfrak {g}, \mathfrak {g}]\) and \(y \in \mathfrak {g}\), \(\operatorname {ad}x \in [L, L]\), so \(\kappa (x, y) = \operatorname {tr}(\operatorname {ad}x\, \operatorname {ad}y) = 0\).

02QE Theorem 12.7.8 Cartan’s criterion for semisimplicity

A Lie algebra is semisimple if and only if its Killing form is nondegenerate.

Proof â–¼

Let \(S = \mathfrak {g}^\perp = \{ x : \kappa (x, \mathfrak {g}) = 0\} \) be the radical of the Killing form; it is an ideal by Lemma 12.7.2(3). The Killing form of \(S\) is the restriction of \(\kappa \) (Lemma 12.7.2(4)), which vanishes on \(S \times S\); by Corollary 12.7.7, \(S\) is solvable, so \(S \subseteq \operatorname {rad}\mathfrak {g}\). Hence if \(\mathfrak {g}\) is semisimple, \(S = 0\).

Conversely, suppose \(\mathfrak {g}\) is not semisimple, and let \(\mathfrak {a} \neq 0\) be an abelian ideal (Lemma 12.7.2(2)). For \(a \in \mathfrak {a}\) and \(y \in \mathfrak {g}\), \(\operatorname {ad}a\, \operatorname {ad}y\) maps \(\mathfrak {g}\) into \(\mathfrak {a}\), and maps \(\mathfrak {a}\) into \([a, \mathfrak {a}] = 0\); so \((\operatorname {ad}a\, \operatorname {ad}y)^2 = 0\) and \(\kappa (a, y) = 0\). Thus \(0 \neq \mathfrak {a} \subseteq S\), and \(\kappa \) is degenerate.

02QF Example 12.7.9

  1. \(\mathfrak {gl}_n\) and \(\mathfrak {sl}_n\). For \(x, y \in \mathfrak {gl}_n(k)\), \(\operatorname {ad}x\, \operatorname {ad}y(z) = xyz - xzy - yzx + zyx\). The map \(z \mapsto xyz\) is left multiplication by \(xy\), which acts on each of the \(n\) columns of \(z\) by \(xy\), so its trace is \(n\operatorname {tr}(xy)\); similarly \(z \mapsto zyx\) has trace \(n\operatorname {tr}(yx)\), and \(z \mapsto xzy\) sends \(E_{ij}\) to \(\sum _{l,m}x_{li}y_{jm}E_{lm}\), whose diagonal coefficient at \(E_{ij}\) is \(x_{ii}y_{jj}\), so its trace is \(\operatorname {tr}x\operatorname {tr}y\), as is that of \(z \mapsto yzx\). Hence

    \[ \kappa _{\mathfrak {gl}_n}(x, y) = 2n\operatorname {tr}(xy) - 2\operatorname {tr}x\operatorname {tr}y . \]

    It is degenerate: \(\kappa (I, y) = 2n\operatorname {tr}y - 2n\operatorname {tr}y = 0\). Indeed \(k \cdot I\) is an abelian ideal, the centre. For \(x, y \in \mathfrak {sl}_n(k)\), \(\operatorname {ad}x\, \operatorname {ad}y\) maps \(\mathfrak {gl}_n\) into \([\mathfrak {gl}_n, \mathfrak {gl}_n] \subseteq \mathfrak {sl}_n\), so by the argument of Lemma 12.7.2(4) its trace on \(\mathfrak {gl}_n\) equals its trace on \(\mathfrak {sl}_n\):

    \[ \kappa _{\mathfrak {sl}_n}(x, y) = 2n\operatorname {tr}(xy). \]

    This form is nondegenerate for \(n \geq 2\): if \(x \in \mathfrak {sl}_n\) and \(\operatorname {tr}(xy) = 0\) for all \(y \in \mathfrak {sl}_n\), then \(y = E_{ji}\) (\(i \neq j\)) gives \(x_{ij} = 0\), and \(y = E_{ii} - E_{jj}\) gives \(x_{ii} = x_{jj}\); so \(x = cI\) with \(nc = \operatorname {tr}x = 0\), and \(x = 0\). Hence \(\mathfrak {sl}_n(k)\) is semisimple. For \(n = 2\), in the basis \(e\), \(h\), \(f\) of Section 12.6, \(\kappa (e, f) = 4\), \(\kappa (h, h) = 8\), and all other pairings of basis vectors vanish.

  2. A solvable example. For the Lie algebra with basis \(x\), \(y\) and \([x, y] = y\) (Example 12.5.3(3)), the matrices of \(\operatorname {ad}x\) and \(\operatorname {ad}y\) in the basis \((x, y)\) are \(\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\) and \(\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\), so \(\kappa (x, x) = 1\) and \(\kappa (x, y) = \kappa (y, y) = 0\). The form vanishes on \([\mathfrak {g}, \mathfrak {g}] \times \mathfrak {g} = ky \times \mathfrak {g}\), as Corollary 12.7.7 predicts, but it is not zero: the Killing form of a solvable Lie algebra need not vanish, in contrast with the nilpotent case (Exercise 12.12.8).

02QG Theorem 12.7.10

Let \(\mathfrak {g}\) be semisimple. There are simple ideals \(\mathfrak {g}_1, \ldots , \mathfrak {g}_t\) with \(\mathfrak {g} = \mathfrak {g}_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus \mathfrak {g}_t\), and \([\mathfrak {g}_i, \mathfrak {g}_j] = 0\) for \(i \neq j\). Every simple ideal of \(\mathfrak {g}\) is one of the \(\mathfrak {g}_i\), and every ideal is a sum of some of them. In particular \([\mathfrak {g}, \mathfrak {g}] = \mathfrak {g}\), and ideals and quotients of \(\mathfrak {g}\) are semisimple.

Proof â–¼

Let \(\mathfrak {a}\) be an ideal. Then \(\mathfrak {a}^\perp \), the orthogonal for \(\kappa \), is an ideal (Lemma 12.7.2(3)), and so is \(\mathfrak {a} \cap \mathfrak {a}^\perp \), on which the Killing form of \(\mathfrak {g}\) vanishes. By Lemma 12.7.2(4) and Corollary 12.7.7, \(\mathfrak {a} \cap \mathfrak {a}^\perp \) is solvable, hence \(0\). Since \(\kappa \) is nondegenerate (Theorem 12.7.8), \(\mathfrak {a}^\perp \) is the kernel of the map \(\mathfrak {g} \to \mathfrak {a}^*\), \(x \mapsto \kappa (x, -)|_{\mathfrak {a}}\), so \(\dim \mathfrak {a}^\perp \geq \dim \mathfrak {g} - \dim \mathfrak {a}\). Hence \(\mathfrak {g} = \mathfrak {a} \oplus \mathfrak {a}^\perp \), and \([\mathfrak {a}, \mathfrak {a}^\perp ] \subseteq \mathfrak {a} \cap \mathfrak {a}^\perp = 0\). Consequently an ideal \(\mathfrak {b}\) of the Lie algebra \(\mathfrak {a}\) is an ideal of \(\mathfrak {g}\), since \([\mathfrak {g}, \mathfrak {b}] = [\mathfrak {a}, \mathfrak {b}] + [\mathfrak {a}^\perp , \mathfrak {b}] = [\mathfrak {a}, \mathfrak {b}] \subseteq \mathfrak {b}\). In particular a solvable ideal of \(\mathfrak {a}\) is a solvable ideal of \(\mathfrak {g}\), hence \(0\): \(\mathfrak {a}\) is semisimple, and so is \(\mathfrak {g}/\mathfrak {a} \cong \mathfrak {a}^\perp \).

Existence of the decomposition, by induction on \(\dim \mathfrak {g}\): if \(\mathfrak {g} \neq 0\), let \(\mathfrak {a}\) be a nonzero ideal of minimal dimension. Its ideals are ideals of \(\mathfrak {g}\), so they are \(0\) and \(\mathfrak {a}\); and \(\mathfrak {a}\) is not abelian, being semisimple and nonzero. So \(\mathfrak {a}\) is simple, and \(\mathfrak {g} = \mathfrak {a} \oplus \mathfrak {a}^\perp \) with \(\mathfrak {a}^\perp \) semisimple, to which the induction hypothesis applies; the simple ideals of \(\mathfrak {a}^\perp \) are ideals of \(\mathfrak {g}\).

Let \(\mathfrak {b}\) be a simple ideal of \(\mathfrak {g}\). Then \([\mathfrak {g}, \mathfrak {b}] = \mathfrak {b}\), since it is a nonzero ideal of \(\mathfrak {b}\) (nonzero because the centre of \(\mathfrak {g}\) is \(0\)). So \(\mathfrak {b} = \sum _i[\mathfrak {g}_i, \mathfrak {b}]\), and each \([\mathfrak {g}_i, \mathfrak {b}] \subseteq \mathfrak {g}_i \cap \mathfrak {b}\) is an ideal of \(\mathfrak {g}\) contained in \(\mathfrak {b}\), hence \(0\) or \(\mathfrak {b}\). As the \(\mathfrak {g}_i\) are independent, exactly one is \(\mathfrak {b}\), and then \(\mathfrak {b} \subseteq \mathfrak {g}_i\) is a nonzero ideal of the simple \(\mathfrak {g}_i\), so \(\mathfrak {b} = \mathfrak {g}_i\). An arbitrary ideal \(\mathfrak {a}\) is semisimple, so by the existence part it is a sum of simple ideals of \(\mathfrak {a}\), which are simple ideals of \(\mathfrak {g}\), hence some of the \(\mathfrak {g}_i\). Finally \([\mathfrak {g}, \mathfrak {g}] = \sum _i[\mathfrak {g}_i, \mathfrak {g}_i] = \sum _i\mathfrak {g}_i\) by Lemma 12.7.2(2).

A derivation of a Lie algebra \(\mathfrak {g}\) is a linear map \(\delta \colon \mathfrak {g} \to \mathfrak {g}\) with \(\delta [x, y] = [\delta x, y] + [x, \delta y]\). The derivations form a Lie subalgebra \(\operatorname {Der}\mathfrak {g} \subseteq \mathfrak {gl}(\mathfrak {g})\), and by the Jacobi identity every \(\operatorname {ad}x\) is a derivation, called inner. For \(\delta \in \operatorname {Der}\mathfrak {g}\) and \(x \in \mathfrak {g}\),

\[ [\delta , \operatorname {ad}x] = \operatorname {ad}(\delta x), \]

since \([\delta , \operatorname {ad}x](y) = \delta [x, y] - [x, \delta y] = [\delta x, y]\); so \(\operatorname {ad}\mathfrak {g}\) is an ideal of \(\operatorname {Der}\mathfrak {g}\).

02QH Theorem 12.7.11

If \(\mathfrak {g}\) is semisimple, every derivation of \(\mathfrak {g}\) is inner, and \(\operatorname {ad}\colon \mathfrak {g} \to \operatorname {Der}\mathfrak {g}\) is an isomorphism of Lie algebras.

Proof â–¼

\(\operatorname {ad}\) is injective, as the centre is \(0\). Let \(D = \operatorname {Der}\mathfrak {g}\) and \(I = \operatorname {ad}\mathfrak {g} \cong \mathfrak {g}\), an ideal of \(D\). The Killing form of \(I\) is the restriction of \(\kappa _D\) (Lemma 12.7.2(4)), and it is nondegenerate since \(I \cong \mathfrak {g}\) is semisimple; so \(I \cap I^\perp = 0\), where \(I^\perp \) is the orthogonal of \(I\) for \(\kappa _D\), an ideal of \(D\). Then \([I, I^\perp ] \subseteq I \cap I^\perp = 0\), and for \(\delta \in I^\perp \) and \(x \in \mathfrak {g}\), \(\operatorname {ad}(\delta x) = [\delta , \operatorname {ad}x] = 0\), so \(\delta x = 0\): \(I^\perp = 0\). As in the proof of Theorem 12.7.10, \(\dim I^\perp \geq \dim D - \dim I\), so \(D = I\).

02QI Proposition 12.7.12 Abstract Jordan decomposition

Let \(k\) be algebraically closed and \(\mathfrak {g}\) semisimple. Every \(x \in \mathfrak {g}\) can be written uniquely as \(x = x_s + x_n\) with \(\operatorname {ad}x_s\) diagonalisable, \(\operatorname {ad}x_n\) nilpotent and \([x_s, x_n] = 0\). Moreover \(\operatorname {ad}x_s\) and \(\operatorname {ad}x_n\) are the semisimple and nilpotent parts of \(\operatorname {ad}x\), and every \(y \in \mathfrak {g}\) commuting with \(x\) commutes with \(x_s\) and \(x_n\).

Proof â–¼

Let \(\operatorname {ad}x = \sigma + \nu \) be the Jordan–Chevalley decomposition in \(\operatorname {End}(\mathfrak {g})\). For \(a \in k\) let \(\mathfrak {g}_a = \ker (\operatorname {ad}x - a)^d\), \(d = \dim \mathfrak {g}\); then \(\mathfrak {g} = \bigoplus _a\mathfrak {g}_a\) and \(\sigma = a\) on \(\mathfrak {g}_a\) (Step 1 of the proof of Theorem 10.7.10). Since \(\operatorname {ad}x\) is a derivation, \((\operatorname {ad}x - a - b)[y, z] = [(\operatorname {ad}x - a)y, z] + [y, (\operatorname {ad}x - b)z]\), and by induction

\[ (\operatorname {ad}x - a - b)^m[y, z] = \sum _{i=0}^m\binom {m}{i}\bigl[(\operatorname {ad}x - a)^iy, (\operatorname {ad}x - b)^{m-i}z\bigr]. \]

For \(y \in \mathfrak {g}_a\), \(z \in \mathfrak {g}_b\) and \(m = 2d\) every term vanishes, so \([y, z] \in \mathfrak {g}_{a+b}\), and \(\sigma [y, z] = (a + b)[y, z] = [\sigma y, z] + [y, \sigma z]\). By bilinearity \(\sigma \) is a derivation, so \(\sigma = \operatorname {ad}x_s\) for a unique \(x_s\) (Theorem 12.7.11). Put \(x_n = x - x_s\); then \(\operatorname {ad}x_n = \nu \) is nilpotent, and \(\operatorname {ad}[x_s, x_n] = [\sigma , \nu ] = 0\) gives \([x_s, x_n] = 0\), as \(\operatorname {ad}\) is injective. If \(x = s^{\prime } + n^{\prime }\) is another such decomposition, then \(\operatorname {ad}x = \operatorname {ad}s^{\prime } + \operatorname {ad}n^{\prime }\) is a Jordan–Chevalley decomposition, so \(\operatorname {ad}s^{\prime } = \sigma \) and \(s^{\prime } = x_s\). Finally, if \([x, y] = 0\), then \(\operatorname {ad}y\) commutes with \(\operatorname {ad}x\), hence with the polynomial \(\sigma \) in \(\operatorname {ad}x\); so \(\operatorname {ad}[y, x_s] = [\operatorname {ad}y, \sigma ] = 0\) and \([y, x_s] = 0\).

We call \(x\) semisimple if \(x = x_s\), that is, if \(\operatorname {ad}x\) is diagonalisable, and nilpotent if \(x = x_n\). For \(\mathfrak {g} \subseteq \mathfrak {gl}(V)\) this agrees with the usual Jordan decomposition of the matrix \(x\) (Corollary 12.8.4 below), but that requires Weyl’s theorem.

To recognise semisimple Lie algebras of matrices, the following criterion is often quicker than computing the Killing form. A Lie algebra is reductive if it is the direct sum \(\mathfrak {z}(\mathfrak {g}) \oplus \mathfrak {s}\) of its centre and a semisimple ideal \(\mathfrak {s}\).

02QJ Proposition 12.7.13

Let \(k = \mathbb {R}\) or \(\mathbb {C}\), and let \(\mathfrak {g}\) be a Lie algebra over \(k\) with an inner product (Hermitian if \(k = \mathbb {C}\)) such that the adjoint of every \(\operatorname {ad}x\) is of the form \(\operatorname {ad}x^{\prime }\) for some \(x^{\prime } \in \mathfrak {g}\). Then the orthogonal complement of an ideal is an ideal, \(\mathfrak {g}\) is the orthogonal direct sum of its centre and of simple ideals, and \(\mathfrak {g} = \mathfrak {z}(\mathfrak {g}) \oplus [\mathfrak {g}, \mathfrak {g}]\) with \([\mathfrak {g}, \mathfrak {g}]\) semisimple. In particular \(\mathfrak {g}\) is reductive, and it is semisimple if its centre is \(0\).

Proof â–¼

Let \(\mathfrak {a}\) be an ideal, \(z \in \mathfrak {a}^\perp \), \(x \in \mathfrak {g}\) and \(a \in \mathfrak {a}\). With \((\operatorname {ad}x)^* = \operatorname {ad}x^{\prime }\), \(\langle [x, z], a\rangle = \langle z, [x^{\prime }, a]\rangle = 0\), since \([x^{\prime }, a] \in \mathfrak {a}\). So \(\mathfrak {a}^\perp \) is an ideal, and \(\mathfrak {g} = \mathfrak {a} \oplus \mathfrak {a}^\perp \).

Every ideal \(\mathfrak {b}\) is an orthogonal direct sum of minimal nonzero ideals of \(\mathfrak {g}\), by induction on \(\dim \mathfrak {b}\): if \(\mathfrak {b} \neq 0\), it contains a minimal nonzero ideal \(\mathfrak {a}\) of \(\mathfrak {g}\), and \(\mathfrak {b} = \mathfrak {a} \oplus (\mathfrak {b} \cap \mathfrak {a}^\perp )\), because for \(y \in \mathfrak {b}\) the component of \(y\) in \(\mathfrak {a}^\perp \) is \(y\) minus an element of \(\mathfrak {a} \subseteq \mathfrak {b}\). So \(\mathfrak {g} = \mathfrak {a}_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus \mathfrak {a}_m\) with minimal nonzero ideals \(\mathfrak {a}_i\), and \([\mathfrak {a}_i, \mathfrak {a}_j] \subseteq \mathfrak {a}_i \cap \mathfrak {a}_j = 0\) for \(i \neq j\). Hence an ideal \(\mathfrak {c}\) of the Lie algebra \(\mathfrak {a}_i\) is an ideal of \(\mathfrak {g}\), as \([\mathfrak {g}, \mathfrak {c}] = [\mathfrak {a}_i, \mathfrak {c}]\), and by minimality \(\mathfrak {a}_i\) has no ideals but \(0\) and itself. So each \(\mathfrak {a}_i\) is either simple or abelian, and in the abelian case every subspace is an ideal, so \(\dim \mathfrak {a}_i = 1\).

An element \(\sum _ix_i\), \(x_i \in \mathfrak {a}_i\), is central if and only if each \(x_i\) is central in \(\mathfrak {a}_i\); the centre of a simple Lie algebra is \(0\), so \(\mathfrak {z}(\mathfrak {g})\) is the sum of the abelian \(\mathfrak {a}_i\). And \([\mathfrak {g}, \mathfrak {g}] = \sum _i[\mathfrak {a}_i, \mathfrak {a}_i]\) is the sum \(\mathfrak {s}\) of the simple \(\mathfrak {a}_i\) (Lemma 12.7.2(2)). It remains to see that \(\mathfrak {s}\) is semisimple. If \(\mathfrak {r}\) is an abelian ideal of \(\mathfrak {s}\) and \(\mathfrak {a}_i\) is one of its simple summands, then \(\mathfrak {a}_i \cap \mathfrak {r}\) is an abelian ideal of \(\mathfrak {a}_i\), hence \(0\), and so \([\mathfrak {a}_i, \mathfrak {r}] \subseteq \mathfrak {a}_i \cap \mathfrak {r} = 0\). Thus \(\mathfrak {r}\) is central in \(\mathfrak {s}\), whose centre is \(0\); so \(\mathfrak {r} = 0\), and \(\mathfrak {s}\) is semisimple by Lemma 12.7.2(2).

The hypothesis holds for every complex Lie subalgebra \(\mathfrak {g} \subseteq \mathfrak {gl}_n(\mathbb {C})\) that is closed under \(x \mapsto x^* = \bar x^T\), with the Hermitian inner product \(\langle x, y\rangle = \operatorname {tr}(xy^*) = \sum _{i,j}x_{ij}\bar y_{ij}\): then \((\operatorname {ad}x)^* = \operatorname {ad}(x^*)\), because

\[ \langle [x, y], z\rangle = \operatorname {tr}(xyz^* - yxz^*) = \operatorname {tr}\bigl(y(z^*x - xz^*)\bigr) = \operatorname {tr}\bigl(y[x^*, z]^*\bigr) = \langle y, [x^*, z]\rangle . \]

It also holds for the Lie algebra of a compact Lie group, with an invariant inner product (Chapter 33).

02QK Example 12.7.14

The classical Lie algebras. Let \(J = \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & I_n \\ -I_n & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\) and let \(\mathfrak {sp}_{2n}(k) = \{ x \in \mathfrak {gl}_{2n}(k) : x^TJ + Jx = 0\} \), the Lie algebra of the bilinear form \(v^TJw\) in the sense of Example 12.4.2(3). Writing \(x\) in blocks of size \(n\), \(x^TJ + Jx = 0\) says exactly that

\[ x = \begin{pmatrix} A & B \\ C & -A^T \end{pmatrix}, \qquad B = B^T, \quad C = C^T, \]

so \(\dim \mathfrak {sp}_{2n} = n^2 + n(n+1) = n(2n + 1)\). The Lie algebras \(\mathfrak {sl}_n(\mathbb {C})\) (\(n \geq 2\)), \(\mathfrak {so}_n(\mathbb {C})\) (\(n \geq 3\)) and \(\mathfrak {sp}_{2n}(\mathbb {C})\) (\(n \geq 1\)) are semisimple, and \(\mathfrak {gl}_n(\mathbb {C}) = \mathbb {C}I \oplus \mathfrak {sl}_n(\mathbb {C})\) is reductive. All four are closed under \(x \mapsto x^*\): for \(\mathfrak {so}_n\) the conjugate of a skew-symmetric matrix is skew-symmetric, and for \(\mathfrak {sp}_{2n}\), conjugating \(x^TJ + Jx = 0\) and multiplying by \(J^{-1} = -J\) on both sides gives \(\bar xJ + J\bar x^T = 0\), which is the condition for \(x^*\). By Proposition 12.7.13 it suffices to compute the centres.

  1. \(\mathfrak {sl}_n\): if \(x\) commutes with \(E_{ij}\), \(i \neq j\), comparing the entries in row \(i\) of \(E_{ij}x = xE_{ij}\) gives \(x_{jl} = 0\) for \(l \neq j\) and \(x_{jj} = x_{ii}\). So a central \(x\) is a scalar matrix \(cI\) with \(nc = \operatorname {tr}x = 0\), and \(x = 0\). The centre of \(\mathfrak {gl}_n\) is \(\mathbb {C}I\) by the same computation.

  2. \(\mathfrak {so}_n\), \(n \geq 3\): a central \(x\) commutes with \(F_{ij} = E_{ij} - E_{ji}\), so it preserves its image \(\operatorname {span}(e_i, e_j)\). For distinct \(i\), \(j\), \(l\) it preserves \(\operatorname {span}(e_i, e_j) \cap \operatorname {span}(e_i, e_l) = \mathbb {C}e_i\); so \(x\) is diagonal, and a diagonal skew-symmetric matrix is \(0\). (For \(n = 2\), \(\mathfrak {so}_2\) is one-dimensional, hence abelian.)

  3. \(\mathfrak {sp}_{2n}\): write a central \(x\) in blocks \(P\), \(Q\), \(R\), \(S\). Commuting with all \(\operatorname {diag}(A, -A^T) \in \mathfrak {sp}_{2n}\) means \(PA = AP\), \(QA^T = -AQ\), \(RA = -A^TR\) and \(SA^T = A^TS\) for all \(A \in \mathfrak {gl}_n\); so \(P\) and \(S\) are scalar, and \(A = I\) gives \(Q = R = 0\). Then \(x = p\operatorname {diag}(I, -I)\), and commuting with \(\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & I \\ 0 & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr) \in \mathfrak {sp}_{2n}\) gives \(2p = 0\).

The real Lie algebras \(\mathfrak {su}(n)\) (\(n \geq 2\)) and \(\mathfrak {so}(n)\) (\(n \geq 3\)) are semisimple for the same reason, with the real inner product \(\operatorname {Re}\operatorname {tr}(xy^*)\) and \(x^* = -x\). These are the Lie algebras \(\mathfrak {sl}_n\) (\(n \geq 2\)), \(\mathfrak {so}_{2n+1}\) (\(n \geq 1\)), \(\mathfrak {sp}_{2n}\) (\(n \geq 1\)) and \(\mathfrak {so}_{2n}\) (\(n \geq 2\)) of the four infinite families in the classification (Section 12.10).
