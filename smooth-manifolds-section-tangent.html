32.4 Tangent vectors017Q

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

For a surface \(S \subseteq \mathbb {R}^3\), the tangent plane at \(p\) is the set of velocities \(\gamma '(0)\) of curves in \(S\) through \(p\), a linear subspace of \(\mathbb {R}^3\). An abstract manifold has no ambient space in which such velocities live, and there are three ways around this:

  1. regard a tangent vector as a curve through \(p\), two curves being identified when they have the same velocity in a chart;

  2. regard a tangent vector as the operation “derivative in this direction” on functions;

  3. regard a tangent vector as a vector of \(\mathbb {R}^n\) in each chart, the vectors attached to two charts being related by the Jacobian matrix of the transition map.

We take (b) as the definition, because it is visibly independent of all choices and visibly a vector space, and then prove that (a) and (c) give the same thing (Propositions 32.4.7 and 32.4.10). The model is \(\mathbb {R}^n\): a vector \(v \in \mathbb {R}^n\) gives the directional derivative \(f \mapsto \frac{d}{dt}f(a + tv)|_{t=0} = \sum _iv^i\partial _if(a)\) at \(a\), which is linear in \(f\) and satisfies the product rule.

017R Definition 32.4.1

Let \(M\) be a smooth manifold and \(p \in M\). A derivation at \(p\) is a linear map \(v \colon C^\infty (M) \to \mathbb {R}\) satisfying the Leibniz rule

\[ v(fg) = v(f)g(p) + f(p)v(g). \]

The tangent space \(T_pM\) is the real vector space of derivations at \(p\); its elements are tangent vectors at \(p\). A smooth map \(f \colon M \to N\) induces the differential \(d_pf \colon T_pM \to T_{f(p)}N\), \((d_pf(v))(g) = v(g \circ f)\) for \(g \in C^\infty (N)\).

The differential is well defined: \(d_pf(v)\) is linear, and it satisfies the Leibniz rule at \(f(p)\), because \(v((g \circ f)(h \circ f)) = v(g \circ f)h(f(p)) + g(f(p))v(h \circ f)\). It is clearly linear in \(v\).

02E0 Lemma 32.4.2

Let \(v \in T_pM\) and \(f, g \in C^\infty (M)\).

  1. \(v(c) = 0\) for every constant function \(c\).

  2. If \(f(p) = g(p) = 0\), then \(v(fg) = 0\).

  3. (Locality) If \(f = g\) on a neighbourhood of \(p\), then \(v(f) = v(g)\).

Proof â–¼

(1) The Leibniz rule gives \(v(1) = v(1 \cdot 1) = 2v(1)\), so \(v(1) = 0\), and \(v(c) = cv(1) = 0\) by linearity. (2) is the Leibniz rule. (3) The function \(h = f - g\) vanishes on an open neighbourhood \(W\) of \(p\). Choose \(\chi \) as in Lemma 32.3.10 with support in \(W\) and \(\chi (p) = 1\). Then \(\chi h = 0\) everywhere (on \(W\) because \(h = 0\), off \(W\) because \(\chi = 0\)), so \(0 = v(\chi h) = v(\chi )h(p) + \chi (p)v(h) = v(h)\).

02E1 Proposition 32.4.3

Let \(W \subseteq M\) be open, \(p \in W\), and \(\iota \colon W \to M\) the inclusion. Then \(d_p\iota \colon T_pW \to T_pM\) is an isomorphism.

We use it to identify \(T_pW\) with \(T_pM\); in particular a tangent vector at \(p\) can be applied to functions defined only near \(p\).

Proof â–¼

Fix \(\chi \) as in Lemma 32.3.10 with compact support inside \(W\) and \(\chi = 1\) near \(p\). For \(g \in C^\infty (W)\), let \(\widetilde{\chi g}\) be the function equal to \(\chi g\) on \(W\) and to \(0\) outside \(W\). It is smooth on \(M\), because it is smooth on \(W\) and vanishes on the open set \(M \setminus \operatorname {supp}\chi \), and these two open sets cover \(M\). It agrees with \(g\) near \(p\).

Injectivity. Suppose \(d_p\iota (v) = 0\), that is, \(v(f|_W) = 0\) for all \(f \in C^\infty (M)\). For \(g \in C^\infty (W)\), the functions \(g\) and \(\widetilde{\chi g}|_W\) agree near \(p\), so \(v(g) = v(\widetilde{\chi g}|_W) = 0\) by Lemma 32.4.2(3). So \(v = 0\).

Surjectivity. Let \(w \in T_pM\), and define \(v(g) = w(\widetilde{\chi g})\) for \(g \in C^\infty (W)\). It is linear. It satisfies the Leibniz rule: the functions \(\widetilde{\chi gh}\) and \(\widetilde{\chi g}\, \widetilde{\chi h}\) agree near \(p\) (both equal \(gh\) there), so by locality \(v(gh) = w(\widetilde{\chi g}\, \widetilde{\chi h}) = v(g)h(p) + g(p)v(h)\). Finally, for \(f \in C^\infty (M)\) the functions \(\widetilde{\chi f|_W}\) and \(f\) agree near \(p\), so \(d_p\iota (v)(f) = v(f|_W) = w(f)\).

02E2 Lemma 32.4.4 Hadamard

Let \(B = B(a, r) \subseteq \mathbb {R}^n\) and \(f \in C^\infty (B)\). There are \(g_1, \ldots , g_n \in C^\infty (B)\) with \(g_i(a) = \partial _if(a)\) and

\[ f(x) = f(a) + \sum _{i=1}^n(x^i - a^i)g_i(x) \qquad (x \in B). \]
Proof â–¼

For \(x \in B\) the segment from \(a\) to \(x\) lies in \(B\). By the chain rule, \(h(t) = f(a + t(x - a))\) has derivative \(h'(t) = \sum _i(x^i - a^i)\partial _if(a + t(x - a))\), so by the fundamental theorem of calculus (Theorem 26.8.3)

\[ f(x) - f(a) = \int _0^1h'(t)\, dt = \sum _i(x^i - a^i)g_i(x), \qquad g_i(x) = \int _0^1\partial _if(a + t(x - a))\, dt, \]

and \(g_i(a) = \partial _if(a)\). It remains to show that the \(g_i\) are smooth. Consider the functions \(K(x) = \int _0^1t^m\ell (a + t(x - a))\, dt\) with \(m \geq 0\) and \(\ell \in C^\infty (B)\). Each is continuous: for \(x_0 \in B\) choose a closed ball \(\overline{B}' \subseteq B\) around \(x_0\); the integrand is uniformly continuous on the compact set \(\overline{B}' \times [0, 1]\), so \(K(x) \to K(x_0)\) as \(x \to x_0\). Each has partial derivatives, of the same form: by Proposition 26.8.5(1), applied in the variable \(x^j\) with the other coordinates fixed, \(\partial _jK(x) = \int _0^1t^{m+1}(\partial _j\ell )(a + t(x - a))\, dt\). By induction, all partial derivatives of all orders of \(K\) exist and are continuous, so \(K\) is smooth (Proposition 26.10.3(3)). The \(g_i\) are of this form, with \(m = 0\) and \(\ell = \partial _if\).

02E3 Proposition 32.4.5

Let \(U \subseteq \mathbb {R}^n\) be open and \(a \in U\). For \(v \in \mathbb {R}^n\) let \(D_v|_a \in T_aU\) be the directional derivative \(D_v|_a(f) = \sum _iv^i\partial _if(a)\). Then \(v \mapsto D_v|_a\) is an isomorphism \(\mathbb {R}^n \to T_aU\), which sends \(e_i\) to \(\partial /\partial x^i|_a \colon f \mapsto \partial _if(a)\). If \(F \colon U \to V \subseteq \mathbb {R}^m\) is smooth, then \(d_aF(D_v|_a) = D_{DF(a)v}|_{F(a)}\): under these isomorphisms the differential is the derivative \(DF(a)\), with the Jacobian matrix.

Proof â–¼

\(D_v|_a\) is linear in \(f\) and satisfies the Leibniz rule by the product rule, and it is linear in \(v\). It is injective in \(v\), since \(D_v|_a(x^j) = v^j\) for the coordinate function \(x^j\). For surjectivity, let \(w \in T_aU\) and put \(v^i = w(x^i)\). Let \(f \in C^\infty (U)\), choose \(r {\gt} 0\) with \(B = B(a, r) \subseteq U\), and write \(f = f(a) + \sum _i(x^i - a^i)g_i\) on \(B\) (Lemma 32.4.4). By Proposition 32.4.3 we may apply \(w\) to functions on \(B\), and by the Leibniz rule and Lemma 32.4.2(1),

\[ w(f) = w(f(a)) + \sum _i\bigl(w(x^i - a^i)g_i(a) + (a^i - a^i)w(g_i)\bigr) = \sum _iv^i\partial _if(a). \]

So \(w = D_v|_a\). Finally, for \(g \in C^\infty (V)\) the chain rule gives \(d_aF(D_v|_a)(g) = D_v|_a(g \circ F) = \sum _j v^j\sum _i\partial _ig(F(a))\partial _jF^i(a) = \sum _i(DF(a)v)^i\partial _ig(F(a))\).

017S Proposition 32.4.6

Let \(M\) be a smooth \(n\)-manifold and \(p \in M\).

  1. Derivations are local: if \(f = g\) near \(p\) then \(v(f) = v(g)\); and \(T_pU = T_pM\) for every open \(U \ni p\). So \(T_pM\) may be computed in any chart.

  2. If \((U, \varphi = (x^1, \ldots , x^n))\) is a chart at \(p\), the derivations

    \[ \frac{\partial }{\partial x^i}\Big|_p \colon f \mapsto \partial _i(f \circ \varphi ^{-1})(\varphi (p)) \]

    form a basis of \(T_pM\), and every \(v \in T_pM\) is \(v = \sum _iv(x^i)\, \partial /\partial x^i|_p\). In particular \(\dim T_pM = n\).

  3. \(d_p(g \circ f) = d_{f(p)}g \circ d_pf\) and \(d_p\mathrm{id}= \mathrm{id}\). In charts \((x^j)\) at \(p\) and \((y^i)\) at \(f(p)\), with local representative \(F = \psi \circ f \circ \varphi ^{-1}\),

    \[ d_pf\Bigl(\frac{\partial }{\partial x^j}\Big|_p\Bigr) = \sum _i\partial _jF^i(\varphi (p))\frac{\partial }{\partial y^i}\Big|_{f(p)}, \]

    so the matrix of \(d_pf\) is the Jacobian matrix of \(F\). A diffeomorphism has invertible differentials.

Proof â–¼

We first prove the chain rule of (3). For \(h \in C^\infty \) of the target, \(d_p(g \circ f)(v)(h) = v(h \circ g \circ f) = d_pf(v)(h \circ g) = d_{f(p)}g(d_pf(v))(h)\), and \(d_p\mathrm{id}(v)(h) = v(h)\). If \(f\) is a diffeomorphism, applying this to \(f^{-1} \circ f = \mathrm{id}\) and \(f \circ f^{-1} = \mathrm{id}\) shows that \(d_pf\) is invertible.

(1) is Lemma 32.4.2(3) and Proposition 32.4.3.

(2) By Proposition 32.3.3(3), \(\varphi \colon U \to \varphi (U)\) is a diffeomorphism, so by the chain rule \(d_{\varphi (p)}(\varphi ^{-1}) \colon T_{\varphi (p)}\varphi (U) \to T_pU = T_pM\) is an isomorphism. By Proposition 32.4.5 the vectors \(\partial /\partial x^i|_{\varphi (p)}\) form a basis of \(T_{\varphi (p)}\varphi (U)\), and \(d_{\varphi (p)}(\varphi ^{-1})\) sends \(\partial /\partial x^i|_{\varphi (p)}\) to \(f \mapsto \partial _i(f \circ \varphi ^{-1})(\varphi (p))\), which is \(\partial /\partial x^i|_p\). So these form a basis of \(T_pM\). Since \(x^j \circ \varphi ^{-1}\) is the \(j\)-th coordinate function of \(\mathbb {R}^n\), \(\partial /\partial x^i|_p(x^j) = \delta _{ij}\); so if \(v = \sum _ic^i\partial /\partial x^i|_p\), applying \(v\) to \(x^j\) gives \(c^j = v(x^j)\).

(3) By (2), the coefficient of \(\partial /\partial y^i|_{f(p)}\) in \(d_pf(\partial /\partial x^j|_p)\) is \(d_pf(\partial /\partial x^j|_p)(y^i) = \partial /\partial x^j|_p(y^i \circ f) = \partial _j(y^i \circ f \circ \varphi ^{-1})(\varphi (p)) = \partial _jF^i(\varphi (p))\).

02E4 Proposition 32.4.7

Let \((x^1, \ldots , x^n)\) and \((y^1, \ldots , y^n)\) be the coordinates of two charts \(\varphi \), \(\psi \) at \(p\), and write \(\partial y^j/\partial x^i(p) = \partial /\partial x^i|_p(y^j) = \partial _i(y^j \circ \varphi ^{-1})(\varphi (p))\), the entries of the Jacobian matrix of the transition map \(\psi \circ \varphi ^{-1}\) at \(\varphi (p)\). Then

\[ \frac{\partial }{\partial x^i}\Big|_p = \sum _j\frac{\partial y^j}{\partial x^i}(p)\frac{\partial }{\partial y^j}\Big|_p, \]

and if \(v = \sum _ia^i\, \partial /\partial x^i|_p = \sum _jb^j\, \partial /\partial y^j|_p\), then \(b^j = \sum _i\frac{\partial y^j}{\partial x^i}(p)\, a^i\).

Proof â–¼

By Proposition 32.4.6(2) applied to the chart \(\psi \), the coefficient of \(\partial /\partial y^j|_p\) in \(\partial /\partial x^i|_p\) is \(\partial /\partial x^i|_p(y^j)\). The formula for the components follows by linearity.

So the vector \((a^1, \ldots , a^n)\) of components of \(v\) in the chart \(\varphi \) and the vector \((b^1, \ldots , b^n)\) of components in \(\psi \) are related by the Jacobian matrix \(D(\psi \circ \varphi ^{-1})(\varphi (p))\). This is description (c) above: a tangent vector is the same thing as a rule that assigns to each chart at \(p\) a vector of \(\mathbb {R}^n\), in such a way that the vectors attached to any two charts are related by the Jacobian matrix of the transition map.

02E5 Example 32.4.8

Let \(U = \mathbb {R}^2 \setminus \{ (x, 0) : x \leq 0\} \). The map \(P(r, \theta ) = (r\cos \theta , r\sin \theta )\) is a bijection \((0, \infty ) \times (-\pi , \pi ) \to U\), since every point of \(U\) has a unique angle in \((-\pi , \pi )\), and its Jacobian determinant is \(r {\gt} 0\) (compare Exercise 26.14.4). By the inverse function theorem its inverse is smooth, so \((r, \theta ) = P^{-1}\) is a chart of \(\mathbb {R}^2\) (Proposition 32.3.3(3)), the polar coordinates. With \(x = r\cos \theta \) and \(y = r\sin \theta \), Proposition 32.4.7 gives

\[ \frac{\partial }{\partial r} = \cos \theta \, \frac{\partial }{\partial x} + \sin \theta \, \frac{\partial }{\partial y} = \frac{x}{r}\frac{\partial }{\partial x} + \frac{y}{r}\frac{\partial }{\partial y}, \qquad \frac{\partial }{\partial \theta } = -r\sin \theta \, \frac{\partial }{\partial x} + r\cos \theta \, \frac{\partial }{\partial y} = -y\frac{\partial }{\partial x} + x\frac{\partial }{\partial y}. \]

So \(\partial /\partial r\) is the unit radial vector and \(\partial /\partial \theta \) is the velocity of rotation about the origin. Note that \(\partial /\partial r\) depends on the whole chart \((r, \theta )\), not only on the function \(r\): in the chart \((r, y)\), defined where \(x {\gt} 0\), the vector \(\partial /\partial r\) is \((r/x)\, \partial /\partial x\), a different vector. The formula \(dr = \cos \theta \, dx + \sin \theta \, dy\) that one often meets is a different statement, about the differential of the function \(r\), a covector rather than a vector; the two are compared in Chapter 35.

Now description (a). A smooth curve in \(M\) is a smooth map \(\gamma \colon J \to M\) from an open interval \(J \subseteq \mathbb {R}\).

0277 Proposition 32.4.9

For a smooth curve \(\gamma \colon J \to M\) and \(t_0 \in J\), the velocity \(\gamma '(t_0) = d_{t_0}\gamma (d/dt|_{t_0}) \in T_{\gamma (t_0)}M\) is the derivation \(\gamma '(t_0)f = (f \circ \gamma )'(t_0)\). In a chart,

\[ \gamma '(t_0) = \sum _i(x^i \circ \gamma )'(t_0)\frac{\partial }{\partial x^i}\Big|_{\gamma (t_0)}. \]

Every \(v \in T_pM\) is the velocity \(\gamma '(0)\) of a smooth curve with \(\gamma (0) = p\), and for smooth \(f \colon M \to N\) we have \(d_pf(\gamma '(0)) = (f \circ \gamma )'(0)\).

Proof â–¼

By definition \(\gamma '(t_0)f = d/dt|_{t_0}(f \circ \gamma ) = (f \circ \gamma )'(t_0)\). The chart formula follows from Proposition 32.4.6(2), since \(\gamma '(t_0)(x^i) = (x^i \circ \gamma )'(t_0)\). Given \(v = \sum _iv^i\partial /\partial x^i|_p\) in a chart \(\varphi \) centred at \(p\), let \(\gamma (t) = \varphi ^{-1}(tv^1, \ldots , tv^n)\) for \(|t|\) small enough that \(tv \in \varphi (U)\). Then \(x^i \circ \gamma (t) = tv^i\), so \(\gamma '(0) = v\) by the chart formula. Finally \(d_pf(\gamma '(0)) = d_pf \circ d_0\gamma (d/dt|_0) = d_0(f \circ \gamma )(d/dt|_0) = (f \circ \gamma )'(0)\) by the chain rule.

02E6 Proposition 32.4.10

Let \(\gamma \) and \(\delta \) be smooth curves defined near \(0\) with \(\gamma (0) = \delta (0) = p\). The following are equivalent: (a) \(\gamma '(0) = \delta '(0)\); (b) \((\varphi \circ \gamma )'(0) = (\varphi \circ \delta )'(0)\) for some chart \(\varphi \) at \(p\); (c) the same holds for every chart at \(p\). Consequently \([\gamma ] \mapsto \gamma '(0)\) is a bijection from the set of smooth curves through \(p\) at time \(0\), modulo the equivalence relation (b), onto \(T_pM\).

Proof â–¼

In a chart \(\varphi = (x^i)\), the vector \((\varphi \circ \gamma )'(0) \in \mathbb {R}^n\) is the vector of components of \(\gamma '(0)\) in the basis \(\partial /\partial x^i|_p\) (Proposition 32.4.9), and similarly for \(\delta \). Since the \(\partial /\partial x^i|_p\) form a basis, (a) is equivalent to (b) for this chart, whichever chart is chosen; this proves the equivalences. The map is well defined and injective by the equivalence of (a) and (b), and surjective by Proposition 32.4.9.

02E7 Remark 32.4.11

We now have three equivalent descriptions of \(T_pM\): derivations at \(p\) (the definition); classes of curves through \(p\) (Proposition 32.4.10); and vectors of components that change by the Jacobian matrix of the transition map (Proposition 32.4.7). Each is useful: derivations for algebra (vector fields as derivations, the Lie bracket), curves for geometry (velocities, flows), components for computation.

02E8 Corollary 32.4.12

If nonempty smooth manifolds \(M\) and \(N\) are diffeomorphic, then \(\dim M = \dim N\).

Proof â–¼

A diffeomorphism \(f\) gives an isomorphism \(d_pf \colon T_pM \to T_{f(p)}N\) (Proposition 32.4.6(3)), and these spaces have dimensions \(\dim M\) and \(\dim N\) by Proposition 32.4.6(2).

The topological statement, that homeomorphic nonempty open subsets of \(\mathbb {R}^m\) and \(\mathbb {R}^n\) force \(m = n\), is harder and needs homology (Corollary 20.6.6(3)); here linear algebra suffices because smooth structures come with tangent spaces.

02E9 Proposition 32.4.13

Let \(M\) and \(N\) be smooth manifolds with projections \(\mathrm{pr}_M\), \(\mathrm{pr}_N\) from \(M \times N\). For \((p, q) \in M \times N\), the map

\[ (d\, \mathrm{pr}_M, d\, \mathrm{pr}_N) \colon T_{(p,q)}(M \times N) \to T_pM \oplus T_qN \]

is an isomorphism. Its inverse sends \((v, w)\) to \(d_p\iota _q(v) + d_q\iota _p(w)\), where \(\iota _q(x) = (x, q)\) and \(\iota _p(y) = (p, y)\).

Proof â–¼

In product charts \((x^1, \ldots , x^m, y^1, \ldots , y^n)\) the projection \(\mathrm{pr}_M\) is \((x, y) \mapsto x\), so its differential sends \(\partial /\partial x^i\) to \(\partial /\partial x^i\) and \(\partial /\partial y^j\) to \(0\) (Proposition 32.4.6(3)); similarly for \(\mathrm{pr}_N\). So the map sends a basis to a basis. The maps \(\iota _q\) and \(\iota _p\) are smooth (Proposition 32.3.3(5)), and in the same charts \(d\iota _q(\partial /\partial x^i) = \partial /\partial x^i\) and \(d\iota _p(\partial /\partial y^j) = \partial /\partial y^j\), which gives the inverse.

The inverse function theorem of analysis now carries over to manifolds.

02EA Theorem 32.4.14 Inverse function theorem on manifolds

Let \(f \colon M \to N\) be smooth and \(p \in M\) with \(d_pf\) an isomorphism. There are open sets \(U \ni p\) and \(V \ni f(p)\) such that \(f|_U \colon U \to V\) is a diffeomorphism. Consequently, a smooth bijection whose differential is everywhere an isomorphism is a diffeomorphism.

Proof â–¼

Choose charts \((U_0, \varphi )\) at \(p\) and \((V_0, \psi )\) at \(f(p)\) with \(f(U_0) \subseteq V_0\), and let \(F = \psi \circ f \circ \varphi ^{-1}\). By Proposition 32.4.6(3), \(DF(\varphi (p))\) is the matrix of \(d_pf\), so it is invertible (and \(\dim M = \dim N\)). By Theorem 26.11.2 there are open sets \(A \ni \varphi (p)\) in \(\varphi (U_0)\) and \(B \ni F(\varphi (p))\) such that \(F \colon A \to B\) is a diffeomorphism. Put \(U = \varphi ^{-1}(A)\) and \(V = \psi ^{-1}(B)\). Then \(f|_U = \psi ^{-1} \circ F \circ \varphi \) is a bijection \(U \to V\), and its inverse \(\varphi ^{-1} \circ F^{-1} \circ \psi \) is smooth as a composite of smooth maps. For the last statement, the inverse of such a bijection is smooth near every point, hence smooth.

A smooth map whose differential is everywhere an isomorphism is called a local diffeomorphism.
