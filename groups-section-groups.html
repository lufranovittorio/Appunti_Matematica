6.2 Groups and homomorphisms00BX

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

00BY Definition 6.2.1

A group is a set \(G\) together with a map \(G \times G \to G\), called the group law and written \((a, b) \mapsto a \cdot b\), and an element \(e \in G\), such that for all \(a, b, c \in G\):

  1. (associativity) \((a \cdot b) \cdot c = a \cdot (b \cdot c)\);

  2. (neutral element) \(e \cdot a = a \cdot e = a\);

  3. (inverses) there is an element \(a' \in G\) with \(a \cdot a' = a' \cdot a = e\).

The group is abelian if \(a \cdot b = b \cdot a\) for all \(a, b \in G\). The order of \(G\) is its cardinality \(|G|\).

00IE Notation 6.2.2

Two notations are used for the group law.

  • Multiplicative notation, used for all groups: the group law is written \(a \cdot b\) or simply \(ab\), the neutral element \(e\) (or \(1\)), the inverse of \(a\) is written \(a^{-1}\) (it is unique by Lemma 6.2.3), and \(a^n\) denotes the \(n\)-th power (Lemma 6.2.9).

  • Additive notation, used only for abelian groups: the group law is written \(a + b\), the neutral element \(0\), the inverse \(-a\), and \(na\) the \(n\)-th multiple.

Every statement in multiplicative notation applies to abelian groups written additively through the dictionary \(ab \leftrightarrow a + b\), \(e \leftrightarrow 0\), \(a^{-1} \leftrightarrow -a\), \(a^n \leftrightarrow na\). General results in this chapter are stated multiplicatively. For groups arising from a familiar structure we keep the familiar symbol: \(+\) for \((\mathbb {Z}, +)\), multiplication for \(\mathbb {Q}^\times \) or for invertible matrices, and composition \(\circ \) (written as juxtaposition) for groups of bijections. The symbol \(*\) never denotes a group law; \(G_1 * G_2\) is the free product of Definition 6.10.6. When the group law has to be named, we write \((G, \cdot )\) or \((G, +)\).

00BZ Lemma 6.2.3

In a group the neutral element and inverses are unique, \((ab)^{-1} = b^{-1}a^{-1}\), \((a^{-1})^{-1} = a\), and \(ab = ac\) or \(ba = ca\) implies \(b = c\).

Proof â–¼

If \(e'\) is also neutral, \(e = ee' = e'\). If \(b\) and \(c\) are inverses of \(a\), then \(b = b(ac) = (ba)c = c\); we write \(a^{-1}\) for the inverse. The element \(b^{-1}a^{-1}\) is inverse to \(ab\), and \(a\) is inverse to \(a^{-1}\). Cancellation follows by multiplying with \(a^{-1}\).

00C0 Example 6.2.4

  1. \((\mathbb {Z}, +)\), \((\mathbb {Q}, +)\), \((\mathbb {R}, +)\) and \((\mathbb {C}, +)\) are abelian groups, as are \(\mathbb {Q}^\times = \mathbb {Q}\setminus \{ 0\} \), \(\mathbb {R}^\times \) and \(\mathbb {C}^\times \) under multiplication. More generally, for a commutative ring \(R\) the invertible elements form a group \(R^\times \).

  2. For a set \(X\), the bijections \(X \to X\) form the symmetric group \(\mathrm{Sym}(X)\) under composition. We write \(S_n = \mathrm{Sym}(\{ 1, \ldots , n\} )\).

  3. For a field \(k\), the invertible \(n \times n\) matrices form the group \(\mathrm{GL}_n(k)\) (Chapter 9).

  4. The trivial group has one element.

  5. A group with one object as a category (Example 5.2.8) is a category with one object in which every morphism is an isomorphism.

00C1 Definition 6.2.5

A homomorphism of groups is a map \(f \colon G \to H\) with \(f(ab) = f(a)f(b)\) for all \(a, b \in G\). Its kernel is \(\ker f = \{ a \in G : f(a) = e\} \) and its image is \(\operatorname {im}f = f[G]\). An isomorphism is a bijective homomorphism; an automorphism of \(G\) is an isomorphism \(G \to G\), and they form a group \(\operatorname {Aut}(G)\). Small groups and homomorphisms form a locally small category \(\mathsf{Grp}\), with the full subcategory \(\mathsf{Ab}\) of abelian groups.

00C2 Lemma 6.2.6

A homomorphism \(f\) satisfies \(f(e) = e\) and \(f(a^{-1}) = f(a)^{-1}\); it is injective if and only if \(\ker f = \{ e\} \); and the inverse of a bijective homomorphism is a homomorphism.

Proof â–¼

\(f(e) = f(e)f(e)\) gives \(f(e) = e\) by cancellation, and then \(f(a)f(a^{-1}) = f(e) = e\). If \(\ker f\) is trivial and \(f(a) = f(b)\), then \(f(ab^{-1}) = e\), so \(a = b\). If \(f\) is bijective with inverse \(g\), then \(f(g(x)g(y)) = xy\), so \(g(xy) = g(x)g(y)\).

00C3 Definition 6.2.7

A subgroup of \(G\) is a subset \(H\) containing \(e\) with \(ab \in H\) and \(a^{-1} \in H\) for all \(a, b \in H\); it is a group with the restricted operation. We write \(H \leq G\). For a subset \(S \subseteq G\), the subgroup generated by \(S\), written \(\langle S \rangle \), is the intersection of all subgroups containing \(S\).

00C4 Lemma 6.2.8

The subgroup \(\langle S \rangle \) consists of all finite products \(s_1^{\epsilon _1} \cdots s_k^{\epsilon _k}\) with \(k \geq 0\), \(s_i \in S\) and \(\epsilon _i \in \{ 1, -1\} \) (the empty product being \(e\)). Kernels and images of homomorphisms are subgroups.

Proof â–¼

The set of such products contains \(S\) and is closed under products and inverses (Lemma 6.2.3), so it contains \(\langle S \rangle \); and every subgroup containing \(S\) contains all such products. The second statement follows from Lemma 6.2.6.

00C5 Lemma 6.2.9

Let \(G\) be a group and \(g \in G\). There is a unique homomorphism \(\mathbb {Z}\to G\) sending \(1\) to \(g\); we write \(n \mapsto g^n\). Thus \(g^{m+n} = g^m g^n\) and \((g^m)^n = g^{mn}\) for all \(m, n \in \mathbb {Z}\).

Proof â–¼

Uniqueness is clear since \(1\) generates \(\mathbb {Z}\). For existence define \(g^n\) for \(n \in \mathbb {N}\) by recursion (\(g^0 = e\), \(g^{n+1} = g^n g\)) and put \(g^{-n} = (g^{-1})^n\). One checks \(g^{m+1} = g^m g\) for all \(m \in \mathbb {Z}\) (for \(m = -n - 1 {\lt} 0\) this reads \((g^{-1})^{n} = (g^{-1})^{n+1} g\), which holds since \(g^{-1}g = e\)), and then \(g^{m+n} = g^m g^n\) follows by induction on \(n \geq 0\) and on \(-n \geq 0\). The last formula holds because \(n \mapsto g^{mn}\) and \(n \mapsto (g^m)^n\) are both homomorphisms sending \(1\) to \(g^m\).

00C6 Proposition 6.2.10

Every subgroup of \(\mathbb {Z}\) is of the form \(n\mathbb {Z}= \{ nk : k \in \mathbb {Z}\} \) for a unique \(n \in \mathbb {N}\).

Proof â–¼

Let \(H \leq \mathbb {Z}\). If \(H = \{ 0\} \) take \(n = 0\). Otherwise \(H\) contains a positive element, and we let \(n\) be the least one. Then \(n\mathbb {Z}\subseteq H\), and for \(h \in H\), division with remainder (Theorem 4.5.2) gives \(h = qn + r\) with \(0 \leq r {\lt} n\) and \(r = h - qn \in H\), so \(r = 0\). The number \(n\) is the least positive element of \(n\mathbb {Z}\), hence unique.

00C7 Definition 6.2.11

The order of \(g \in G\) is the least \(n \geq 1\) with \(g^n = e\) if it exists, and \(\infty \) otherwise. By Proposition 6.2.10, the kernel of \(n \mapsto g^n\) is \(\operatorname {ord}(g)\mathbb {Z}\) (with \(\infty \mathbb {Z}:= \{ 0\} \)). A group is cyclic if it is generated by one element.
