30.11 Dual spaces and reflexivity0334

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

To understand a Banach space one often determines its dual. For sequence spaces this is elementary. Let \(c_0 \subseteq \ell ^\infty \) be the closed subspace of sequences tending to \(0\), and for \(1 \leq p \leq \infty \) let \(q\) be the conjugate exponent, \(1/p + 1/q = 1\) (with \(q = \infty \) for \(p = 1\) and \(q = 1\) for \(p = \infty \)). For a sequence \(y\), let \(\Lambda _y(x) = \sum _nx_ny_n\) whenever this converges absolutely.

0335 Proposition 30.11.1

  1. For \(1 \leq p {\lt} \infty \), the map \(y \mapsto \Lambda _y\) is an isometric isomorphism \(\ell ^q \to (\ell ^p)^*\).

  2. The map \(y \mapsto \Lambda _y\) is an isometric isomorphism \(\ell ^1 \to (c_0)^*\).

Proof â–¼

(1) By Hölder’s inequality for the counting measure (Proposition 28.5.2), \(|\Lambda _y(x)| \leq \| x\| _p\| y\| _q\), so \(\Lambda _y \in (\ell ^p)^*\) with \(\| \Lambda _y\| \leq \| y\| _q\), and \(y \mapsto \Lambda _y\) is linear and injective, as \(\Lambda _y(e_n) = y_n\) for the unit vectors \(e_n\). Let \(\Lambda \in (\ell ^p)^*\) and \(y_n = \Lambda (e_n)\); we show \(\| y\| _q \leq \| \Lambda \| \) and \(\Lambda = \Lambda _y\). If \(p = 1\), \(|y_n| = |\Lambda (e_n)| \leq \| \Lambda \| \), so \(\| y\| _\infty \leq \| \Lambda \| \). If \(1 {\lt} p {\lt} \infty \), so \(q {\lt} \infty \) and \((q - 1)p = q\), let \(x^{(N)}_n = |y_n|^{q-2}\bar y_n\) for \(n \leq N\) (and \(0\) if \(y_n = 0\) or \(n {\gt} N\)). Then

\[ \sum _{n \leq N}|y_n|^q = \Lambda (x^{(N)}) \leq \| \Lambda \| \, \| x^{(N)}\| _p = \| \Lambda \| \Bigl(\sum _{n \leq N}|y_n|^{(q-1)p}\Bigr)^{1/p} = \| \Lambda \| \Bigl(\sum _{n \leq N}|y_n|^q\Bigr)^{1/p}, \]

so \((\sum _{n \leq N}|y_n|^q)^{1/q} \leq \| \Lambda \| \) for all \(N\), and \(\| y\| _q \leq \| \Lambda \| \). In both cases \(\Lambda \) and \(\Lambda _y\) are continuous and agree on the finitely supported sequences, which are dense in \(\ell ^p\) for \(p {\lt} \infty \) (for \(x \in \ell ^p\) the tail \(\sum _{n {\gt} N}|x_n|^p\) tends to \(0\)); so \(\Lambda = \Lambda _y\), and \(\| \Lambda \| \leq \| y\| _q \leq \| \Lambda \| \).

(2) In the same way, \(|\Lambda _y(x)| \leq \| x\| _\infty \| y\| _1\) for \(x \in c_0\) and \(y \in \ell ^1\). Given \(\Lambda \in c_0^*\), put \(y_n = \Lambda (e_n)\) and \(x^{(N)}_n = \bar y_n/|y_n|\) for \(n \leq N\), \(y_n \neq 0\) (and \(0\) otherwise); then \(\| x^{(N)}\| _\infty \leq 1\) and \(\sum _{n \leq N}|y_n| = \Lambda (x^{(N)}) \leq \| \Lambda \| \). The finitely supported sequences are dense in \(c_0\), so again \(\Lambda = \Lambda _y\) and \(\| \Lambda \| = \| y\| _1\).

The dual of \(\ell ^\infty \) is strictly larger than \(\ell ^1\). The subspace \(c_0\) is closed and not dense in \(\ell ^\infty \), since \(\mathbf{1} = (1, 1, \ldots )\) has distance \(1\) from it, so by Corollary 30.10.4(4) there is \(0 \neq \Lambda \in (\ell ^\infty )^*\) vanishing on \(c_0\). It is not of the form \(\Lambda _y\) with \(y \in \ell ^1\), since then \(y_n = \Lambda (e_n) = 0\) for all \(n\). Such functionals are invisible on every finite part of a sequence, like the Banach limits of Exercise 30.12.16; they are produced by the Hahn–Banach theorem, that is, by Zorn’s lemma, and no explicit formula for one is known.

0336 Lemma 30.11.2

Let \(M\) be a closed subspace of a normed space \(X\) and \(x_0 \notin M\), with \(d = \operatorname {dist}(x_0, M)\). There is \(\Lambda \in X^*\) with \(\| \Lambda \| = 1\), \(\Lambda |_M = 0\) and \(\Lambda (x_0) = d\).

Proof â–¼

The quotient \(X/M\) with \(\| x + M\| = \operatorname {dist}(x, M)\) is a normed space (the proof of Lemma 30.2.4 that this is a norm did not use completeness), and the projection \(\pi \colon X \to X/M\) has norm at most \(1\). As \(\| x_0 + M\| = d {\gt} 0\), the Hahn–Banach theorem gives \(\lambda \in (X/M)^*\) with \(\| \lambda \| = 1\) and \(\lambda (x_0 + M) = d\). Then \(\Lambda = \lambda \circ \pi \) vanishes on \(M\), has \(\| \Lambda \| \leq 1\) and \(\Lambda (x_0) = d\); and for \(m \in M\), \(d = |\Lambda (x_0 - m)| \leq \| \Lambda \| \, \| x_0 - m\| \), so taking the infimum over \(m\), \(d \leq \| \Lambda \| d\) and \(\| \Lambda \| = 1\).

0337 Lemma 30.11.3

If the dual \(X^*\) of a normed space \(X\) is separable, then \(X\) is separable.

Proof â–¼

We may assume \(X \neq 0\). Let \((\Lambda _n)\) be dense in \(X^*\), and choose unit vectors \(x_n\) with \(|\Lambda _n(x_n)| \geq \| \Lambda _n\| /2\). The closed span \(M\) of the \(x_n\) is separable (finite combinations with coefficients in \(\mathbb {Q}\) or \(\mathbb {Q}+ i\mathbb {Q}\) are dense in it). If \(M \neq X\), Lemma 30.11.2 gives \(\Lambda \) with \(\| \Lambda \| = 1\) and \(\Lambda |_M = 0\). Choose \(n\) with \(\| \Lambda - \Lambda _n\| {\lt} \frac13\). Then \(\| \Lambda _n\| {\gt} \frac23\), so \(|\Lambda _n(x_n)| {\gt} \frac13\); but \(|\Lambda _n(x_n)| = |(\Lambda _n - \Lambda )(x_n)| {\lt} \frac13\). So \(M = X\).

The converse fails: \(\ell ^1\) is separable, but its dual \(\ell ^\infty \) (Proposition 30.11.1) is not (Remark 30.2.5).

0338 Definition 30.11.4

The bidual of a normed space \(X\) is \(X^{**} = (X^*)^*\), and the canonical map \(J \colon X \to X^{**}\) is \((Jx)(\Lambda ) = \Lambda (x)\). By Theorem 30.4.1, \(\| Jx\| = \sup _{\| \Lambda \| \leq 1}|\Lambda (x)| = \| x\| \), so \(J\) is a linear isometry. \(X\) is reflexive if \(J\) is surjective. A reflexive space is complete, being isometric to the Banach space \(X^{**}\).

0339 Proposition 30.11.5

  1. Finite-dimensional normed spaces and Hilbert spaces are reflexive.

  2. \(\ell ^p\) is reflexive for \(1 {\lt} p {\lt} \infty \).

  3. A Banach space \(X\) is reflexive if and only if \(X^*\) is reflexive.

  4. \(c_0\), \(\ell ^1\) and \(\ell ^\infty \) are not reflexive.

Proof â–¼

(1) If \(\dim X {\lt} \infty \), then \(\dim X^{**} = \dim X\) and the injective map \(J\) is bijective. Let \(H\) be a Hilbert space, and \(\Phi \colon H \to H^*\), \(\Phi (y) = \langle \cdot , y \rangle \), the conjugate-linear bijection of Theorem 30.3.4. Given \(\xi \in H^{**}\), the map \(y \mapsto \overline{\xi (\Phi (y))}\) is linear (as \(\Phi (ty) = \bar t\Phi (y)\)) and bounded, so it is \(\langle y, x \rangle \) for some \(x \in H\). Then \(\xi (\Phi (y)) = \overline{\langle y, x \rangle } = \langle x, y \rangle = \Phi (y)(x) = (Jx)(\Phi (y))\) for all \(y\), and as \(\Phi \) is onto, \(\xi = Jx\).

(2) Let \(\xi \in (\ell ^p)^{**}\). By Proposition 30.11.1(1), \(y \mapsto \xi (\Lambda _y)\) is a bounded linear functional on \(\ell ^q\), and since \(1 {\lt} q {\lt} \infty \) the same proposition gives \(x \in \ell ^p\) with \(\xi (\Lambda _y) = \sum _ny_nx_n = \Lambda _y(x) = (Jx)(\Lambda _y)\). As every element of \((\ell ^p)^*\) is some \(\Lambda _y\), \(\xi = Jx\).

(3) Write \(J_X\) and \(J_{X^*}\) for the canonical maps. Suppose \(X\) reflexive and \(\varphi \in X^{***}\). Then \(\Lambda = \varphi \circ J_X \in X^*\), and for \(\xi = J_Xx \in X^{**}\), \(\varphi (\xi ) = \Lambda (x) = \xi (\Lambda ) = (J_{X^*}\Lambda )(\xi )\); so \(\varphi = J_{X^*}\Lambda \), and \(X^*\) is reflexive. Conversely, suppose \(X^*\) reflexive and \(J_X(X) \neq X^{**}\). Since \(X\) is complete and \(J_X\) is an isometry, \(J_X(X)\) is closed, and Lemma 30.11.2 gives \(0 \neq \varphi \in X^{***}\) vanishing on \(J_X(X)\). Write \(\varphi = J_{X^*}\Lambda \); then \(0 = \varphi (J_Xx) = (J_Xx)(\Lambda ) = \Lambda (x)\) for all \(x\), so \(\Lambda = 0\) and \(\varphi = 0\), a contradiction.

(4) By Proposition 30.11.1, every \(\xi \in c_0^{**}\) is determined by the sequence \(z_n = \xi (\Lambda _{e_n})\), which is bounded, \(|z_n| \leq \| \xi \| \), and every bounded sequence \(z\) arises, from \(\xi (\Lambda _y) = \sum _ny_nz_n\). For \(x \in c_0\), \((Jx)(\Lambda _{e_n}) = x_n\). So the element \(\xi \) with \(z = (1, 1, \ldots )\) is not in \(J(c_0)\), and \(c_0\) is not reflexive. If \(\ell ^1\) were reflexive, then its bidual \((\ell ^1)^{**} \cong (\ell ^\infty )^*\) would be isometric to the separable space \(\ell ^1\), so \(\ell ^\infty \) would be separable by Lemma 30.11.3, which it is not. Finally \(\ell ^\infty \cong (\ell ^1)^*\), so by (3) it is not reflexive either.

033A Remark 30.11.6

  1. Duals of \(L^p\). For a \(\sigma \)-finite measure space and \(1 \leq p {\lt} \infty \), every bounded linear functional on \(L^p(\mu )\) is \(f \mapsto \int fg\, d\mu \) for a unique \(g \in L^q(\mu )\), and \((L^p)^* \cong L^q\) isometrically; hence \(L^p\) is reflexive for \(1 {\lt} p {\lt} \infty \). The spaces \(L^1([0, 1])\) and \(L^\infty ([0, 1])\) are not reflexive. See [Bre11, Chapter 4]; for \(p = 2\) this is the Riesz representation theorem.

  2. The dual of \(C(K)\). For a compact metric space \(K\), \(C(K)^*\) is the space of signed (or complex) finite Borel measures with the total variation norm; for positive functionals this is the Riesz–Markov theorem (Theorem 28.14.6, Remark 28.14.7). Weak-\(*\) convergence in \(C(K)^*\) is then weak convergence of measures, and Theorem 28.15.5 is a case of the Banach–Alaoglu theorem.

  3. Weak compactness. A Banach space is reflexive if and only if its closed unit ball is weakly compact (Kakutani), and then every bounded sequence has a weakly convergent subsequence (Eberlein–Šmulian). Every uniformly convex space, such as \(L^p\) with \(1 {\lt} p {\lt} \infty \), is reflexive (Milman–Pettis). See [Bre11, Chapter 3]. This is why the direct method (Proposition 30.10.6) works in \(L^p\) and in the Sobolev spaces built from it, and fails in \(L^1\): there the integrable functions \(n\mathbf{1}_{[0, 1/n]}\), of norm \(1\), concentrate at a point and have no weakly convergent subsequence in \(L^1\), their limit being the Dirac measure, which lies outside \(L^1\).
