30.11 Dual spaces and reflexivity0334

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

To understand a Banach space one often determines its dual. For sequence spaces this is elementary. Let \(c_0 \subseteq \ell ^\infty \) be the closed subspace of sequences tending to \(0\), and for \(1 \leq p \leq \infty \) let \(q\) be the conjugate exponent, \(1/p + 1/q = 1\) (with \(q = \infty \) for \(p = 1\) and \(q = 1\) for \(p = \infty \)). For a sequence \(y\), let \(\Lambda _y(x) = \sum _nx_ny_n\) whenever this converges absolutely.

0335 Proposition 30.11.1

  1. For \(1 \leq p {\lt} \infty \), the map \(y \mapsto \Lambda _y\) is an isometric isomorphism \(\ell ^q \to (\ell ^p)^*\).

  2. The map \(y \mapsto \Lambda _y\) is an isometric isomorphism \(\ell ^1 \to (c_0)^*\).

Proof â–¼

(1) By Hölder’s inequality for the counting measure (Proposition 28.5.2), \(|\Lambda _y(x)| \leq \| x\| _p\| y\| _q\), so \(\Lambda _y \in (\ell ^p)^*\) with \(\| \Lambda _y\| \leq \| y\| _q\), and \(y \mapsto \Lambda _y\) is linear and injective, as \(\Lambda _y(e_n) = y_n\) for the unit vectors \(e_n\). Let \(\Lambda \in (\ell ^p)^*\) and \(y_n = \Lambda (e_n)\); we show \(\| y\| _q \leq \| \Lambda \| \) and \(\Lambda = \Lambda _y\). If \(p = 1\), \(|y_n| = |\Lambda (e_n)| \leq \| \Lambda \| \), so \(\| y\| _\infty \leq \| \Lambda \| \). If \(1 {\lt} p {\lt} \infty \), so \(q {\lt} \infty \) and \((q - 1)p = q\), let \(x^{(N)}_n = |y_n|^{q-2}\bar y_n\) for \(n \leq N\) (and \(0\) if \(y_n = 0\) or \(n {\gt} N\)). Then

\[ \sum _{n \leq N}|y_n|^q = \Lambda (x^{(N)}) \leq \| \Lambda \| \, \| x^{(N)}\| _p = \| \Lambda \| \Bigl(\sum _{n \leq N}|y_n|^{(q-1)p}\Bigr)^{1/p} = \| \Lambda \| \Bigl(\sum _{n \leq N}|y_n|^q\Bigr)^{1/p}, \]

so \((\sum _{n \leq N}|y_n|^q)^{1/q} \leq \| \Lambda \| \) for all \(N\), and \(\| y\| _q \leq \| \Lambda \| \). In both cases \(\Lambda \) and \(\Lambda _y\) are continuous and agree on the finitely supported sequences, which are dense in \(\ell ^p\) for \(p {\lt} \infty \) (for \(x \in \ell ^p\) the tail \(\sum _{n {\gt} N}|x_n|^p\) tends to \(0\)); so \(\Lambda = \Lambda _y\), and \(\| \Lambda \| \leq \| y\| _q \leq \| \Lambda \| \).

(2) In the same way, \(|\Lambda _y(x)| \leq \| x\| _\infty \| y\| _1\) for \(x \in c_0\) and \(y \in \ell ^1\). Given \(\Lambda \in c_0^*\), put \(y_n = \Lambda (e_n)\) and \(x^{(N)}_n = \bar y_n/|y_n|\) for \(n \leq N\), \(y_n \neq 0\) (and \(0\) otherwise); then \(\| x^{(N)}\| _\infty \leq 1\) and \(\sum _{n \leq N}|y_n| = \Lambda (x^{(N)}) \leq \| \Lambda \| \). The finitely supported sequences are dense in \(c_0\), so again \(\Lambda = \Lambda _y\) and \(\| \Lambda \| = \| y\| _1\).

The dual of \(\ell ^\infty \) is strictly larger than \(\ell ^1\). The subspace \(c_0\) is closed and not dense in \(\ell ^\infty \), since \(\mathbf{1} = (1, 1, \ldots )\) has distance \(1\) from it, so by Corollary 30.10.4(4) there is \(0 \neq \Lambda \in (\ell ^\infty )^*\) vanishing on \(c_0\). It is not of the form \(\Lambda _y\) with \(y \in \ell ^1\), since then \(y_n = \Lambda (e_n) = 0\) for all \(n\). Such functionals are invisible on every finite part of a sequence, like the Banach limits of Exercise 30.12.16; they are produced by the Hahn–Banach theorem, that is, by Zorn’s lemma, and no explicit formula for one is known.

0336 Lemma 30.11.2

Let \(M\) be a closed subspace of a normed space \(X\) and \(x_0 \notin M\), with \(d = \operatorname {dist}(x_0, M)\). There is \(\Lambda \in X^*\) with \(\| \Lambda \| = 1\), \(\Lambda |_M = 0\) and \(\Lambda (x_0) = d\).

Proof â–¼

The quotient \(X/M\) with \(\| x + M\| = \operatorname {dist}(x, M)\) is a normed space (the proof of Lemma 30.2.4 that this is a norm did not use completeness), and the projection \(\pi \colon X \to X/M\) has norm at most \(1\). As \(\| x_0 + M\| = d {\gt} 0\), the Hahn–Banach theorem gives \(\lambda \in (X/M)^*\) with \(\| \lambda \| = 1\) and \(\lambda (x_0 + M) = d\). Then \(\Lambda = \lambda \circ \pi \) vanishes on \(M\), has \(\| \Lambda \| \leq 1\) and \(\Lambda (x_0) = d\); and for \(m \in M\), \(d = |\Lambda (x_0 - m)| \leq \| \Lambda \| \, \| x_0 - m\| \), so taking the infimum over \(m\), \(d \leq \| \Lambda \| d\) and \(\| \Lambda \| = 1\).

0337 Lemma 30.11.3

If the dual \(X^*\) of a normed space \(X\) is separable, then \(X\) is separable.

Proof â–¼

We may assume \(X \neq 0\). Let \((\Lambda _n)\) be dense in \(X^*\), and choose unit vectors \(x_n\) with \(|\Lambda _n(x_n)| \geq \| \Lambda _n\| /2\). The closed span \(M\) of the \(x_n\) is separable (finite combinations with coefficients in \(\mathbb {Q}\) or \(\mathbb {Q}+ i\mathbb {Q}\) are dense in it). If \(M \neq X\), Lemma 30.11.2 gives \(\Lambda \) with \(\| \Lambda \| = 1\) and \(\Lambda |_M = 0\). Choose \(n\) with \(\| \Lambda - \Lambda _n\| {\lt} \frac13\). Then \(\| \Lambda _n\| {\gt} \frac23\), so \(|\Lambda _n(x_n)| {\gt} \frac13\); but \(|\Lambda _n(x_n)| = |(\Lambda _n - \Lambda )(x_n)| {\lt} \frac13\). So \(M = X\).

The converse fails: \(\ell ^1\) is separable, but its dual \(\ell ^\infty \) (Proposition 30.11.1) is not (Remark 30.2.5).

0338 Definition 30.11.4

The bidual of a normed space \(X\) is \(X^{**} = (X^*)^*\), and the canonical map \(J \colon X \to X^{**}\) is \((Jx)(\Lambda ) = \Lambda (x)\). By Theorem 30.4.1, \(\| Jx\| = \sup _{\| \Lambda \| \leq 1}|\Lambda (x)| = \| x\| \), so \(J\) is a linear isometry. \(X\) is reflexive if \(J\) is surjective. A reflexive space is complete, being isometric to the Banach space \(X^{**}\).

0339 Proposition 30.11.5

  1. Finite-dimensional normed spaces and Hilbert spaces are reflexive.

  2. \(\ell ^p\) is reflexive for \(1 {\lt} p {\lt} \infty \).

  3. A Banach space \(X\) is reflexive if and only if \(X^*\) is reflexive.

  4. \(c_0\), \(\ell ^1\) and \(\ell ^\infty \) are not reflexive.

Proof â–¼

(1) If \(\dim X {\lt} \infty \), then \(\dim X^{**} = \dim X\) and the injective map \(J\) is bijective. Let \(H\) be a Hilbert space, and \(\Phi \colon H \to H^*\), \(\Phi (y) = \langle \cdot , y \rangle \), the conjugate-linear bijection of Theorem 30.3.4. Given \(\xi \in H^{**}\), the map \(y \mapsto \overline{\xi (\Phi (y))}\) is linear (as \(\Phi (ty) = \bar t\Phi (y)\)) and bounded, so it is \(\langle y, x \rangle \) for some \(x \in H\). Then \(\xi (\Phi (y)) = \overline{\langle y, x \rangle } = \langle x, y \rangle = \Phi (y)(x) = (Jx)(\Phi (y))\) for all \(y\), and as \(\Phi \) is onto, \(\xi = Jx\).

(2) Let \(\xi \in (\ell ^p)^{**}\). By Proposition 30.11.1(1), \(y \mapsto \xi (\Lambda _y)\) is a bounded linear functional on \(\ell ^q\), and since \(1 {\lt} q {\lt} \infty \) the same proposition gives \(x \in \ell ^p\) with \(\xi (\Lambda _y) = \sum _ny_nx_n = \Lambda _y(x) = (Jx)(\Lambda _y)\). As every element of \((\ell ^p)^*\) is some \(\Lambda _y\), \(\xi = Jx\).

(3) Write \(J_X\) and \(J_{X^*}\) for the canonical maps. Suppose \(X\) reflexive and \(\varphi \in X^{***}\). Then \(\Lambda = \varphi \circ J_X \in X^*\), and for \(\xi = J_Xx \in X^{**}\), \(\varphi (\xi ) = \Lambda (x) = \xi (\Lambda ) = (J_{X^*}\Lambda )(\xi )\); so \(\varphi = J_{X^*}\Lambda \), and \(X^*\) is reflexive. Conversely, suppose \(X^*\) reflexive and \(J_X(X) \neq X^{**}\). Since \(X\) is complete and \(J_X\) is an isometry, \(J_X(X)\) is closed, and Lemma 30.11.2 gives \(0 \neq \varphi \in X^{***}\) vanishing on \(J_X(X)\). Write \(\varphi = J_{X^*}\Lambda \); then \(0 = \varphi (J_Xx) = (J_Xx)(\Lambda ) = \Lambda (x)\) for all \(x\), so \(\Lambda = 0\) and \(\varphi = 0\), a contradiction.

(4) By Proposition 30.11.1, every \(\xi \in c_0^{**}\) is determined by the sequence \(z_n = \xi (\Lambda _{e_n})\), which is bounded, \(|z_n| \leq \| \xi \| \), and every bounded sequence \(z\) arises, from \(\xi (\Lambda _y) = \sum _ny_nz_n\). For \(x \in c_0\), \((Jx)(\Lambda _{e_n}) = x_n\). So the element \(\xi \) with \(z = (1, 1, \ldots )\) is not in \(J(c_0)\), and \(c_0\) is not reflexive. If \(\ell ^1\) were reflexive, then its bidual \((\ell ^1)^{**} \cong (\ell ^\infty )^*\) would be isometric to the separable space \(\ell ^1\), so \(\ell ^\infty \) would be separable by Lemma 30.11.3, which it is not. Finally \(\ell ^\infty \cong (\ell ^1)^*\), so by (3) it is not reflexive either.

30.11.1 Weak compactness: the theorems of Goldstine and Kakutani0380

The closed unit ball of an infinite-dimensional normed space is never norm compact. In a Hilbert space it is weakly sequentially compact (Theorem 30.9.2), and this is what makes minimisation arguments work (Proposition 30.10.6). Kakutani’s theorem says exactly which Banach spaces share this property: the reflexive ones. The bridge between \(X\) and its bidual is the following density theorem. Recall that \(X^{**}\) is the dual of the normed space \(X^*\), so it carries a weak-\(*\) topology, the initial topology for the maps \(\xi \mapsto \xi (\Lambda )\), \(\Lambda \in X^*\); its basic open neighbourhoods of \(\xi \) are the sets \(\{ \eta : |\eta (\Lambda _i) - \xi (\Lambda _i)| {\lt} \varepsilon , \ i = 1, \ldots , k\} \). We write \(B_X\) for the closed unit ball of a normed space \(X\). For \(\Lambda \in X^*\), \(\sup _{x \in B_X}\operatorname {Re}\Lambda (x) = \| \Lambda \| \), since \(x\) may be multiplied by a scalar of modulus \(1\) to make \(\Lambda (x)\) real and positive.

0381 Theorem 30.11.6 Goldstine

For every normed space \(X\), the image \(J(B_X)\) of the closed unit ball under the canonical map is weak-\(*\) dense in the closed unit ball \(B_{X^{**}}\) of the bidual. In particular \(J(X)\) is weak-\(*\) dense in \(X^{**}\).

Proof â–¼

Let \(\xi \in B_{X^{**}}\), \(\Lambda _1, \ldots , \Lambda _k \in X^*\) and \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\). We must find \(x \in B_X\) with \(|\Lambda _i(x) - \xi (\Lambda _i)| {\lt} \varepsilon \) for all \(i\). Consider the linear map \(T \colon X \to \mathbb {K}^k\), \(T(x) = (\Lambda _1(x), \ldots , \Lambda _k(x))\), the point \(y = (\xi (\Lambda _1), \ldots , \xi (\Lambda _k))\), and the closure \(C\) of \(T(B_X)\) in \(\mathbb {K}^k\), a closed convex set. It suffices to show \(y \in C\). If not, Theorem 30.10.3(2), applied in the finite-dimensional normed space \(\mathbb {K}^k\) to the compact set \(\{ y\} \) and the closed set \(C\), gives a linear functional, necessarily of the form \(z \mapsto \sum _ic_iz_i\), and numbers \(c_1^{\prime } {\lt} c_2^{\prime }\) with

\[ \operatorname {Re}\sum _ic_iy_i \leq c_1^{\prime } {\lt} c_2^{\prime } \leq \operatorname {Re}\sum _ic_iz_i \qquad (z \in C). \]

Put \(\Lambda = -\sum _ic_i\Lambda _i \in X^*\). Taking \(z = T(x)\) with \(x \in B_X\) gives \(\operatorname {Re}\Lambda (x) \leq -c_2^{\prime }\), hence \(\| \Lambda \| \leq -c_2^{\prime }\). On the other hand \(\operatorname {Re}\xi (\Lambda ) = -\operatorname {Re}\sum _ic_iy_i \geq -c_1^{\prime } {\gt} -c_2^{\prime } \geq \| \Lambda \| \), while \(|\xi (\Lambda )| \leq \| \xi \| \, \| \Lambda \| \leq \| \Lambda \| \). This contradiction shows \(y \in C\). The last statement follows by scaling.

For \(X = c_0\), whose bidual is \(\ell ^\infty \) by Proposition 30.11.1 (with \(J\) the inclusion), the theorem says that every bounded sequence is a weak-\(*\) limit, that is, a limit against every \(y \in \ell ^1\), of sequences in \(c_0\) of no larger norm; for \(\mathbf{1} = (1, 1, \ldots )\) one can take the truncations \((1, \ldots , 1, 0, 0, \ldots )\), since \(\sum _{n \leq N}y_n \to \sum _ny_n\). In norm, \(\mathbf{1}\) has distance \(1\) from \(c_0\).

0382 Theorem 30.11.7 Kakutani

A Banach space \(X\) is reflexive if and only if its closed unit ball \(B_X\) is weakly compact.

Proof â–¼

First, \(J\) is a homeomorphism from \(X\) with the weak topology onto \(J(X)\) with the subspace topology of the weak-\(*\) topology of \(X^{**}\). Indeed, the weak topology of \(X\) is the initial topology for the maps \(\Lambda \in X^*\), the weak-\(*\) topology of \(X^{**}\) is the initial topology for the maps \(\xi \mapsto \xi (\Lambda )\), and \((J x)(\Lambda ) = \Lambda (x)\); so \(J\) carries the subbasic open sets \(\Lambda ^{-1}(W)\) of the one to the traces on \(J(X)\) of the subbasic open sets of the other.

Suppose \(X\) is reflexive. Then \(J\) is a bijection \(X \to X^{**}\), an isometry, so it maps \(B_X\) onto \(B_{X^{**}}\). The ball \(B_{X^{**}}\) is weak-\(*\) compact by the Banach–Alaoglu theorem (Theorem 30.9.2, applied to the normed space \(X^*\)), so \(B_X = J^{-1}(B_{X^{**}})\) is weakly compact.

Conversely, suppose \(B_X\) is weakly compact. Then \(J(B_X)\) is weak-\(*\) compact (Proposition 18.6.3(3)). The weak-\(*\) topology is Hausdorff, since two different elements of \(X^{**}\) differ at some \(\Lambda \), so \(J(B_X)\) is weak-\(*\) closed (Proposition 18.6.3(2)). By Goldstine’s theorem it is also dense in \(B_{X^{**}}\), so \(J(B_X) = B_{X^{**}}\), and by scaling \(J(X) = X^{**}\).

0383 Corollary 30.11.8

Let \(X\) be a reflexive Banach space.

  1. Every bounded, closed, convex subset of \(X\) is weakly compact.

  2. Every \(\Lambda \in X^*\) attains its norm: there is \(x \in B_X\) with \(\Lambda (x) = \| \Lambda \| \).

  3. Let \(C \subseteq X\) be nonempty, closed and convex, and \(F \colon C \to \mathbb {R}\) continuous and convex with \(F(x) \to \infty \) as \(\| x\| \to \infty \) in \(C\) (no condition if \(C\) is bounded). Then \(F\) attains its minimum on \(C\). In particular, for every \(x_0 \in X\) there is a point of \(C\) nearest to \(x_0\).

Proof â–¼

(1) Let \(C\) be such a set, contained in the ball \(rB_X\). Multiplication by \(r\) is a weak homeomorphism, so \(rB_X\) is weakly compact by Theorem 30.11.7, and \(C\) is weakly closed by Mazur’s theorem (Corollary 30.10.4(1)), hence weakly compact (Proposition 18.6.3(1)).

(2) The function \(x \mapsto \operatorname {Re}\Lambda (x)\) is weakly continuous, so it attains a maximum on the weakly compact set \(B_X\) (Corollary 18.6.6) at some \(x_1\), and \(\operatorname {Re}\Lambda (x_1) = \| \Lambda \| \). Since \(|\Lambda (x_1)| \leq \| \Lambda \| \), \(\Lambda (x_1) = \| \Lambda \| \).

(3) Let \(m = \inf _CF \geq -\infty \) and fix \(x_1 \in C\); if \(F(x_1) = m\) we are done. For \(m {\lt} t \leq F(x_1)\) the set \(C_t = \{ x \in C : F(x) \leq t\} \) is nonempty, convex (as \(F\) is convex), closed (as \(F\) is continuous and \(C\) closed) and bounded (by the growth condition). By (1) the sets \(C_t\) are weakly compact, hence weakly closed subsets of the weakly compact set \(C_{F(x_1)}\), and they decrease as \(t\) decreases. Any finitely many of them have nonempty intersection, the smallest one, so by compactness \(\bigcap _tC_t \neq \emptyset \). A point \(x\) of the intersection has \(F(x) \leq t\) for all \(t {\gt} m\), so \(F(x) = m\), and in particular \(m {\gt} -\infty \). For the nearest point take \(F(x) = \| x - x_0\| \).

This removes the separability and Hilbert space assumptions from the direct method, Proposition 30.10.6; by Remark 30.11.12(1) it applies in \(L^p\) for \(1 {\lt} p {\lt} \infty \). The use of compactness through decreasing families of closed sets, instead of subsequences, is what lets the argument work although the weak topology is in general not metrisable.

0384 Counterexample 30.11.9

In the non-reflexive space \(c_0\) (real scalars) both (2) and (3) fail. Let \(y = (2^{-n})_{n \geq 1} \in \ell ^1\), so \(\Lambda _y(x) = \sum _n2^{-n}x_n\) has norm \(\| y\| _1 = 1\) (Proposition 30.11.1(2)). For \(x \in c_0\) with \(\| x\| _\infty \leq 1\), \(|x_n| {\lt} 1\) for large \(n\), so \(|\Lambda _y(x)| {\lt} \sum _n2^{-n} = 1\): the norm is not attained. Consequently the closed convex set \(C = \{ x \in c_0 : \Lambda _y(x) = 1\} \) has no point nearest to \(0\): every \(x \in C\) has \(\| x\| _\infty {\gt} 1\) (by the previous sentence applied to \(x/\| x\| _\infty \)), while \(x^{(N)} = (1, \ldots , 1, 0, \ldots )/(1 - 2^{-N})\), with \(N\) ones, lies in \(C\) and has norm \(1/(1 - 2^{-N}) \to 1\). By the corollary, this gives another proof that \(c_0\) is not reflexive.

Kakutani’s name is attached to a second, quite different theorem of functional analysis, a fixed point theorem for commuting affine maps, which produces invariant functionals. We prove it for weak-\(*\) compact sets of functionals, which is the case needed in practice. A map \(T \colon K \to K\) on a convex set is affine if \(T((1 - t)a + tb) = (1 - t)T(a) + tT(b)\) for \(a, b \in K\) and \(t \in [0, 1]\); it then preserves all finite convex combinations.

0385 Theorem 30.11.10 Markov–Kakutani

Let \(X\) be a normed space, \(K \subseteq X^*\) a nonempty convex weak-\(*\) compact set, and \(\mathcal{T}\) a family of affine weak-\(*\) continuous maps \(K \to K\) that commute pairwise. Then there is \(\Lambda \in K\) with \(T(\Lambda ) = \Lambda \) for all \(T \in \mathcal{T}\).

Proof â–¼

For \(T \in \mathcal{T}\) and \(n \geq 1\) let \(A_{T,n} = \frac1n(\mathrm{id} + T + \cdots + T^{n-1})\). It maps \(K\) into \(K\), as \(K\) is convex, and it is weak-\(*\) continuous, since for each \(x \in X\) the map \(\Lambda \mapsto (A_{T,n}\Lambda )(x) = \frac1n\sum _{j{\lt}n}(T^j\Lambda )(x)\) is continuous. Since the maps of \(\mathcal{T}\) are affine and commute, \(S \circ A_{T,n} = A_{T,n} \circ S\) for \(S \in \mathcal{T}\), and hence the maps \(A_{T,n}\) commute with each other. Let \(\mathcal{A}\) be the set of finite composites of maps \(A_{T,n}\). For \(A \in \mathcal{A}\), \(A(K)\) is weak-\(*\) compact, hence weak-\(*\) closed in the Hausdorff space \(X^*\). For \(A_1, \ldots , A_m \in \mathcal{A}\), the composite \(A_1 \circ \cdots \circ A_m\) can be written with any \(A_i\) on the left, so \(A_1 \cdots A_m(K) \subseteq A_i(K)\) for each \(i\): the closed subsets \(A(K)\) of the compact set \(K\) have the finite intersection property, and there is \(\Lambda \in \bigcap _{A \in \mathcal{A}}A(K)\).

Let \(T \in \mathcal{T}\) and \(n \geq 1\). Then \(\Lambda = A_{T,n}(M)\) for some \(M \in K\), and since \(T\) is affine,

\[ T(\Lambda ) - \Lambda = \frac1n\sum _{j=1}^{n}T^jM - \frac1n\sum _{j=0}^{n-1}T^jM = \frac1n(T^nM - M). \]

For \(x \in X\) the continuous function \(N \mapsto |N(x)|\) is bounded on the compact set \(K\), say by \(c_x\), so \(|(T\Lambda )(x) - \Lambda (x)| \leq 2c_x/n\). Letting \(n \to \infty \), \(T(\Lambda ) = \Lambda \).

0386 Example 30.11.11

Banach limits again. Let \(X = \ell ^\infty \) (real) and \(S\) the left shift, \((Sx)_n = x_{n+1}\). The set \(K = \{ L \in (\ell ^\infty )^* : \| L\| \leq 1, \ L(\mathbf{1}) = 1\} \) is convex, nonempty (it contains \(x \mapsto x_1\)) and weak-\(*\) closed in the weak-\(*\) compact unit ball (Theorem 30.9.2), hence weak-\(*\) compact. The map \(T(L) = L \circ S\) is linear, weak-\(*\) continuous (as \((TL)(x) = L(Sx)\) is evaluation at \(Sx\)) and maps \(K\) into \(K\), since \(\| S\| \leq 1\) and \(S\mathbf{1} = \mathbf{1}\). A fixed point \(L\) is shift-invariant. It is positive: for \(0 \leq x \leq \mathbf{1}\), \(\| \mathbf{1} - x\| _\infty \leq 1\) gives \(1 - L(x) = L(\mathbf{1} - x) \leq 1\). Hence \(L(x) = L(S^nx) \geq \inf _{k {\gt} n}x_k\) for all \(n\), so \(L(x) \geq \liminf _nx_n\), and likewise \(L(x) \leq \limsup _nx_n\): \(L\) is a Banach limit, as in Exercise 30.12.16. The same argument, with the translations of an abelian group \(G\) acting on the bounded functions on \(G\), produces a translation-invariant mean on every abelian group (the translations commute because the group is abelian); groups with such a mean are called amenable.

033A Remark 30.11.12

  1. Duals of \(L^p\). For a \(\sigma \)-finite measure space and \(1 \leq p {\lt} \infty \), every bounded linear functional on \(L^p(\mu )\) is \(f \mapsto \int fg\, d\mu \) for a unique \(g \in L^q(\mu )\), and \((L^p)^* \cong L^q\) isometrically; hence \(L^p\) is reflexive for \(1 {\lt} p {\lt} \infty \). The spaces \(L^1([0, 1])\) and \(L^\infty ([0, 1])\) are not reflexive. See [Bre11, Chapter 4]; for \(p = 2\) this is the Riesz representation theorem.

  2. The dual of \(C(K)\). For a compact metric space \(K\), \(C(K)^*\) is the space of signed (or complex) finite Borel measures with the total variation norm; for positive functionals this is the Riesz–Markov theorem (Theorem 28.14.6, Remark 28.14.7). Weak-\(*\) convergence in \(C(K)^*\) is then weak convergence of measures, and Theorem 28.15.5 is a case of the Banach–Alaoglu theorem.

  3. Weak compactness. A Banach space is reflexive if and only if its closed unit ball is weakly compact (Theorem 30.11.7), and then every bounded sequence has a weakly convergent subsequence (Eberlein–Šmulian). Every uniformly convex space, such as \(L^p\) with \(1 {\lt} p {\lt} \infty \), is reflexive (Milman–Pettis). See [Bre11, Chapter 3]. This is why the direct method (Proposition 30.10.6) works in \(L^p\) and in the Sobolev spaces built from it, and fails in \(L^1\): there the integrable functions \(n\mathbf{1}_{[0, 1/n]}\), of norm \(1\), concentrate at a point and have no weakly convergent subsequence in \(L^1\), their limit being the Dirac measure, which lies outside \(L^1\).
