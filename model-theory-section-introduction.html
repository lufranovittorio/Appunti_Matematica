5.1 Introduction02FG

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

Chapter 2 proved the compactness and Löwenheim–Skolem theorems for countable languages, as consequences of the completeness theorem, and without the axiom of choice. With the set theory of Chapter 3 (cardinals and choice) we can now say how large the models of a first-order theory can be. The downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem (Theorem 5.2.5) produces small elementary substructures; ultraproducts and Łoś’s theorem (Theorem 5.3.4) give the compactness theorem for languages of any size (Theorem 5.3.5); and together they give the upward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem (Theorem 5.4.1): a first-order theory with an infinite model has models of every infinite cardinality at least that of the language. So first-order logic cannot pin down an infinite structure up to isomorphism.

In the other direction, the Łoś–Vaught test (Theorem 5.4.3) shows that a theory with a unique model of some infinite cardinality is complete, and Cantor’s back-and-forth argument (Theorem 5.4.5) supplies an example: the dense linear orders. Back-and-forth arguments are formalised by Ehrenfeucht–Fraïssé theory (Section 5.5), which leads to Lindström’s theorem (Theorem 5.6.2): first-order logic is the strongest logic that has both the compactness and the Löwenheim–Skolem property. References: [CK90], [Mar02], [EFT94, Chapters XII and XIII].

Prerequisites. Chapter 2 (Mathematical Logic), Chapter 3 (Set Theory) and Chapter 4 (Number Systems).

On a first reading. No later chapter depends on this one, and it can be skipped entirely on the way to the Hodge conjecture.

02FH Notation 5.1.1

We work in ZFC. Languages, formulas and structures are as in Chapter 2, but a language \(\mathcal{L}\) may now have any cardinality; \(|\mathcal{L}|\) is the cardinality of its set of constant, function and relation symbols, and \(\| \mathcal{L}\| = \max (|\mathcal{L}|, \aleph _0)\). If the free variables of a formula \(\varphi \) are among the distinct variables \(x_1, \ldots , x_n\), we write \(\varphi (x_1, \ldots , x_n)\), and for elements \(a_1, \ldots , a_n\) of a structure \(\mathcal{A}\) we write \(\mathcal{A} \models \varphi [a_1, \ldots , a_n]\) if \(\mathcal{A} \models \varphi [s]\) for an assignment \(s\) with \(s(x_i) = a_i\); by the coincidence lemma (Lemma 2.4.4) this does not depend on \(s\). Tuples are written \(\bar{a} = (a_1, \ldots , a_n)\), and \(\mathcal{A}\) also denotes the universe of \(\mathcal{A}\). If \(\mathcal{L} \subseteq \mathcal{L}'\) and \(\mathcal{A}'\) is an \(\mathcal{L}'\)-structure, its reduct \(\mathcal{A}'|_{\mathcal{L}}\) forgets the symbols not in \(\mathcal{L}\), and \(\mathcal{A}'\) is an expansion of \(\mathcal{A}'|_{\mathcal{L}}\); by induction on formulas, an \(\mathcal{L}\)-formula has the same truth value in \(\mathcal{A}'\) and in \(\mathcal{A}'|_{\mathcal{L}}\).

02FI Lemma 5.1.2

A language \(\mathcal{L}\) has at most \(\| \mathcal{L}\| \) terms and at most \(\| \mathcal{L}\| \) formulas.

Proof â–¼

Let \(\kappa = \| \mathcal{L}\| \). Let \(T_0\) be the set of variables and constant symbols, and \(T_{k+1}\) the union of \(T_k\) with the set of terms \(f(t_1, \ldots , t_n)\) with \(t_i \in T_k\). By induction \(|T_k| \leq \kappa \): the new terms are indexed by the pairs of a function symbol and a finite tuple of elements of \(T_k\), and there are at most \(\kappa \cdot \kappa = \kappa \) of these, by Proposition 3.7.5 and Theorem 3.6.12. The union of the \(T_k\) contains the variables and constants and is closed under the function symbols, so it contains every term, and it has cardinality at most \(\kappa \) by Proposition 3.7.5. The same argument, starting from the atomic formulas and closing under \(\neg \), \(\to \) and \(\forall x\), bounds the formulas.
