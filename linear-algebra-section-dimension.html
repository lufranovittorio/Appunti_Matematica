11.2 Linear maps and dimension00IG

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

02T2 Definition 11.2.1

A vector space over a field \(k\) is a set \(V\) with an addition \(V \times V \to V\), \((v, w) \mapsto v + w\), making \(V\) an abelian group with neutral element \(0\), and a scalar multiplication \(k \times V \to V\), \((a, v) \mapsto av\), such that \(a(v + w) = av + aw\), \((a + b)v = av + bv\), \((ab)v = a(bv)\) and \(1v = v\); that is, a module over the ring \(k\) (Chapter 8). A linear map is a homomorphism of modules: \(f(v + w) = f(v) + f(w)\) and \(f(av) = af(v)\). A subspace is a subset containing \(0\) and closed under addition and scalar multiplication.

From the axioms, \(0v = 0\) (as \(0v = (0 + 0)v = 0v + 0v\)), \(a0 = 0\), and \((-1)v = -v\). Bases and dimension are those of free modules (Section 8.8): every vector space has a basis, and all bases have the same cardinality, the dimension.

02T3 Example 11.2.2

  1. \(k^n\), with the standard basis \(e_1, \ldots , e_n\); the matrices \(M_{m \times n}(k)\), of dimension \(mn\).

  2. Polynomials. The polynomial ring \(k[x]\) (Definition 8.6.1) is a vector space under addition of polynomials and multiplication by constants, and the monomials \(1, x, x^2, \ldots \) form a basis. This is Lemma 8.6.2 in the language of linear algebra. A polynomial is the sequence of its coefficients, \(x^i\) is the sequence with \(1\) in position \(i\) and \(0\) elsewhere, and a constant \(c\) multiplies a sequence termwise, \(c(a_0, a_1, \ldots ) = (ca_0, ca_1, \ldots )\); hence \(\sum _{i=0}^na_ix^i = (a_0, \ldots , a_n, 0, 0, \ldots )\). The monomials span: a polynomial has only finitely many nonzero coefficients \(a_0, \ldots , a_n\), and it equals the finite combination \(\sum _{i=0}^na_ix^i\). They are linearly independent: if a finite combination \(\sum _{i=0}^na_ix^i\) is the zero polynomial, then the sequence \((a_0, \ldots , a_n, 0, \ldots )\) is zero, so every \(a_i = 0\). In other words, \(k[x]\) is the space \(k^{(\mathbb {N})}\) of finitely supported sequences of the next item, and the monomials are its standard basis vectors \(e_n\). Its dimension is infinite and countable, the basis being indexed by \(\mathbb {N}\).

    The polynomials of degree at most \(d\), together with \(0\), form a subspace \(k[x]_{\leq d}\), as the coefficients of \(f + g\) and \(cf\) in degrees above \(d\) vanish when those of \(f\) and \(g\) do. The same argument shows that \(1, x, \ldots , x^d\) is a basis of it, so \(\dim k[x]_{\leq d} = d + 1\). Here is a second basis. For distinct \(a_0, \ldots , a_d \in k\), the Lagrange polynomials

    \[ L_i = \prod _{j \neq i}\frac{x - a_j}{a_i - a_j}, \qquad i = 0, \ldots , d, \]

    have degree \(d\) and satisfy \(L_i(a_l) = 1\) if \(l = i\) and \(L_i(a_l) = 0\) if \(l \neq i\): for \(l \neq i\) the factor with \(j = l\) vanishes at \(a_l\), and at \(a_i\) every factor equals \(1\). They are independent: evaluating \(\sum _ic_iL_i = 0\) at \(a_l\) gives \(c_l = 0\). They span: for \(p \in k[x]_{\leq d}\), the polynomial \(q = p - \sum _ip(a_i)L_i\) lies in \(k[x]_{\leq d}\) and vanishes at the \(d + 1\) points \(a_0, \ldots , a_d\), so \(q = 0\), as a nonzero polynomial of degree at most \(d\) has at most \(d\) roots (Corollary 8.6.7). So \(p = \sum _ip(a_i)L_i\): the coordinates of \(p\) in this basis are its values \(p(a_i)\). Equivalently (Lagrange interpolation), for any \(b_0, \ldots , b_d \in k\) there is exactly one polynomial of degree at most \(d\) with \(p(a_i) = b_i\) for all \(i\), namely \(\sum _ib_iL_i\). For instance, through the points \((0, 1)\), \((1, 3)\), \((2, 7)\) passes exactly one polynomial of degree at most \(2\), namely \(\frac12(x - 1)(x - 2) - 3x(x - 2) + \frac72x(x - 1) = x^2 + x + 1\).

  3. Function spaces. For a set \(S\), the functions \(S \to k\) form a vector space \(k^S\) under pointwise operations; for \(S\) finite it is \(k^{|S|}\), and for \(S\) infinite it has infinite dimension, since the indicator functions of points are independent. The continuous functions \(C([0, 1])\) form an infinite-dimensional real vector space: the monomials \(x^n\) are linearly independent, since a nonzero polynomial has finitely many roots. So do the sequences \(k^{\mathbb {N}}\), which contain the subspace \(k^{(\mathbb {N})}\) of finitely supported sequences, with basis the \(e_n\); a basis of \(k^{\mathbb {N}}\) itself exists by Zorn’s lemma but cannot be written down.

  4. \(\mathbb {R}\) is a vector space over \(\mathbb {Q}\) of uncountable dimension, since a countable-dimensional \(\mathbb {Q}\)-vector space is countable; \(\mathbb {C}\) is a real vector space of dimension \(2\), with basis \(1, i\).

  5. The solutions of a homogeneous linear system \(Ax = 0\), or of a homogeneous linear differential equation \(y^{\prime \prime } + y = 0\) (basis \(\cos \), \(\sin \)), form vector spaces; the solutions of \(Ax = b\) with \(b \neq 0\) do not, as they do not contain \(0\).

Subspaces behave well under linear maps. The following facts are used constantly, usually without comment.

03G7 Lemma 11.2.3

Let \(f \colon V \to W\) be a linear map.

  1. If \(U \subseteq V\) is a subspace, its image \(f(U) = \{ f(u) : u \in U\} \) is a subspace of \(W\). In particular the image \(\operatorname {im}f = f(V)\) is a subspace.

  2. If \(U^{\prime } \subseteq W\) is a subspace, its preimage \(f^{-1}(U^{\prime }) = \{ v \in V : f(v) \in U^{\prime }\} \) is a subspace of \(V\) containing \(\ker f = f^{-1}(0)\).

  3. \(f\) maps the span of a set \(S \subseteq V\) onto the span of \(f(S)\). Hence \(\dim f(U) \leq \dim U\); if \(f\) is injective, it maps linearly independent sets to linearly independent sets and \(\dim f(U) = \dim U\).

  4. Intersections of subspaces are subspaces, and \(U_1 + U_2 = \{ u_1 + u_2 : u_i \in U_i\} \) is the smallest subspace containing the subspaces \(U_1\) and \(U_2\). A union \(U_1 \cup U_2\) is a subspace only if \(U_1 \subseteq U_2\) or \(U_2 \subseteq U_1\).

Proof â–¼

(1) \(0 = f(0) \in f(U)\); and \(f(u_1) + f(u_2) = f(u_1 + u_2)\) and \(af(u) = f(au)\) lie in \(f(U)\), since \(u_1 + u_2, au \in U\). (2) \(f(0) = 0 \in U^{\prime }\); if \(f(v_1), f(v_2) \in U^{\prime }\), then \(f(v_1 + v_2) = f(v_1) + f(v_2) \in U^{\prime }\) and \(f(av_1) = af(v_1) \in U^{\prime }\). Since \(0 \in U^{\prime }\), \(\ker f \subseteq f^{-1}(U^{\prime })\). (3) \(f(\sum a_is_i) = \sum a_if(s_i)\), so \(f\) maps the combinations of elements of \(S\) exactly onto the combinations of elements of \(f(S)\). A basis \(B\) of \(U\) spans \(U\), so \(f(B)\) spans \(f(U)\), and \(f(U)\) has a basis contained in \(f(B)\) (Theorem 8.8.4), of cardinality at most \(|B|\). If \(f\) is injective and \(\sum a_if(s_i) = 0\) for distinct \(s_i \in S\), then \(f(\sum a_is_i) = 0\), so \(\sum a_is_i = 0\); if \(S\) is independent, all \(a_i = 0\). Then \(f(B)\) is a basis of \(f(U)\), of the same cardinality as \(B\). (4) The conditions defining a subspace are preserved by intersections. \(U_1 + U_2\) is the image of the linear map \(U_1 \times U_2 \to V\), \((u_1, u_2) \mapsto u_1 + u_2\), so a subspace by (1), and every subspace containing \(U_1\) and \(U_2\) contains all sums \(u_1 + u_2\). If neither \(U_i\) contains the other, pick \(u_1 \in U_1 \setminus U_2\) and \(u_2 \in U_2 \setminus U_1\). Then \(u_1 + u_2 \notin U_1\), since otherwise \(u_2 = (u_1 + u_2) - u_1 \in U_1\), and similarly \(u_1 + u_2 \notin U_2\); so \(U_1 \cup U_2\) is not closed under addition.

03G8 Example 11.2.4

Let \(f \colon \mathbb {R}^3 \to \mathbb {R}^2\), \(f(x, y, z) = (x + z, y)\). The plane \(U = \{ z = 0\} \) is mapped onto all of \(\mathbb {R}^2\), and the line \(L = \mathbb {R}(1, 0, -1)\) to \(0\): it is \(\ker f\). The preimage of the line \(\mathbb {R}(1, 0)\) is \(\{ y = 0\} \), a plane containing \(\ker f\). Images of intersections can be smaller than intersections of images: for \(U_1 = \mathbb {R}(1, 0, 0)\) and \(U_2 = \mathbb {R}(0, 0, 1)\), \(U_1 \cap U_2 = 0\), but \(f(U_1) = f(U_2) = \mathbb {R}(1, 0)\). The coordinate axes of \(\mathbb {R}^2\) are subspaces whose union is not: \((1, 0) + (0, 1) = (1, 1)\) lies on neither axis.

00IH Proposition 11.2.5

Let \(V\) be a vector space and \(W \subseteq V\) a subspace.

  1. \(W\) has a complement: a subspace \(U\) with \(V = W \oplus U\) (that is, \(W + U = V\) and \(W \cap U = 0\)). Then \(U \cong V/W\) and \(\dim V = \dim W + \dim V/W\).

  2. \(\dim W \leq \dim V\), and if \(\dim V\) is finite and \(\dim W = \dim V\) then \(W = V\).

  3. If \(U, W \subseteq V\) are finite-dimensional, \(\dim (U + W) + \dim (U \cap W) = \dim U + \dim W\).

Proof â–¼

(1) Extend a basis \(B_W\) of \(W\) to a basis \(B\) of \(V\) (Theorem 8.8.4) and let \(U\) be spanned by \(B \setminus B_W\). The projection \(V \to V/W\) restricts to an isomorphism \(U \to V/W\), and \(B = B_W \sqcup (B \setminus B_W)\) gives the formula (as cardinals). (2) The first claim follows from (1). If \(\dim W = \dim V {\lt} \infty \), a basis of \(W\) has \(\dim V\) elements and extends to a basis of \(V\), which by Theorem 8.8.5 has no further elements. (3) Extend a basis of \(U \cap W\) to bases of \(U\) and of \(W\); the union of these bases is a basis of \(U + W\).

00II Theorem 11.2.6 Rank–nullity

For a linear map \(f \colon V \to W\), \(V/\ker f \cong \operatorname {im}f\), so \(\dim V = \dim \ker f + \dim \operatorname {im}f\). If \(\dim V = \dim W {\lt} \infty \), then \(f\) is injective if and only if it is surjective.

Proof â–¼

The isomorphism is the first isomorphism theorem (Proposition 8.7.3); combine with Proposition 11.2.5(1). If \(\dim V = \dim W = n\), then \(f\) is injective iff \(\dim \operatorname {im}f = n\) iff \(\operatorname {im}f = W\) by Proposition 11.2.5(2).

00IJ Counterexample 11.2.7

The last statement fails in infinite dimension. On \(V = k^{(\mathbb {N})}\) the shift \(e_n \mapsto e_{n+1}\) is injective but not surjective, and \(e_0 \mapsto 0\), \(e_{n+1} \mapsto e_n\) is surjective but not injective.
