10.5 Sums of squares02YI

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

Which integers are sums of two squares? The first few are

\[ 0, 1, 2, 4, 5, 8, 9, 10, 13, 16, 17, 18, 20, 25, 26, 29, \ldots , \]

and among the odd primes, \(5, 13, 17, 29, 37, 41\) appear, while \(3, 7, 11, 19, 23, 31\) do not. By Example 10.2.2(2), no number \(\equiv 3 \pmod4\) is a sum of two squares. Fermat stated in 1640 that every prime \(p \equiv 1 \pmod4\) is one; Euler gave the first proof. We give a proof through the Gaussian integers, in which a sum of two squares factors: \(a^2 + b^2 = (a + bi)(a - bi)\).

Recall from Example 8.5.5 that \(\mathbb {Z}[i] = \{ a + bi : a, b \in \mathbb {Z}\} \) is a Euclidean domain for the norm \(N(a + bi) = a^2 + b^2 = |a + bi|^2\), hence a PID and a UFD (Theorem 8.5.4). The norm is multiplicative, \(N(zw) = |zw|^2 = N(z)N(w)\). Its units are the elements of norm \(1\), namely \(\pm 1\) and \(\pm i\): if \(zw = 1\) then \(N(z)N(w) = 1\) in \(\mathbb {N}\), so \(N(z) = 1\); conversely \(N(z) = z\bar{z} = 1\) gives \(z^{-1} = \bar{z} \in \mathbb {Z}[i]\).

02YJ Theorem 10.5.1 Fermat

An odd prime \(p\) is a sum of two squares if and only if \(p \equiv 1 \pmod4\). In that case the representation \(p = a^2 + b^2\) is unique up to order and signs of \(a\) and \(b\).

Proof â–¼

If \(p = a^2 + b^2\) then \(p \not\equiv 3 \pmod4\) by Example 10.2.2(2), so \(p \equiv 1 \pmod4\). Conversely let \(p \equiv 1 \pmod4\). By Corollary 10.4.3 there is \(x \in \mathbb {Z}\) with \(p \mid x^2 + 1 = (x + i)(x - i)\). But \(p\) divides neither factor in \(\mathbb {Z}[i]\), since \((x \pm i)/p = x/p \pm i/p\) does not have integer coefficients. So \(p\) is not a prime element of \(\mathbb {Z}[i]\), and since \(\mathbb {Z}[i]\) is a UFD, \(p\) is not irreducible (Lemma 8.5.3): \(p = zw\) with \(z\), \(w\) not units. Then \(p^2 = N(p) = N(z)N(w)\) with \(N(z), N(w) {\gt} 1\), so \(N(z) = p\), and writing \(z = a + bi\), \(p = a^2 + b^2\).

For uniqueness, let \(p = a^2 + b^2 = c^2 + d^2\). Then \(z = a + bi\) is irreducible: if \(z = uv\), then \(p = N(u)N(v)\), so one of \(N(u)\), \(N(v)\) is \(1\). In a UFD irreducible elements are prime, and \(z\) divides \(p = (c + di)(c - di)\), so \(z\) divides \(c + di\) or \(c - di\); as both have norm \(p = N(z)\), the quotient has norm \(1\) and is a unit. So \(c + di\) or \(c - di\) is \(\pm (a + bi)\) or \(\pm i(a + bi) = \mp (b - ai)\), and \((c, d)\) is \((a, b)\) or \((b, a)\) up to order and signs.

02YK Example 10.5.2

\(5 = 1^2 + 2^2\), \(13 = 2^2 + 3^2\), \(17 = 1^2 + 4^2\), \(29 = 2^2 + 5^2\), \(37 = 1^2 + 6^2\), \(41 = 4^2 + 5^2\), and \(1013 = 22^2 + 23^2\). The proof can be made into an algorithm: find \(x\) with \(x^2 \equiv -1 \pmod p\), and compute a greatest common divisor of \(p\) and \(x + i\) in \(\mathbb {Z}[i]\) with the Euclidean algorithm. For \(p = 13\), \(x = 5\): dividing \(13\) by \(5 + i\) gives \(13/(5 + i) = 13(5 - i)/26 = (5 - i)/2\), rounded to \(q = 2\) (or \(3 - i\)), with remainder \(13 - 2(5 + i) = 3 - 2i\); and \((5 + i)/(3 - 2i) = (5 + i)(3 + 2i)/13 = (13 + 13i)/13 = 1 + i\) exactly. So the gcd is \(3 - 2i\), and \(13 = 3^2 + 2^2\).

Products of sums of two squares are sums of two squares, by the multiplicativity of the norm: writing out \(N(zw) = N(z)N(w)\) for \(z = a + bi\), \(w = c + di\) gives the identity of Brahmagupta and Fibonacci,

\begin{equation} (a^2 + b^2)(c^2 + d^2) = (ac - bd)^2 + (ad + bc)^2 . \end{equation}

02YM Theorem 10.5.3

An integer \(n \geq 1\) is a sum of two squares if and only if every prime \(q \equiv 3 \pmod4\) occurs in the prime factorisation of \(n\) with an even exponent.

Proof â–¼

Suppose the condition holds. Then \(n\) is a product of factors \(2 = 1^2 + 1^2\), primes \(p \equiv 1 \pmod4\), which are sums of two squares by Theorem 10.5.1, and squares \(q^2 = q^2 + 0^2\); by 10.4 and induction on the number of factors, so is \(n\).

Conversely let \(n = a^2 + b^2\) and let \(q \equiv 3 \pmod4\) be a prime dividing \(n\). We claim that \(q \mid a\) and \(q \mid b\). If \(q \nmid b\), let \(b^{\prime }\) be an inverse of \(b\) modulo \(q\); then \(a^2 \equiv -b^2\) gives \((ab^{\prime })^2 \equiv -1 \pmod q\), contradicting Corollary 10.4.3. So \(q \mid b\), hence \(q \mid a^2\) and \(q \mid a\). Then \(q^2 \mid n\) and \(n/q^2 = (a/q)^2 + (b/q)^2\) is again a sum of two squares. By induction on \(n\), the exponent of \(q\) in \(n/q^2\) is even, hence so is its exponent in \(n\).

02YN Example 10.5.4

\(2026 = 2 \cdot 1013\) with \(1013 \equiv 1 \pmod4\) prime, so \(2026\) is a sum of two squares; by 10.4 with \(2 = 1^2 + 1^2\) and \(1013 = 22^2 + 23^2\), \(2026 = (22 - 23)^2 + (23 + 22)^2 = 1^2 + 45^2\). The number \(65 = 5 \cdot 13\) has the two essentially different representations \(1^2 + 8^2\) and \(4^2 + 7^2\), coming from \((1 + 2i)(2 + 3i) = -4 + 7i\) and \((1 + 2i)(2 - 3i) = 8 + i\). On the other hand \(21 = 3 \cdot 7\) and \(2027 \equiv 3 \pmod4\) are not sums of two squares, while \(9 \cdot 7^2 = 441 = 21^2 + 0^2\) is.

Not every positive integer is a sum of three squares: no number \(\equiv 7 \pmod8\) is, since squares are \(\equiv 0, 1, 4 \pmod8\) and no three of these add up to \(7\). (Legendre and Gauss proved that the numbers that are not sums of three squares are exactly those of the form \(4^a(8b + 7)\); we do not prove this, see [Ser73].) Four squares always suffice. The proof follows the same pattern as for two squares, with an identity of Euler in place of 10.4, and Fermat’s method of descent in place of the Gaussian integers.

02YO Lemma 10.5.5 Euler’s four-square identity

For all \(a_1, \ldots , a_4, b_1, \ldots , b_4\) in a commutative ring,

\[ \Bigl(\sum _{i=1}^{4}a_i^2\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum _{i=1}^{4}b_i^2\Bigr) = z_1^2 + z_2^2 + z_3^2 + z_4^2, \]

where

\[ \begin{aligned} z_1 & = a_1b_1 + a_2b_2 + a_3b_3 + a_4b_4, & z_2 & = a_1b_2 - a_2b_1 + a_3b_4 - a_4b_3, \\ z_3 & = a_1b_3 - a_3b_1 + a_4b_2 - a_2b_4, & z_4 & = a_1b_4 - a_4b_1 + a_2b_3 - a_3b_2 . \end{aligned} \]
Proof â–¼

Expand. Each \(z_k^2\) contains four squares \(a_i^2b_j^2\), and the sixteen squares that occur are all the \(a_i^2b_j^2\), each once: \(z_1\) contains the products \(a_ib_i\), and \(z_2\), \(z_3\), \(z_4\) contain the products \(a_ib_j\) for the pairs \(\{ i, j\} \) equal to \(\{ 1, 2\} \) and \(\{ 3, 4\} \), to \(\{ 1, 3\} \) and \(\{ 2, 4\} \), and to \(\{ 1, 4\} \) and \(\{ 2, 3\} \) respectively. The cross terms cancel in pairs; for instance the terms containing \(a_1a_2\) are \(2a_1a_2b_1b_2\) from \(z_1^2\), \(-2a_1a_2b_2b_1\) from \(z_2^2\), \(-2a_1a_2b_3b_4\) from \(z_3^2\) and \(2a_1a_2b_4b_3\) from \(z_4^2\), with sum \(0\); the other five pairs \(a_ia_j\) are handled in the same way. (The identity expresses the multiplicativity of the norm of quaternions, \(|xy|^2 = |x|^2|y|^2\).)

02YP Theorem 10.5.6 Lagrange

Every positive integer is a sum of four squares of integers.

Proof â–¼

By Lemma 10.5.5 and the factorisation into primes, and since \(1 = 1^2 + 0^2 + 0^2 + 0^2\) and \(2 = 1^2 + 1^2 + 0^2 + 0^2\), it suffices to treat an odd prime \(p\).

Step 1: some multiple \(mp\) with \(1 \leq m {\lt} p\) is a sum of four squares. The \(\frac{p+1}{2}\) numbers \(x^2\), \(0 \leq x \leq \frac{p-1}{2}\), are pairwise incongruent modulo \(p\): if \(x^2 \equiv x^{\prime 2}\), then \(p \mid (x - x^{\prime })(x + x^{\prime })\), and \(0 \leq x + x^{\prime } {\lt} p\), \(|x - x^{\prime }| {\lt} p\), so \(x = x^{\prime }\). Likewise the \(\frac{p+1}{2}\) numbers \(-1 - y^2\), \(0 \leq y \leq \frac{p-1}{2}\), are pairwise incongruent. Since \(\frac{p+1}{2} + \frac{p+1}{2} {\gt} p\), some \(x^2\) is congruent to some \(-1 - y^2\), so \(x^2 + y^2 + 1^2 + 0^2 = mp\) for some \(m \geq 1\), and \(mp \leq 2\bigl(\frac{p-1}{2}\bigr)^2 + 1 {\lt} p^2\), so \(m {\lt} p\).

Step 2: descent. Let \(m\) be the least positive integer such that \(mp\) is a sum of four squares; by Step 1, \(m {\lt} p\). Suppose \(m {\gt} 1\), and write \(mp = x_1^2 + x_2^2 + x_3^2 + x_4^2\).

If \(m\) is even, then \(\sum x_i^2\) is even, so an even number of the \(x_i\) are odd, and we may number them so that \(x_1 \equiv x_2\) and \(x_3 \equiv x_4 \pmod2\). Then

\[ \frac{m}{2}p = \Bigl(\frac{x_1 + x_2}{2}\Bigr)^2 + \Bigl(\frac{x_1 - x_2}{2}\Bigr)^2 + \Bigl(\frac{x_3 + x_4}{2}\Bigr)^2 + \Bigl(\frac{x_3 - x_4}{2}\Bigr)^2, \]

a sum of four squares of integers, contradicting the minimality of \(m\).

If \(m\) is odd, \(m \geq 3\), choose \(y_i \equiv x_i \pmod m\) with \(|y_i| {\lt} m/2\) (possible since \(m\) is odd). Then \(\sum y_i^2 \equiv \sum x_i^2 = mp \equiv 0 \pmod m\), so \(\sum y_i^2 = mr\) with \(0 \leq r\), and \(mr {\lt} 4(m/2)^2 = m^2\), so \(r {\lt} m\). If \(r = 0\), all \(y_i = 0\), so \(m\) divides every \(x_i\), \(m^2 \mid mp\) and \(m \mid p\), impossible for \(1 {\lt} m {\lt} p\). So \(1 \leq r {\lt} m\). By Lemma 10.5.5,

\[ m^2rp = \Bigl(\sum x_i^2\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum y_i^2\Bigr) = z_1^2 + z_2^2 + z_3^2 + z_4^2, \]

with the \(z_k\) formed from \(a_i = x_i\), \(b_i = y_i\). Since \(y_i \equiv x_i \pmod m\), \(z_1 \equiv \sum x_i^2 \equiv 0\), and \(z_2 \equiv x_1x_2 - x_2x_1 + x_3x_4 - x_4x_3 = 0 \pmod m\), and likewise \(z_3 \equiv z_4 \equiv 0\). So \(rp = \sum (z_k/m)^2\) is a sum of four squares of integers with \(1 \leq r {\lt} m\), again contradicting the minimality of \(m\). Hence \(m = 1\).

02YQ Example 10.5.7

\(7 = 2^2 + 1^2 + 1^2 + 1^2\) needs four squares, as \(7 \equiv 7 \pmod8\). Also \(2026 = 45^2 + 1^2 + 0^2 + 0^2\), \(15 = 3^2 + 2^2 + 1^2 + 1^2\) and \(2027 = 45^2 + 1^2 + 1^2 + 0^2\).
