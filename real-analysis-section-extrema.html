27.13 Extrema in several variables, Lagrange multipliers and the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions036X

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

In one variable, Fermat’s lemma and the second derivative test (Lemma 27.6.4, Corollary 27.6.14) locate the extrema of a function. This section does the same in several variables, first for functions on open sets, then for functions restricted to a set defined by equations (Lagrange multipliers), and finally to a set defined by equations and inequalities (the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions), which is the basic situation of optimisation theory. The tools are the chain rule, Taylor’s formula and the implicit function theorem of the previous sections, together with one piece of convex geometry, Farkas’ lemma.

27.13.1 Gradient and Hessian036Y

Let \(U \subseteq \mathbb {R}^n\) be open and \(f \colon U \to \mathbb {R}\) differentiable at \(a\). The gradient of \(f\) at \(a\) is the vector \(\nabla f(a) = (\partial _1f(a), \ldots , \partial _nf(a)) \in \mathbb {R}^n\); by Proposition 27.10.3(1) it represents the derivative through the standard inner product,

\[ Df(a)h = \langle \nabla f(a), h\rangle \qquad (h \in \mathbb {R}^n). \]

If \(\nabla f(a) \neq 0\), the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality shows that among unit vectors \(h\) the directional derivative \(\langle \nabla f(a), h\rangle \) is largest for \(h = \nabla f(a)/\| \nabla f(a)\| \): the gradient points in the direction of steepest increase, and its length is the rate of increase in that direction. It is orthogonal to the level set through \(a\), in the sense that \(\langle \nabla f(a), \gamma '(0)\rangle = (f \circ \gamma )'(0) = 0\) for every differentiable curve \(\gamma \) with \(\gamma (0) = a\) on which \(f\) is constant.

If \(f\) is \(C^2\), its Hessian at \(a\) is the matrix \(D^2f(a) = (\partial _i\partial _jf(a))_{i,j}\), which is symmetric by Schwarz’s theorem (Theorem 27.10.5). We write \(D^2f(a)(h, h) = h^TD^2f(a)h = \sum _{i,j}\partial _i\partial _jf(a)h_ih_j\) for the associated quadratic form. With this notation Taylor’s theorem (Theorem 27.10.7) for \(k = 1\) reads

\begin{equation} f(a + h) = f(a) + \langle \nabla f(a), h\rangle + \tfrac 12D^2f(\xi )(h, h) \qquad \text{for some } \xi \in (a, a + h), \end{equation}
27.2

since \(\sum _{|\alpha | = 2}\frac{\partial ^\alpha f}{\alpha !}h^\alpha = \frac12\sum _{i,j}\partial _i\partial _jfh_ih_j\) (a diagonal term \(\alpha = 2e_i\) has \(\alpha ! = 2\) and occurs once, an off-diagonal pair \(\{ i, j\} \) has \(\alpha ! = 1\) and occurs twice in the double sum). Along a line, if \(g(t) = f(a + th)\), the chain rule gives

\begin{equation} g'(t) = \langle \nabla f(a + th), h\rangle , \qquad g''(t) = D^2f(a + th)(h, h). \end{equation}
27.3

A real symmetric matrix \(H\), or its quadratic form, is positive definite if \(h^THh {\gt} 0\) for all \(h \neq 0\), positive semidefinite if \(h^THh \geq 0\) for all \(h\), negative (semi)definite if \(-H\) is positive (semi)definite, and indefinite if \(h^THh\) takes both signs. By the spectral theorem (Theorem 11.10.6), \(H = QDQ^T\) with \(Q\) orthogonal, and \(h^THh = \sum _i\lambda _iy_i^2\) with \(y = Q^Th\): so \(H\) is positive definite if and only if all its eigenvalues are positive, positive semidefinite if and only if they are all \(\geq 0\), and indefinite if and only if it has eigenvalues of both signs.

0371 Definition 27.13.1

Let \(S \subseteq \mathbb {R}^n\) and \(f \colon S \to \mathbb {R}\). Then \(f\) has a local minimum at \(a \in S\) if there is \(\delta {\gt} 0\) with \(f(x) \geq f(a)\) for all \(x \in S\) with \(\| x - a\| {\lt} \delta \), and a strict local minimum if moreover \(f(x) {\gt} f(a)\) for these \(x \neq a\); similarly for (strict) local maxima. A global minimum on \(S\) is a point \(a\) with \(f(x) \geq f(a)\) for all \(x \in S\). When \(S\) is given by equations or inequalities and \(f\) is the restriction of a function defined on a larger open set, one speaks of a constrained extremum. A point \(a\) of an open set \(U\) where \(f\) is differentiable is a critical point of \(f\) if \(\nabla f(a) = 0\).

0372 Proposition 27.13.2

Let \(U \subseteq \mathbb {R}^n\) be open, \(f \colon U \to \mathbb {R}\) of class \(C^2\), and \(a \in U\).

  1. If \(f\) has a local minimum at \(a\), then \(\nabla f(a) = 0\) and \(D^2f(a)\) is positive semidefinite.

  2. If \(\nabla f(a) = 0\) and \(D^2f(a)\) is positive definite, then \(f\) has a strict local minimum at \(a\).

  3. If \(\nabla f(a) = 0\) and \(D^2f(a)\) is indefinite, then \(f\) has no local extremum at \(a\) (a saddle point).

The same holds for maxima with “negative” in place of “positive”.

Proof â–¼

(1) Let \(h \in \mathbb {R}^n\) and \(g(t) = f(a + th)\), defined and \(C^2\) on an interval around \(0\), with a local minimum at \(0\). By Lemma 27.6.4 and 27.3, \(0 = g'(0) = \langle \nabla f(a), h\rangle \); as \(h\) is arbitrary, \(\nabla f(a) = 0\). If \(g''(0) = D^2f(a)(h, h)\) were negative, \(g\) would have a strict local maximum at \(0\) by Corollary 27.6.14, so \(g(t) {\lt} g(0) \leq g(t)\) for small \(t \neq 0\), which is absurd. Hence \(D^2f(a)(h, h) \geq 0\).

(2) The unit sphere \(\{ \| u\| = 1\} \) is compact (Theorem 18.6.5) and \(u \mapsto D^2f(a)(u, u)\) is continuous and positive on it, so it has a minimum \(m {\gt} 0\) (Corollary 18.6.6). For \(\| u\| = 1\), \(|D^2f(\xi )(u, u) - D^2f(a)(u, u)| \leq \sum _{i,j}|\partial _i\partial _jf(\xi ) - \partial _i\partial _jf(a)|\), which is \({\lt} m/2\) for \(\| \xi - a\| {\lt} \delta \) with \(\delta \) small, by continuity of the second derivatives. Then for \(0 {\lt} \| h\| {\lt} \delta \) (and \(\overline{B}(a, \delta ) \subseteq U\)), 27.2 with \(\nabla f(a) = 0\) gives

\[ f(a + h) - f(a) = \tfrac 12\| h\| ^2D^2f(\xi )\bigl(\tfrac {h}{\| h\| }, \tfrac {h}{\| h\| }\bigr) \geq \tfrac {m}{4}\| h\| ^2 {\gt} 0. \]

(3) By (1), applied to \(f\) and to \(-f\), a local minimum forces \(D^2f(a)\) positive semidefinite and a local maximum forces it negative semidefinite; an indefinite form is neither.

0373 Example 27.13.3

For \(n = 2\) write \(D^2f(a) = \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} A & B \\ B & C \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\). Completing the square, \(Ah_1^2 + 2Bh_1h_2 + Ch_2^2 = A(h_1 + \frac BAh_2)^2 + \frac{AC - B^2}{A}h_2^2\) when \(A \neq 0\). So the Hessian is positive definite if and only if \(A {\gt} 0\) and \(AC - B^2 {\gt} 0\), negative definite if and only if \(A {\lt} 0\) and \(AC - B^2 {\gt} 0\), and indefinite if \(AC - B^2 {\lt} 0\) (the determinant is the product of the two eigenvalues). For instance:

  1. \(f(x, y) = x^2 + xy + y^2 - 3x\) has the single critical point \((2, -1)\), with \(A = C = 2\), \(B = 1\), \(AC - B^2 = 3\): a strict local minimum, in fact global, as \(f\) minus its value there is a positive definite quadratic form in \((x - 2, y + 1)\).

  2. \(f(x, y) = x^3 - 3x + y^2\) has critical points \((\pm 1, 0)\), with \(D^2f = \operatorname {diag}(6x, 2)\): a strict local minimum at \((1, 0)\) and a saddle point at \((-1, 0)\). The local minimum is not global, since \(f(x, 0) \to -\infty \) as \(x \to -\infty \).

  3. \(x^2 + y^4\), \(x^2 - y^4\) and \(x^2 + y^3\) all have the critical point \(0\) with the semidefinite Hessian \(\operatorname {diag}(2, 0)\), and they have a minimum, a saddle and a saddle there: when the Hessian is degenerate the test says nothing, as in Counterexample 27.6.15.

0374 Counterexample 27.13.4

Checking all lines through \(a\) is not enough. Let \(f(x, y) = (y - x^2)(y - 3x^2)\). On every line through \(0\) the restriction of \(f\) has a strict local minimum at \(0\): on the \(x\)-axis \(f = 3x^4\), and on \(y = mx\) we get \(f = m^2x^2 - 4mx^3 + 3x^4\), whose second derivative at \(0\) is \(2m^2 {\gt} 0\) for \(m \neq 0\) (and on the \(y\)-axis \(f = y^2\)). But \(f {\lt} 0\) between the two parabolas, at \((x, 2x^2)\), arbitrarily close to \(0\); so \(0\) is not a local minimum. Here \(D^2f(0) = \operatorname {diag}(0, 2)\) is only semidefinite, as Proposition 27.13.2(2) requires.

27.13.2 Lagrange multipliers0375

Suppose we want the extrema of \(f(x, y)\) on the curve \(M = \{ h(x, y) = 0\} \). Walking along \(M\), the value of \(f\) stops increasing at a point \(a\) where \(M\) does not cross the level curve of \(f\) through \(a\) but touches it. There the two curves have the same tangent line, so their normals \(\nabla f(a)\) and \(\nabla h(a)\) are parallel: \(\nabla f(a) = \lambda \nabla h(a)\) for some number \(\lambda \), the Lagrange multiplier. In this way a constrained problem in \(n\) unknowns becomes an unconstrained system of \(n + 1\) equations (\(\nabla f = \lambda \nabla h\) and \(h = 0\)) in \(n + 1\) unknowns. To prove this we need curves inside the constraint set with a prescribed velocity; they are provided by the submersion theorem.

0376 Lemma 27.13.5

Let \(U \subseteq \mathbb {R}^n\) be open, \(h = (h_1, \ldots , h_q) \colon U \to \mathbb {R}^q\) of class \(C^k\) with \(k \geq 1\), and \(a \in U\) with \(h(a) = 0\) and \(Dh(a)\) surjective, that is, with \(\nabla h_1(a), \ldots , \nabla h_q(a)\) linearly independent. For every \(d \in \ker Dh(a)\) there are \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\) and a \(C^k\) curve \(x \colon (-\varepsilon , \varepsilon ) \to U\) with \(x(0) = a\), \(x'(0) = d\) and \(h(x(t)) = 0\) for all \(t\).

Proof â–¼

By Theorem 27.12.4(1) there is a \(C^k\)-diffeomorphism \(\psi \) from an open \(V \ni a\) onto an open \(W \subseteq \mathbb {R}^n\) with \(h \circ \psi ^{-1} = \pi \), where \(\pi (z) = (z_1, \ldots , z_q)\). (If \(q = 0\) there is nothing to prove: take \(x(t) = a + td\).) Thus \(h = \pi \circ \psi \) on \(V\), so \(\pi (\psi (a)) = h(a) = 0\) and, by the chain rule, \(\pi (D\psi (a)d) =Dh(a)d = 0\). Put \(v = D\psi (a)d\) and \(x(t) = \psi ^{-1}(\psi (a) + tv)\), defined for \(|t| {\lt} \varepsilon \) since \(W\) is open. Then \(h(x(t)) = \pi (\psi (a)) + t\pi (v) = 0\), and by the chain rule and Theorem 27.12.2, \(x'(0) = D\psi (a)^{-1}v = d\).

0377 Theorem 27.13.6 Lagrange multipliers

Let \(U \subseteq \mathbb {R}^n\) be open, \(f \colon U \to \mathbb {R}\) and \(h = (h_1, \ldots , h_q) \colon U \to \mathbb {R}^q\) of class \(C^1\), and \(M = \{ x \in U : h(x) = 0\} \). Let \(a \in M\) be a point where \(\nabla h_1(a), \ldots , \nabla h_q(a)\) are linearly independent. If \(f|_M\) has a local extremum at \(a\), there are unique numbers \(\lambda _1, \ldots , \lambda _q\) with

\begin{equation} \nabla f(a) = \lambda _1\nabla h_1(a) + \cdots + \lambda _q\nabla h_q(a). \end{equation}
27.4

Equivalently, \((a, \lambda )\) is a critical point of the Lagrangian \(L(x, \lambda ) = f(x) - \sum _j\lambda _jh_j(x)\) on \(U \times \mathbb {R}^q\).

Proof â–¼

Let \(d \in \ker Dh(a)\), and let \(x(t)\) be the curve of Lemma 27.13.5. Since \(x(t) \in M\) and \(x(t) \to a\), the function \(f \circ x\) has a local extremum at \(t = 0\), so by Lemma 27.6.4 and the chain rule \(0 = (f \circ x)'(0) = \langle \nabla f(a), d\rangle \). Thus \(\nabla f(a)\) is orthogonal to \(\ker Dh(a)\). Now \(\ker Dh(a)\) is exactly the orthogonal complement of the span \(R\) of the rows \(\nabla h_j(a)\) of the Jacobian matrix, so \(\nabla f(a) \in (R^\perp )^\perp = R\) (in a finite-dimensional inner product space \((R^\perp )^\perp = R\), since \(R \subseteq (R^\perp )^\perp \) and both have dimension \(\dim R\)). Uniqueness of the \(\lambda _j\) is the linear independence of the \(\nabla h_j(a)\). Finally, \(\partial L/\partial x_i = 0\) for all \(i\) is 27.4, and \(\partial L/\partial \lambda _j = -h_j = 0\) is the constraint.

The space \(\ker Dh(a)\) is the tangent space of \(M\) at \(a\): by the lemma it is the set of velocities of curves in \(M\) through \(a\), and by the submersion theorem \(M\) is, near \(a\), a submanifold of dimension \(n - q\) in the sense of Chapter 33. The theorem says that at a constrained extremum the gradient of \(f\) is normal to \(M\). The linear independence hypothesis cannot be dropped:

0379 Counterexample 27.13.7

Minimise \(f(x, y) = x\) on the cusp \(M = \{ h = 0\} \), \(h(x, y) = y^2 - x^3\). On \(M\), \(x^3 = y^2 \geq 0\), so \(x \geq 0\) and the minimum is at \((0, 0)\). But \(\nabla f = (1, 0)\), while \(\nabla h = (-3x^2, 2y)\) vanishes at the origin, so no \(\lambda \) satisfies \(\nabla f(0) = \lambda \nabla h(0)\). Solving the Lagrange system \(1 = -3\lambda x^2\), \(0 = 2\lambda y\), \(y^2 = x^3\) gives no solution at all: the method finds nothing, and the minimum sits at the singular point of \(M\). In practice one must examine separately the points of \(M\) where the gradients of the constraints are dependent.

037A Example 27.13.8

  1. Extrema of \(x + y\) on the circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\). The circle is compact, so both extrema exist (Corollary 18.6.6), and \(\nabla h = (2x, 2y) \neq 0\) on it. The system \((1, 1) = \lambda (2x, 2y)\) gives \(x = y = \frac1{2\lambda }\), and \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\) gives \((x, y) = \pm (\frac1{\sqrt2}, \frac1{\sqrt2})\): maximum \(\sqrt2\), minimum \(-\sqrt2\).

  2. The inequality of the means. Maximise \(f(x) = x_1x_2\cdots x_k\) on the compact set \(\Delta = \{ x : x_i \geq 0, \sum _ix_i = k\} \). The maximum is positive (take all \(x_i = 1\)), so it is attained at a point with all \(x_i {\gt} 0\), which is a local maximum of \(f\) on \(M = \{ x \in U : \sum _ix_i = k\} \) with \(U = \{ x_i {\gt} 0\} \). With \(h = \sum _ix_i - k\), \(\nabla h = (1, \ldots , 1)\) and \(\partial _if = f(x)/x_i\), so \(f(x)/x_i = \lambda \) for all \(i\): all \(x_i\) are equal, hence equal to \(1\), and \(f \leq 1\) on \(\Delta \). By homogeneity, \(\sqrt[k]{x_1\cdots x_k} \leq \frac1k\sum _ix_i\) for \(x_i \geq 0\), which recovers Corollary 27.7.7 by a quite different route.

  3. Eigenvectors as critical points. Let \(A\) be a real symmetric \(n \times n\) matrix and \(q(x) = x^TAx\), so that \(\nabla q(x) = 2Ax\). On the compact unit sphere, \(h(x) = \| x\| ^2 - 1\) with \(\nabla h(x) = 2x \neq 0\), the function \(q\) has a maximum at some \(u\), and 27.4 reads \(Au = \lambda u\): \(u\) is an eigenvector, and \(\lambda = u^TAu\) is the maximum of \(q\) on the sphere, the largest eigenvalue. This proves, without the fundamental theorem of algebra, that a real symmetric matrix has a real eigenvector, which is the key step of Theorem 11.10.6(2). More generally the critical points of the Rayleigh quotient \(x^TAx/x^Tx\) on \(\mathbb {R}^n \setminus \{ 0\} \) are exactly the eigenvectors.

  4. Nearest points. Let \(h \colon \mathbb {R}^n \to \mathbb {R}\) be \(C^1\), \(M = \{ h = 0\} \) nonempty with \(\nabla h \neq 0\) on \(M\), and \(p \in \mathbb {R}^n\). Since \(M\) is closed, \(f(x) = \| x - p\| ^2\) attains a minimum on \(M\) (on the compact set \(M \cap \overline{B}(p, R)\) for \(R\) large). At the minimum \(a\), \(2(a - p) = \lambda \nabla h(a)\): the segment from \(p\) to the nearest point is normal to \(M\). For the hyperplane \(\langle n, x\rangle = c\) this gives \(a = p + tn\) with \(t = (c - \langle n, p\rangle )/\| n\| ^2\), and the familiar distance \(|c - \langle n, p\rangle |/\| n\| \).

The multiplier has a meaning of its own: it is the rate at which the optimal value changes when the constraint is relaxed.

037B Proposition 27.13.9

Let \(f, h \colon U \to \mathbb {R}\) be \(C^1\) on an open \(U \subseteq \mathbb {R}^n\), \(J\) an open interval, and \(c \mapsto x(c)\) a differentiable map \(J \to U\) such that for every \(c \in J\), \(h(x(c)) = c\) and \(\nabla f(x(c)) = \lambda (c)\nabla h(x(c))\) for some number \(\lambda (c)\). Then \(V(c) = f(x(c))\) is differentiable on \(J\) and \(V'(c) = \lambda (c)\).

Proof â–¼

By the chain rule, \(V'(c) = \langle \nabla f(x(c)), x'(c)\rangle = \lambda (c)\langle \nabla h(x(c)), x'(c)\rangle = \lambda (c)\frac{d}{dc}h(x(c)) = \lambda (c)\).

For example, the largest product \(xy\) with \(x + y = c\) is \(V(c) = c^2/4\), at \(x = y = c/2\), where \((y, x) = \frac c2(1, 1)\): indeed \(\lambda = c/2 = V'(c)\). In economics, where \(f\) is a profit and \(h \leq c\) a budget, \(\lambda \) is the shadow price of the resource: the extra profit that one more unit of it would bring. In mechanics, where Lagrange introduced the method in his Mécanique analytique (1788), \(\lambda \nabla h\) is the force by which the constraint keeps the system on \(M\).

27.13.3 Second-order conditions037C

A critical point of the Lagrangian need not be an extremum, and as in Proposition 27.13.2 one looks at second derivatives. The correct quadratic form is not the Hessian of \(f\) but that of the Lagrangian, and only in tangent directions: the curvature of \(M\) contributes through the multipliers.

037D Theorem 27.13.10

Let \(f\) and \(h = (h_1, \ldots , h_q)\) be \(C^2\) on the open set \(U \subseteq \mathbb {R}^n\), \(M = \{ h = 0\} \), and \(a \in M\) with \(\nabla f(a) = \sum _j\lambda _j\nabla h_j(a)\). Put \(L = f - \sum _j\lambda _jh_j\) (with these fixed \(\lambda _j\)) and \(T = \ker Dh(a)\).

  1. If \(\nabla h_1(a), \ldots , \nabla h_q(a)\) are linearly independent and \(f|_M\) has a local minimum at \(a\), then \(D^2L(a)(d, d) \geq 0\) for all \(d \in T\).

  2. If \(D^2L(a)(d, d) {\gt} 0\) for all nonzero \(d \in T\), then \(f|_M\) has a strict local minimum at \(a\).

Proof â–¼

Note that \(\nabla L(a) = 0\) and that \(L = f\) on \(M\).

(1) Let \(d \in T\) and let \(x(t)\) be the \(C^2\) curve of Lemma 27.13.5, and \(\varphi (t) = f(x(t)) = L(x(t))\). Differentiating \(\varphi ^{\prime }(t) = \sum _i\partial _iL(x(t))x_i^{\prime }(t)\) once more,

\[ \varphi ^{\prime \prime }(0) = \sum _{i,j}\partial _j\partial _iL(a)x_i^{\prime }(0)x_j^{\prime }(0) + \sum _i\partial _iL(a)x_i^{\prime \prime }(0) = D^2L(a)(d, d), \]

because \(\nabla L(a) = 0\); this is where the Lagrangian, rather than \(f\), is needed, since \(x''(0)\) is unknown. Now \(\varphi \) has a local minimum at \(0\), and as in the proof of Proposition 27.13.2(1), \(\varphi ''(0) \geq 0\).

(2) Suppose not. Then there are \(x_k \in M\) with \(x_k \neq a\), \(x_k \to a\) and \(f(x_k) \leq f(a)\). Write \(x_k = a + t_kd_k\) with \(t_k = \| x_k - a\| \to 0\) and \(\| d_k\| = 1\); passing to a subsequence (the unit sphere is compact), \(d_k \to d\) with \(\| d\| = 1\). From \(h(x_k) = h(a) = 0\) we get \(0 = (h(a + t_kd_k) - h(a))/t_k \to Dh(a)d\), so \(d \in T\). By 27.2 for \(L\), with \(\nabla L(a) = 0\),

\[ 0 \geq f(x_k) - f(a) = L(x_k) - L(a) = \tfrac 12t_k^2D^2L(\xi _k)(d_k, d_k) \]

with \(\xi _k \to a\). Dividing by \(t_k^2/2\) and letting \(k \to \infty \), \(D^2L(a)(d, d) \leq 0\), contradicting the hypothesis.

037E Example 27.13.11

Let \(M\) be the parabola \(y = x^2\), \(h(x, y) = y - x^2\), and \(f(x, y) = y + cx^2\) with a constant \(c\). At \(a = 0\), \(\nabla f = (0, 1) = 1 \cdot \nabla h(0)\), so \(\lambda = 1\) and \(T = \mathbb {R}(1, 0)\). Then \(D^2L = D^2f - D^2h = \operatorname {diag}(2c + 2, 0)\), so \(D^2L(0)(e_1, e_1) = 2(1 + c)\): by the theorem, \(0\) is a strict local minimum of \(f|_M\) for \(c {\gt} -1\) and a strict local maximum for \(c {\lt} -1\). This is correct, since \(f = (1 + c)x^2\) on \(M\). The Hessian of \(f\) alone gives \(D^2f(0)(e_1, e_1) = 2c\), which has the wrong sign for \(-1 {\lt} c {\lt} 0\): the term \(-\lambda D^2h\) records the bending of \(M\).

037F Example 27.13.12 The bordered Hessian in two variables

Let \(n = 2\), \(q = 1\), \(\nabla h(a) = (h_x, h_y) \neq 0\) and \(D^2L(a) = \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} L_{xx} & L_{xy} \\ L_{xy} & L_{yy} \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\). Then \(T\) is spanned by \(d = (-h_y, h_x)\) and

\[ D^2L(a)(d, d) = L_{xx}h_y^2 - 2L_{xy}h_xh_y + L_{yy}h_x^2 = -\det \begin{pmatrix} 0 & h_x & h_y \\ h_x & L_{xx} & L_{xy} \\ h_y & L_{xy} & L_{yy} \end{pmatrix}, \]

as one sees by expanding the determinant along the first row. So if the bordered Hessian on the right has negative determinant, \(a\) is a strict local minimum of \(f|_M\), and if it has positive determinant, a strict local maximum. In Example 27.13.8(1), at \(\pm (\frac1{\sqrt2}, \frac1{\sqrt2})\) we have \(\lambda = \pm \frac1{\sqrt2}\), \(D^2L = -2\lambda I\) and \((h_x, h_y) = \pm (\sqrt2, \sqrt2)\); the determinant is \(-(-2\lambda )(h_x^2 + h_y^2) = 8\lambda \), positive at the maximum and negative at the minimum.

27.13.4 Inequality constraints and Farkas’ lemma037G

Many problems constrain the unknowns by inequalities: quantities are nonnegative, budgets are bounded. Consider

\begin{equation} \text{minimise } f(x) \quad \text{subject to} \quad g_i(x) \leq 0 \ (1 \leq i \leq p), \qquad h_j(x) = 0 \ (1 \leq j \leq q), \end{equation}
27.5

where \(f, g_i, h_j \colon U \to \mathbb {R}\) are \(C^1\) on an open \(U \subseteq \mathbb {R}^n\); the feasible set is the set \(F\) of \(x \in U\) satisfying the constraints. At a feasible point \(a\), the inequality \(g_i \leq 0\) is active if \(g_i(a) = 0\); let \(I(a)\) be the set of active indices. An inactive constraint, \(g_i(a) {\lt} 0\), still holds near \(a\) and plays no role locally; an active one behaves like a wall.

To see what to expect, take one inequality \(g \leq 0\) and no equations. If the minimum \(a\) is interior (\(g(a) {\lt} 0\)), then \(\nabla f(a) = 0\). If \(g(a) = 0\), the minimum lies on the wall \(\{ g = 0\} \), so \(\nabla f(a) = \lambda \nabla g(a)\) by Theorem 27.13.6; and now the sign of \(\lambda \) is determined: moving into the feasible region, in the direction \(-\nabla g(a)\), must not decrease \(f\), so \(\nabla f(a)\) points into the feasible region and \(-\nabla f(a) = \mu \nabla g(a)\) with \(\mu \geq 0\). The general statement needs a lemma of linear algebra over the ordered field \(\mathbb {R}\).

037I Lemma 27.13.13 Farkas

Let \(v_1, \ldots , v_r, b \in \mathbb {R}^n\). Exactly one of the following holds:

  1. \(b = c_1v_1 + \cdots + c_rv_r\) with all \(c_i \geq 0\);

  2. there is \(w \in \mathbb {R}^n\) with \(\langle w, v_i\rangle \leq 0\) for all \(i\) and \(\langle w, b\rangle {\gt} 0\).

Geometrically: either \(b\) lies in the convex cone \(C = \{ \sum _ic_iv_i : c_i \geq 0\} \), or a hyperplane through \(0\), with normal \(w\), separates \(b\) from \(C\).

Proof â–¼

Both cannot hold, since then \(\langle w, b\rangle = \sum _ic_i\langle w, v_i\rangle \leq 0\). Assume (1) fails, that is, \(b \notin C\).

Step 1: \(C\) is closed. First, every \(y \in C\) is a nonnegative combination of a linearly independent subset of the \(v_i\) (Carathéodory’s lemma). Indeed, choose \(y = \sum _{i \in S}c_iv_i\) with all \(c_i {\gt} 0\) and \(S\) as small as possible. If the \(v_i\) with \(i \in S\) were dependent, there would be a relation \(\sum _{i \in S}\gamma _iv_i = 0\) with some \(\gamma _i {\gt} 0\) (change the sign of \(\gamma \) if necessary); with \(t = \min \{ c_i/\gamma _i : \gamma _i {\gt} 0\} \), \(y = \sum _{i \in S}(c_i - t\gamma _i)v_i\) has nonnegative coefficients, one of which vanishes, contradicting the minimality of \(S\). So \(C\) is the union of the finitely many cones \(C_S\) spanned by linearly independent subsets \(S\), and it suffices that each \(C_S\) is closed. The map \(\mathbb {R}^S \to \mathbb {R}^n\), \(c \mapsto \sum _{i \in S}c_iv_i\), is injective and linear, so on the compact unit sphere of \(\mathbb {R}^S\) its norm has a positive minimum \(m\), and \(\| \sum _ic_iv_i\| \geq m\| c\| \). If \(\sum _ic_i^{(k)}v_i \to y\) with \(c^{(k)} \geq 0\), then \((c^{(k)})\) is a Cauchy sequence, with a limit \(c \geq 0\), and \(y = \sum _ic_iv_i \in C_S\).

Step 2: a nearest point. The continuous function \(x \mapsto \| b - x\| \) attains a minimum on the compact set \(C \cap \overline{B}(b, \| b\| )\), which contains \(0\); the minimum point \(p\) is then a nearest point to \(b\) in all of \(C\).

Step 3: separation. Let \(w = b - p\), nonzero since \(b \notin C\). For \(c \in C\) and \(0 {\lt} t \leq 1\), \((1 - t)p + tc \in C\), so \(\| w - t(c - p)\| ^2 \geq \| w\| ^2\), that is, \(-2t\langle w, c - p\rangle + t^2\| c - p\| ^2 \geq 0\); dividing by \(t\) and letting \(t \to 0\), \(\langle w, c - p\rangle \leq 0\). With \(c = 0\) and \(c = 2p\) this gives \(\langle w, p\rangle = 0\), hence \(\langle w, c\rangle \leq 0\) for all \(c \in C\), in particular \(\langle w, v_i\rangle \leq 0\). Finally \(\langle w, b\rangle = \langle w, w + p\rangle = \| w\| ^2 {\gt} 0\).

037J Theorem 27.13.14 Karush–Kuhn–Tucker

In the situation 27.5, let \(a \in F\) be a local minimum of \(f\) on \(F\), and assume the linear independence constraint qualification: the gradients \(\nabla g_i(a)\) for \(i \in I(a)\) and \(\nabla h_j(a)\) for \(1 \leq j \leq q\) are linearly independent. Then there are unique numbers \(\mu _1, \ldots , \mu _p\) and \(\lambda _1, \ldots , \lambda _q\) with

\begin{equation} \nabla f(a) + \sum _{i=1}^p\mu _i\nabla g_i(a) + \sum _{j=1}^q\lambda _j\nabla h_j(a) = 0, \qquad \mu _i \geq 0, \qquad \mu _ig_i(a) = 0 \quad (1 \leq i \leq p). \end{equation}
27.6

The last condition, complementary slackness, says that \(\mu _i = 0\) unless the \(i\)-th constraint is active. The signs of the \(\lambda _j\) are not restricted, and for \(p = 0\) the theorem is Theorem 27.13.6 for minima (with \(-\lambda _j\) in place of \(\lambda _j\)).

Proof â–¼

Write \(I = I(a)\).

Step 1. Let \(d \in \mathbb {R}^n\) with \(\langle \nabla g_i(a), d\rangle {\lt} 0\) for \(i \in I\) and \(Dh(a)d = 0\). We show \(\langle \nabla f(a), d\rangle \geq 0\). The \(\nabla h_j(a)\) are linearly independent, so Lemma 27.13.5 gives a \(C^1\) curve \(x(t)\) with \(x(0) = a\), \(x'(0) = d\) and \(h(x(t)) = 0\). For \(i \in I\), \(\varphi _i(t) = g_i(x(t))\) has \(\varphi _i(0) = 0\) and \(\varphi _i'(0) = \langle \nabla g_i(a), d\rangle {\lt} 0\), so \(\varphi _i(t) {\lt} 0\) for small \(t {\gt} 0\); for \(i \notin I\), \(g_i(x(t)) {\lt} 0\) for small \(t\) by continuity. So \(x(t) \in F\) for small \(t {\gt} 0\), and since \(x(t) \to a\), \(f(x(t)) \geq f(a)\) for small \(t {\gt} 0\). Hence \(\langle \nabla f(a), d\rangle = \lim _{t \to 0^+}(f(x(t)) - f(a))/t \geq 0\).

Step 2. The same holds if only \(\langle \nabla g_i(a), d\rangle \leq 0\) for \(i \in I\) and \(Dh(a)d = 0\). Indeed, the linear map \(d \mapsto ((\langle \nabla g_i(a), d\rangle )_{i \in I}, Dh(a)d)\) has linearly independent rows, hence is surjective, so there is \(d_0\) with \(\langle \nabla g_i(a), d_0\rangle = -1\) for \(i \in I\) and \(Dh(a)d_0 = 0\). For \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\), \(d + \varepsilon d_0\) satisfies the hypothesis of Step 1, so \(\langle \nabla f(a), d + \varepsilon d_0\rangle \geq 0\); let \(\varepsilon \to 0\).

Step 3. Apply Lemma 27.13.13 to \(b = -\nabla f(a)\) and the vectors \(\nabla g_i(a)\) (\(i \in I\)), \(\nabla h_j(a)\) and \(-\nabla h_j(a)\) (\(1 \leq j \leq q\)). Alternative (2) would give \(w\) with \(\langle \nabla g_i(a), w\rangle \leq 0\) for \(i \in I\), \(\langle \pm \nabla h_j(a), w\rangle \leq 0\), that is, \(Dh(a)w = 0\), and \(\langle \nabla f(a), w\rangle {\lt} 0\), contradicting Step 2. So alternative (1) holds: \(-\nabla f(a) = \sum _{i \in I}\mu _i\nabla g_i(a) + \sum _j(c_j^+ - c_j^-)\nabla h_j(a)\) with \(\mu _i, c_j^\pm \geq 0\). Put \(\lambda _j = c_j^+ - c_j^-\) and \(\mu _i = 0\) for \(i \notin I\); this gives 27.6. Uniqueness follows from the linear independence, since \(\mu _i = 0\) is forced for \(i \notin I\).

The conditions were found by W. Karush in his 1939 master’s thesis and, independently, by H. W. Kuhn and A. W. Tucker in 1951, at the start of nonlinear programming. In the language of the introduction to this subsection: at a constrained minimum, \(-\nabla f(a)\), the direction of steepest descent, lies in the cone spanned by the outward normals \(\nabla g_i(a)\) of the active walls (and the normal space of the equations), so that every feasible direction is uphill to first order.

037L Counterexample 27.13.15

Without a constraint qualification the conclusion can fail. Minimise \(f(x, y) = x\) subject to \(g_1 = y - x^3 \leq 0\) and \(g_2 = -y \leq 0\). Feasibility means \(0 \leq y \leq x^3\), so \(x \geq 0\), and the minimum is at \((0, 0)\), where both constraints are active. There \(\nabla f = (1, 0)\), \(\nabla g_1 = (0, 1)\) and \(\nabla g_2 = (0, -1)\), and \((1, 0) + \mu _1(0, 1) + \mu _2(0, -1) = 0\) is impossible. The two active gradients are dependent: the feasible set has a cusp at the origin, and the linearised constraints \(\langle \nabla g_i, d\rangle \leq 0\), which allow every \(d = (d_1, 0)\), do not describe the feasible directions, which have \(d_1 \geq 0\).

037M Remark 27.13.16

Reading the proof again shows that weaker hypotheses suffice for the existence of multipliers (not for their uniqueness, which used the full linear independence).

  1. Mangasarian–Fromovitz condition: the \(\nabla h_j(a)\) are linearly independent and there is \(d_0\) with \(Dh(a)d_0 = 0\) and \(\langle \nabla g_i(a), d_0\rangle {\lt} 0\) for \(i \in I(a)\). Step 1 used only the independence of the \(\nabla h_j(a)\), Step 2 only the existence of such a \(d_0\), and Step 3 nothing else.

  2. Affine constraints: if all \(g_i\) and \(h_j\) are affine, no qualification is needed. Indeed, for \(d\) with \(\langle \nabla g_i(a), d\rangle \leq 0\) (\(i \in I(a)\)) and \(Dh(a)d = 0\), the points \(a + td\) are feasible for small \(t {\gt} 0\) (for active \(i\), \(g_i(a + td) = t\langle \nabla g_i(a), d\rangle \leq 0\)), so \(\langle \nabla f(a), d\rangle \geq 0\) directly, and Step 3 applies.

27.13.5 Convex problems037N

The conditions of the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker theorem are necessary. For convex problems they are also sufficient, and give global minima. A function \(f\) on a convex set \(K \subseteq \mathbb {R}^n\) is convex if \(f((1 - t)x + ty) \leq (1 - t)f(x) + tf(y)\) for all \(x, y \in K\) and \(t \in [0, 1]\), that is, if its restriction to every segment is convex in the sense of Definition 27.6.16, and strictly convex if the inequality is strict whenever \(x \neq y\) and \(0 {\lt} t {\lt} 1\). A sublevel set \(\{ x \in K : f(x) \leq c\} \) of a convex function is convex, and so is the zero set of an affine function; hence the feasible set of 27.5 is convex when \(U\) is convex, the \(g_i\) are convex and the \(h_j\) are affine.

037O Lemma 27.13.17

Let \(U \subseteq \mathbb {R}^n\) be open and convex, and \(f \colon U \to \mathbb {R}\) convex and \(C^1\). Then \(f(y) \geq f(x) + \langle \nabla f(x), y - x\rangle \) for all \(x, y \in U\). If \(f\) is \(C^2\) with positive semidefinite Hessian everywhere, then \(f\) is convex.

Proof â–¼

We may assume \(x \neq y\). As \(U\) is open, the points \(x - \varepsilon (y - x)\) and \(y + \varepsilon (y - x)\) lie in \(U\) for small \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\), and as \(U\) is convex so does the segment joining them. Hence \(\varphi (t) = f(x + t(y - x))\) is defined on the open interval \(J = (-\varepsilon , 1 + \varepsilon )\), it is convex there (being the restriction of \(f\) to a segment), and it is differentiable with \(\varphi '(0) = \langle \nabla f(x), y - x\rangle \) by the chain rule. By Proposition 27.6.18(2), \(\varphi (1) \geq \varphi (0) + \varphi '(0)\), which is the inequality. For the second statement, \(\varphi ''(t) = D^2f(x + t(y - x))(y - x, y - x) \geq 0\) on \(J\) by 27.3, so \(\varphi \) is convex by Proposition 27.6.18(3); this is convexity of \(f\) on the segment \([x, y]\).

037P Theorem 27.13.18

In 27.5, let \(U\) be convex, \(f\) and \(g_1, \ldots , g_p\) convex and \(h_1, \ldots , h_q\) affine. If \(a \in F\) and numbers \(\mu _i\), \(\lambda _j\) satisfy 27.6, then \(a\) is a global minimum of \(f\) on \(F\). If \(f\) is strictly convex, it is the only one.

Proof â–¼

Let \(x \in F\). By Lemma 27.13.17 and 27.6,

\[ f(x) - f(a) \geq \langle \nabla f(a), x - a\rangle = -\sum _i\mu _i\langle \nabla g_i(a), x - a\rangle - \sum _j\lambda _j\langle \nabla h_j(a), x - a\rangle . \]

Since \(h_j\) is affine, \(\langle \nabla h_j(a), x - a\rangle = h_j(x) - h_j(a) = 0\). By the lemma again and complementary slackness, \(\mu _i\langle \nabla g_i(a), x - a\rangle \leq \mu _i(g_i(x) - g_i(a)) = \mu _ig_i(x) \leq 0\). Hence \(f(x) \geq f(a)\). If \(f\) is strictly convex and \(f(x) = f(a)\) with \(x \neq a\), then the midpoint \(m = \frac12(x + a)\) is feasible (\(F\) is convex) and \(f(m) {\lt} \frac12f(x) + \frac12f(a) = f(a)\), a contradiction.

037Q Example 27.13.19 Water-filling

Given \(\alpha _1, \ldots , \alpha _n {\gt} 0\) and \(P {\gt} 0\), maximise \(\sum _i\log (\alpha _i + x_i)\) subject to \(x_i \geq 0\) and \(\sum _ix_i = P\). (In information theory, \(x_i\) is the power sent through the \(i\)-th of \(n\) parallel channels with noise level \(\alpha _i\), and \(\frac12\log (1 + x_i/\alpha _i)\) is its capacity, so this maximises the total capacity for a total power \(P\).) Minimise the strictly convex function \(f(x) = -\sum _i\log (\alpha _i + x_i)\) on \(U = \{ x_i {\gt} -\alpha _i\} \), with \(g_i = -x_i\) and \(h = \sum _ix_i - P\). The conditions 27.6 read

\[ -\frac{1}{\alpha _i + x_i} - \mu _i + \lambda = 0, \qquad \mu _i \geq 0, \qquad \mu _ix_i = 0. \]

So \(\lambda \geq 1/(\alpha _i + x_i) {\gt} 0\); put \(\nu = 1/\lambda \). If \(x_i {\gt} 0\), then \(\mu _i = 0\) and \(x_i = \nu - \alpha _i\); if \(x_i = 0\), then \(\lambda \geq 1/\alpha _i\), that is, \(\alpha _i \geq \nu \). In both cases

\[ x_i = \max (0, \nu - \alpha _i), \]

and \(\nu \) is fixed by \(\sum _i\max (0, \nu - \alpha _i) = P\); the left side is continuous and strictly increasing in \(\nu \geq \min _i\alpha _i\), from \(0\) to \(\infty \), so there is exactly one such \(\nu \) (Theorem 27.4.8). Conversely this \(x\), with \(\lambda = 1/\nu \) and \(\mu _i = \lambda - 1/(\alpha _i + x_i) \geq 0\), satisfies the conditions, so by Theorem 27.13.18 it is the unique optimum. The picture: a vessel whose floor has height \(\alpha _i\) over the \(i\)-th cell is filled with a volume \(P\) of water; the water settles at level \(\nu \), and \(x_i\) is the depth over the \(i\)-th cell. The noisiest channels get nothing.

037R Example 27.13.20 Maximum entropy and the Boltzmann distribution

Let \(E_1, \ldots , E_N\) be real numbers (the energies of the states of a physical system), not all equal, and \(\min _iE_i {\lt} E {\lt} \max _iE_i\). Among probability vectors \(p\) with \(p_i {\gt} 0\), \(\sum _ip_i = 1\) and mean energy \(\sum _ip_iE_i = E\), which one has the largest entropy \(H(p) = -\sum _ip_i\log p_i\)? The function \(-H(p) = \sum _i\phi (p_i)\), \(\phi (t) = t\log t\), is strictly convex on \(U = \{ p_i {\gt} 0\} \): \(\phi ''(t) = 1/t {\gt} 0\), so \(\phi \) is strictly convex (Proposition 27.6.18(3)), and for \(p \neq p^{\prime }\) the convexity inequality is strict in a coordinate where they differ. The two constraints are affine. The conditions 27.6 for minimising \(-H\) read \(\log p_i + 1 + \lambda _1 + \lambda _2E_i = 0\), so

\[ p_i = \frac{e^{-\beta E_i}}{Z(\beta )}, \qquad Z(\beta ) = \sum _ke^{-\beta E_k}, \]

with \(\beta = \lambda _2\), after normalising. This is the Boltzmann (Gibbs) distribution of statistical mechanics, \(\beta \) being the inverse temperature and \(Z\) the partition function. It remains to choose \(\beta \) with mean energy \(E\). The mean \(m(\beta ) = \sum _kE_ke^{-\beta E_k}/Z(\beta )\) is continuous, has derivative \(m'(\beta ) = -(\sum _kE_k^2p_k - m(\beta )^2) {\lt} 0\), minus the variance of the energy, which is positive since the \(E_k\) are not all equal, and tends to \(\min E_k\) as \(\beta \to +\infty \) and to \(\max E_k\) as \(\beta \to -\infty \). So exactly one \(\beta \) gives \(m(\beta ) = E\), and by Theorem 27.13.18 the corresponding \(p\) is the unique entropy maximiser. Without the energy constraint (\(\lambda _2 = 0\)) the maximiser is the uniform distribution, with \(H = \log N\).

037S Remark 27.13.21 Duality

For 27.5 define the Lagrangian \(L(x, \mu , \lambda ) = f(x) + \sum _i\mu _ig_i(x) + \sum _j\lambda _jh_j(x)\) and the dual function \(\theta (\mu , \lambda ) = \inf _{x \in U}L(x, \mu , \lambda )\). For \(\mu \geq 0\) and \(x \in F\), \(L(x, \mu , \lambda ) \leq f(x)\), so

\[ \theta (\mu , \lambda ) \leq \inf _Ff \qquad (\mu \geq 0) \qquad \text{(\emph{weak duality})}: \]

every choice of multipliers gives a lower bound for the minimum. In the convex situation of Theorem 27.13.18, if \((a, \mu , \lambda )\) satisfies the conditions 27.6, then \(x \mapsto L(x, \mu , \lambda )\) is convex with vanishing gradient at \(a\), so by Lemma 27.13.17 it is minimal at \(a\), and \(\theta (\mu , \lambda ) = L(a, \mu , \lambda ) = f(a)\) by complementary slackness and \(h(a) = 0\): the bound is attained, and the multipliers solve the dual problem of maximising \(\theta \) over \(\mu \geq 0\). For convex problems with a strictly feasible point (Slater’s condition) the maximum of the dual equals the minimum of the primal even without assuming that the minimum is attained [BV04, Chapter 5]; for linear programmes this is the duality theorem of linear programming, a consequence of Farkas’ lemma. The point \((a, (\mu , \lambda ))\) is then a saddle point of \(L\): a minimum in \(x\) and a maximum in \((\mu , \lambda )\) with \(\mu \geq 0\).
