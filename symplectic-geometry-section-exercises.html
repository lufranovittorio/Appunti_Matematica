41.8 Exercises02V7

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

02V8 Exercise 41.8.1

For which real numbers \(a\) is \(\sigma = dx^1 \wedge dy^1 + a\, dx^1 \wedge dx^2 + dx^2 \wedge dy^2\) a symplectic form on \(\mathbb {R}^4\)? Find a Lagrangian plane for it.

Solution â–¼

\(\sigma \) is closed (constant coefficients). \(\sigma ^2 = 2\, dx^1 \wedge dy^1 \wedge dx^2 \wedge dy^2\), since \(dx^1 \wedge dx^2\) wedges to zero with the other two terms and with itself; so \(\sigma \) is symplectic for every \(a\) (Proposition 41.2.2(1)). The plane \(\{ x^1 = x^2 = 0\} \) is Lagrangian, as every term contains \(dx^1\) or \(dx^2\).

02V9 Exercise 41.8.2

Using Example 41.5.5, compute the area of the spherical cap \(\{ z {\gt} \cos \vartheta _0\} \) and compare with the holonomy of the sphere computed in Example 38.5.6(2).

Solution â–¼

The cap is \(\{ \cos \vartheta _0 {\lt} z {\lt} 1\} \), of area \(2\pi (1 - \cos \vartheta _0)\); this is the angle of the rotation produced by parallel transport around its boundary circle, modulo \(2\pi \), in accordance with the Gauss–Bonnet theorem for the curvature \(1\).

02VA Exercise 41.8.3

Show that \(S^4\), \(S^2 \times S^4\) and \(S^1 \times S^3\) admit no symplectic form, and that \(T^4\), \(S^2 \times S^2\) and \(S^2 \times T^2\) do.

Solution â–¼

\(H^2_{\mathrm{dR}}\) vanishes for \(S^4\) and for \(S^1 \times S^3\) (Künneth, Theorem 37.3.8), and for \(S^2 \times S^4\) the square of every class in \(H^2\) vanishes, as in Example 41.3.3(3); Proposition 41.3.2 applies. Products of symplectic manifolds (tori, area forms on \(S^2\)) are symplectic.

02VB Exercise 41.8.4

Let \(f \colon \mathbb {R}^n \to \mathbb {R}\) be smooth. Show that \(\Phi (q, p) = (q, p + df(q))\) is a symplectomorphism of \(T^*\mathbb {R}^n\), the time-one map of the Hamiltonian \(H(q, p) = -f(q)\), which maps the zero section onto the Lagrangian graph of \(df\).

Solution â–¼

\(\Phi ^*\sum dq^i \wedge dp_i = \sum dq^i \wedge (dp_i + \sum _j\partial _i\partial _jf\, dq^j) = \sum dq^i \wedge dp_i\), since \(\sum _{i,j}\partial _i\partial _jf\, dq^i \wedge dq^j = 0\) by symmetry. For \(H = -f(q)\), Hamilton’s equations are \(\dot q = 0\), \(\dot p = \nabla f(q)\), with time-one map \(\Phi \).

02VC Exercise 41.8.5

On \(T^*\mathbb {R}^n\) compute \(\{ q^i, q^j\} \), \(\{ p_i, p_j\} \) and \(\{ q^i, p_j\} \), and show that the angular momentum \(L = q^1p_2 - q^2p_1\) Poisson-commutes with every rotation-invariant Hamiltonian \(H = h(|q|^2, |p|^2, \langle q, p\rangle )\) on \(T^*\mathbb {R}^2\).

Solution â–¼

With \(\{ F, G\} = \sum _k(\partial _{q^k}F\, \partial _{p_k}G - \partial _{p_k}F\, \partial _{q^k}G)\): \(\{ q^i, q^j\} = \{ p_i, p_j\} = 0\) and \(\{ q^i, p_j\} = \delta _{ij}\). The flow of \(X_L\) is \(\dot q = (-q^2, q^1)\), \(\dot p = (-p_2, p_1)\), the simultaneous rotation of \(q\) and \(p\), which preserves \(|q|^2\), \(|p|^2\) and \(\langle q, p\rangle \); so \(H\) is constant along it, and \(\{ H, L\} = X_LH = 0\) (Proposition 41.5.3(3)).

02XF Exercise 41.8.6

Let \(q_0\) be a nondegenerate critical point of a smooth function \(f\) on \(M\) (Remark 41.4.7). Show that \(q_0\) has a neighbourhood containing no other critical point of \(f\). Deduce that a Morse function on a compact manifold has finitely many critical points.

Solution â–¼

In a chart around \(q_0\), the critical points of \(f\) are the zeros of \(F = (\partial _1f, \ldots , \partial _nf)\), a smooth map from an open subset of \(\mathbb {R}^n\) to \(\mathbb {R}^n\). Its Jacobian matrix at \(q_0\) is \((\partial _j\partial _if(q_0))\), the matrix of \(\operatorname {Hess}f_{q_0}\) (Corollary 41.4.6(3)), which is invertible. By the inverse function theorem (Theorem 33.4.14), \(F\) maps a neighbourhood \(W\) of \(q_0\) bijectively onto an open set; so \(F\) vanishes on \(W\) only at \(q_0\). If \(M\) is compact and \(f\) is a Morse function, the set of critical points is closed (the zero set of the continuous map \(df\) into \(T^*M\), a preimage of the closed zero section) hence compact, and each of its points has a neighbourhood containing no other; a cover of it by such neighbourhoods has a finite subcover, so it is finite.

02XG Exercise 41.8.7

Let \(\alpha \) be a \(1\)-form on \(M\) and \(\nabla \) a connection on \(TM\) with Christoffel symbols \(\Gamma ^k_{ij}\). Show that in coordinates the vertical part of \(\alpha _*(\partial _{q^j})\) (Proposition 41.4.5(3)) is \(\sum _k\bigl(\partial _ja_k - \sum _i\Gamma ^i_{jk}a_i\bigr)\, dq^k\). Deduce that at a point where \(\alpha \) vanishes, the vertical part of \(\alpha _*\) does not depend on the connection, and that elsewhere it does: for \(\alpha = dq^1\) on \(\mathbb {R}^2\), find a torsion-free connection for which \(\nabla _{\partial _{q^1}}\alpha \neq 0\), although the Jacobian matrix of \(\alpha \) vanishes.

Solution â–¼

By Proposition 38.3.6(1), \((\nabla _{\partial _j}\alpha )(\partial _k) = \partial _j(a_k) - \alpha (\nabla _{\partial _j}\partial _k) = \partial _ja_k - \sum _i\Gamma ^i_{jk}a_i\), which is the formula. Where all \(a_i\) vanish it reduces to \(\partial _ja_k\), which does not involve \(\Gamma \). For \(\alpha = dq^1\), so \(a_1 = 1\) and \(a_2 = 0\), take the connection with \(\Gamma ^1_{11} = 1\) and all other symbols zero; it is torsion-free, since \(\Gamma ^k_{ij} = \Gamma ^k_{ji}\). Then \((\nabla _{\partial _1}\alpha )(\partial _1) = 0 - \Gamma ^1_{11} = -1\), while \(\partial _ja_k = 0\) for all \(j, k\).

03EO Exercise 41.8.8

On \(S^*\mathbb {R}^2 = \mathbb {R}^2 \times S^1\), write \(\xi = (\cos \phi , \sin \phi )\), so that the contact form is \(\alpha = \cos \phi \, dx + \sin \phi \, dy\).

  1. Check directly that \(\alpha \wedge d\alpha = dx \wedge d\phi \wedge dy\), so that \(\alpha \) is a contact form.

  2. Let \(P\) be a convex polygon with vertices \(p_1, \ldots , p_m\) in counterclockwise order, and let \(\phi _j\) be the angle of the outward normal of the edge \(E_j\) from \(p_j\) to \(p_{j+1}\) (indices modulo \(m\)). Its normal cycle \(N(P)\) is the closed curve made of the segments \(E_j \times \{ \phi _j\} \) and of the arcs \(\{ p_{j+1}\} \times [\phi _j, \phi _{j+1}]\), where the angle increases by the exterior angle \(\theta _j \in (0, \pi )\) at \(p_{j+1}\). Show that \(\alpha \) vanishes on \(N(P)\), and that \(\int _{N(P)}d\phi = 2\pi = 2\pi \chi (P)\).

  3. Show that \(\int _{N(P)}(-\sin \phi \, dx + \cos \phi \, dy)\) is the perimeter \(L\) of \(P\), and that the \(\varepsilon \)-neighbourhood of \(P\) has area \(\operatorname {area}(P) + \varepsilon L + \pi \varepsilon ^2\): the coefficients of Steiner’s formula are integrals over the normal cycle, and the curvature of \(P\) is concentrated at its vertices.

Solution â–¼

(1) \(d\alpha = -\sin \phi \, d\phi \wedge dx + \cos \phi \, d\phi \wedge dy\), so \(\alpha \wedge d\alpha = \cos ^2\phi \, dx \wedge d\phi \wedge dy - \sin ^2\phi \, dy \wedge d\phi \wedge dx = (\cos ^2\phi + \sin ^2\phi )\, dx \wedge d\phi \wedge dy\), since exchanging the first and the last factor changes the sign.

(2) Along \(E_j \times \{ \phi _j\} \) the velocity is \((T_j, 0)\) with \(T_j\) the direction of the edge, orthogonal to the outward normal \(\xi _j = (\cos \phi _j, \sin \phi _j)\), so \(\alpha (T_j, 0) = \langle \xi _j, T_j\rangle = 0\), and \(d\phi \) vanishes too. Along the arcs \(x\) is constant, so \(dx = dy = 0\) and \(\alpha = 0\), while \(\int d\phi = \theta _j\). Hence \(\int _{N(P)}d\phi = \sum _j\theta _j\). The interior angle at \(p_{j+1}\) is \(\pi - \theta _j\), and the interior angles of a convex \(m\)-gon add up to \((m - 2)\pi \) (cut it into \(m - 2\) triangles from one vertex), so \(\sum _j\theta _j = m\pi - (m - 2)\pi = 2\pi \).

(3) The form \(\beta = -\sin \phi \, dx + \cos \phi \, dy\) evaluates the \(x\)-velocity against the unit vector \((-\sin \phi , \cos \phi )\), which on \(E_j \times \{ \phi _j\} \) is the counterclockwise unit tangent \(T_j\) (the outward normal rotated by \(+\pi /2\)). So \(\int _{E_j \times \{ \phi _j\} }\beta = |E_j|\), the arcs contribute \(0\), and the total is \(L\). A point at distance \(t \in (0, \varepsilon ]\) from \(P\) has a unique nearest point in \(P\) (Theorem 30.3.3), either in the interior of an edge \(E_j\), and then it lies in the rectangle of width \(\varepsilon \) erected outwards on \(E_j\), or a vertex \(p_{j+1}\), and then it lies in the circular sector of radius \(\varepsilon \) and angle \(\theta _j\) between the normals \(\xi _j\) and \(\xi _{j+1}\). These pieces overlap only along segments, so the area of the \(\varepsilon \)-neighbourhood is \(\operatorname {area}(P) + \sum _j\varepsilon |E_j| + \sum _j\frac12\theta _j\varepsilon ^2 = \operatorname {area}(P) + \varepsilon L + \pi \varepsilon ^2\). The coefficient of \(\varepsilon \) is \(\int _{N(P)}\beta \), and that of \(\varepsilon ^2\) is \(\frac12\int _{N(P)}d\phi \), which comes only from the arcs over the vertices.
