5.8 Limits00AP

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

00AQ Definition 5.8.1

Let \(\mathcal{I}\) be a small category and \(F \colon \mathcal{I} \to \mathcal{C}\) a functor, called a diagram of shape \(\mathcal{I}\). A cone over \(F\) is an object \(L\) together with morphisms \(\lambda _i \colon L \to F(i)\) for all \(i \in \mathcal{I}\) such that \(F(u) \circ \lambda _i = \lambda _j\) for every \(u \colon i \to j\). A limit of \(F\) is a cone \((L, \lambda )\) such that for every cone \((M, \mu )\) there is a unique morphism \(m \colon M \to L\) with \(\lambda _i \circ m = \mu _i\) for all \(i\). We write \(L = \lim F\) or \(\lim _{i \in \mathcal{I}} F(i)\).

00AR Lemma 5.8.2

If \((L, \lambda )\) and \((L', \lambda ')\) are limits of \(F\), there is a unique morphism \(m \colon L' \to L\) with \(\lambda _i \circ m = \lambda '_i\) for all \(i\), and it is an isomorphism.

Proof â–¼

Existence and uniqueness of \(m\) is the definition. Let \(m' \colon L \to L'\) be the morphism given by the universal property of \(L'\). Then \(m \circ m'\) and \(\mathrm{id}_L\) are both morphisms of cones from \((L, \lambda )\) to itself, so they are equal by uniqueness; similarly \(m' \circ m = \mathrm{id}_{L'}\).

00AS Example 5.8.3

  1. If \(\mathcal{I}\) is empty, a limit is a terminal object: an object \(T\) such that every object has exactly one morphism to \(T\).

  2. If \(\mathcal{I}\) is a discrete category, a limit of \((X_i)_{i \in \mathcal{I}}\) is a product \(\prod _i X_i\) with projections \(p_i\).

  3. If \(\mathcal{I}\) has two objects \(0, 1\) and two non-identity morphisms \(u, v \colon 0 \to 1\), a diagram is a pair of parallel morphisms \(f, g \colon X \to Y\), and a limit is an equaliser: a morphism \(e \colon E \to X\) with \(fe = ge\) through which every \(h\) with \(fh = gh\) factors uniquely.

  4. If \(\mathcal{I}\) is \(1 \to 0 \leftarrow 2\), a diagram is a pair \(f \colon X \to Z\), \(g \colon Y \to Z\), and a limit is a fibre product \(X \times _Z Y\).

  5. If \(\mathcal{I}\) is the poset \(\mathbb {N}\) with the opposite order, a limit of a sequence \(\cdots \to X_2 \to X_1 \to X_0\) is an inverse limit.

00AT Definition 5.8.4

A category has limits (respectively finite limits) if every diagram \(F \colon \mathcal{I} \to \mathcal{C}\) with \(\mathcal{I}\) small (respectively with finitely many morphisms) has a limit. A functor \(G \colon \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{D}\) preserves the limit \((L, \lambda )\) of \(F\) if \((G(L), G(\lambda ))\) is a limit of \(G \circ F\).

00AU Proposition 5.8.5

\(\mathsf{Set}\) has limits. For \(F \colon \mathcal{I} \to \mathsf{Set}\) with \(\mathcal{I}\) small, a limit is

\[ \lim F = \Bigl\{ (x_i) \in \prod _{i \in \operatorname {Ob}(\mathcal{I})} F(i) : F(u)(x_i) = x_j \text{ for all } u \colon i \to j\Bigr\} \]

with the projections \(\lambda _i((x_k)_k) = x_i\).

Proof â–¼

The set \(\lim F\) is small by Lemma 3.8.2, and it is a cone by construction. If \((M, \mu )\) is a cone, the map \(m(y) = (\mu _i(y))_i\) lands in \(\lim F\), satisfies \(\lambda _i \circ m = \mu _i\), and is the only such map.

00AV Theorem 5.8.6

If \(\mathcal{C}\) has products indexed by small sets and equalisers, then it has limits. If \(\mathcal{C}\) has a terminal object, binary products and equalisers, then it has finite limits.

Proof â–¼

Let \(F \colon \mathcal{I} \to \mathcal{C}\) be a diagram. Let \(P = \prod _{i} F(i)\) over the objects of \(\mathcal{I}\), and \(Q = \prod _{u} F(t(u))\) over all morphisms \(u \colon s(u) \to t(u)\) of \(\mathcal{I}\), with projections \(p_i\) and \(q_u\). Define \(a, b \colon P \to Q\) by \(q_u \circ a = F(u) \circ p_{s(u)}\) and \(q_u \circ b = p_{t(u)}\). Let \(e \colon L \to P\) be an equaliser of \(a\) and \(b\), and \(\lambda _i = p_i \circ e\). The equation \(ae = be\) says exactly that \(F(u) \circ \lambda _{s(u)} = \lambda _{t(u)}\) for all \(u\), so \((L, \lambda )\) is a cone. If \((M, \mu )\) is a cone, the morphism \(\mu \colon M \to P\) with \(p_i \circ \mu = \mu _i\) satisfies \(a\mu = b\mu \), so it factors uniquely as \(\mu = e \circ m\); and a morphism \(m\) satisfies \(\lambda _i \circ m = \mu _i\) for all \(i\) if and only if \(e \circ m = \mu \). So \((L, \lambda )\) is a limit. If \(\mathcal{I}\) is finite, only finite products are needed, and these can be built from binary products and a terminal object (the empty product) by induction.

00AW Proposition 5.8.7

Let \(\mathcal{C}\) be locally small, \(F \colon \mathcal{I} \to \mathcal{C}\) a diagram and \((L, \lambda )\) a cone over \(F\). Then \((L, \lambda )\) is a limit if and only if for every \(X \in \mathcal{C}\) the map

\[ \operatorname {Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(X, L) \longrightarrow \lim _{i \in \mathcal{I}} \operatorname {Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(X, F(i)), \qquad m \longmapsto (\lambda _i \circ m)_i \]

is a bijection, where the right-hand side is the limit in \(\mathsf{Set}\) of Proposition 5.8.5. In particular the functors \(h^X = \operatorname {Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(X, -)\) preserve all limits.

Proof â–¼

An element of the right-hand side is exactly a cone with vertex \(X\), so bijectivity for all \(X\) is the definition of a limit.

00AX Proposition 5.8.8

Let \(\mathcal{A}\) be a category and suppose that \(\mathcal{C}\) has limits of shape \(\mathcal{I}\). Then \(\operatorname {Fun}(\mathcal{A}, \mathcal{C})\) has limits of shape \(\mathcal{I}\), computed pointwise: for \(F \colon \mathcal{I} \to \operatorname {Fun}(\mathcal{A}, \mathcal{C})\) there is a limit \(L\) with \(L(a) = \lim _i F(i)(a)\) for all \(a \in \mathcal{A}\).

Proof â–¼

For each \(a\) choose a limit \((L(a), \lambda ^a)\) of \(i \mapsto F(i)(a)\). For \(f \colon a \to a'\), the morphisms \(F(i)(f) \circ \lambda ^a_i\) form a cone over \(i \mapsto F(i)(a')\) (by naturality of the \(F(u)\)), so there is a unique \(L(f) \colon L(a) \to L(a')\) with \(\lambda ^{a'}_i \circ L(f) = F(i)(f) \circ \lambda ^a_i\). Uniqueness shows that \(L\) is a functor and that the \(\lambda _i = (\lambda ^a_i)_a\) are natural transformations \(L \Rightarrow F(i)\) forming a cone. If \((M, \mu )\) is a cone in the functor category, then for each \(a\) there is a unique \(m_a\) with \(\lambda ^a_i \circ m_a = \mu _{i,a}\), and uniqueness again shows that \(m = (m_a)_a\) is natural; it is the unique morphism of cones.

00AY Counterexample 5.8.9

The category \(\mathsf{Field}\) of small fields and ring homomorphisms does not have binary products. First, a ring homomorphism \(f \colon K \to K'\) between fields is injective: if \(f(a) = 0\) with \(a \neq 0\) then \(1 = f(a)f(a^{-1}) = 0\). Let \(\mathbb {F}_2 = \{ 0, 1\} \) be the field with \(1 + 1 = 0\). If \(P\) were a product of \(\mathbb {Q}\) and \(\mathbb {F}_2\), there would be homomorphisms \(P \to \mathbb {Q}\) and \(P \to \mathbb {F}_2\). The second one sends \(1 + 1\) to \(0\), so \(1 + 1 = 0\) in \(P\) by injectivity; but then the first one sends \(0\) to \(2 \neq 0\).
