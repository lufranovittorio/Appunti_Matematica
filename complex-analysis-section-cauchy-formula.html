29.4 The Cauchy integral formula014J

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

014K Theorem 29.4.1

Let \(U\) be convex, \(f\) holomorphic on \(U\), \(\gamma \) a closed path in \(U\) and \(a \in U \setminus \gamma ^*\). Then

\[ n(\gamma , a)f(a) = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\int _\gamma \frac{f(z)}{z - a}\, dz. \]

In particular, if \(\overline{D}(a, r) \subseteq U\) and \(\gamma \) is the circle \(\partial D(a, r)\) traversed once, then \(f(w) = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\int _\gamma \frac{f(z)}{z - w}dz\) for \(w \in D(a, r)\).

Proof â–¼

Define \(g(z) = (f(z) - f(a))/(z - a)\) for \(z \neq a\) and \(g(a) = f'(a)\). Then \(g\) is continuous on \(U\) and holomorphic on \(U \setminus \{ a\} \), so \(\int _\gamma g = 0\) by Corollary 29.3.2. For the second statement, note that \(n(\gamma , w) = n(\gamma , a) = 1\) for \(w \in D(a, r)\) by Proposition 29.3.5, and apply the formula in the convex set \(D(a, r')\) for \(r {\lt} r' \) with \(\overline{D}(a, r') \subseteq U\).

014L Theorem 29.4.2

Let \(f\) be holomorphic on an open \(U\) and \(D(a, R) \subseteq U\). Then \(f(w) = \sum _{n \geq 0}c_n(w - a)^n\) on \(D(a, R)\), with \(c_n = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\int _{\partial D(a, r)}\frac{f(z)}{(z - a)^{n+1}}dz = \frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}\) for any \(0 {\lt} r {\lt} R\). In particular holomorphic functions have holomorphic derivatives of all orders, and \(|f^{(n)}(a)| \leq n!\, r^{-n}\sup _{|z - a| = r}|f|\) (Cauchy estimates).

Proof â–¼

Fix \(|w - a| {\lt} r {\lt} R\). For \(|z - a| = r\), \(\frac{1}{z - w} = \sum _{n \geq 0}\frac{(w - a)^n}{(z - a)^{n+1}}\), converging uniformly in \(z\) since \(|w - a|/r {\lt} 1\). Multiplying by \(f(z)/2\pi i\) and integrating term by term (uniform convergence, Proposition 27.8.2) gives the expansion with the stated \(c_n\), by Theorem 29.4.1. A power series is infinitely complex differentiable with \(c_n = f^{(n)}(a)/n!\) (Theorem 27.6.20), and the estimate follows from Lemma 29.2.8.

The last statement of Theorem 29.4.2 deserves a closer look, because nothing like it is true for real functions. Definition 29.2.1 only asks that \(f^{\prime }(a)\) exists at every point; it does not ask that \(f^{\prime }\) be continuous. For a function of one real variable this would be a much weaker requirement than being of class \(C^1\): \(x^2\sin (1/x)\), extended by \(0\) at \(0\), is differentiable everywhere with a discontinuous derivative, and its antiderivatives are differentiable twice but not three times. For complex differentiability the hierarchy collapses: one derivative gives all of them, and they are all continuous.

035V Corollary 29.4.3

Let \(f = u + iv\) be holomorphic on an open \(U \subseteq \mathbb {C}\), and identify \(\mathbb {C}\) with \(\mathbb {R}^2\), \(z = x + iy\).

  1. The derivatives \(f^{\prime }, f^{\prime \prime }, \ldots , f^{(k)}, \ldots \) exist and are holomorphic, hence continuous, on \(U\).

  2. As a function of the two real variables \(x, y\), \(f\) is of class \(C^\infty \), and for all \(a, b \geq 0\)

    \[ \partial _x^a\partial _y^bf = i^bf^{(a + b)} . \]

    In particular \(u\) and \(v\) are of class \(C^\infty \).

  3. \(u\) and \(v\) are harmonic: \(\partial _x^2u + \partial _y^2u = 0\) and \(\partial _x^2v + \partial _y^2v = 0\) on \(U\).

Proof â–¼

(1) By Theorem 29.4.2, applied on discs \(D(a, R) \subseteq U\), the derivative of a holomorphic function is holomorphic. By induction on \(k\) every \(f^{(k)}\) exists and is holomorphic. A function that is complex differentiable at a point is continuous there, since \(f(a + h) - f(a) = h \cdot (f(a + h) - f(a))/h \to 0 \cdot f^{\prime }(a) = 0\).

(2) Let \(g\) be holomorphic on \(U\). By Proposition 29.2.3, \(g\) is real differentiable and \(Dg(a)\) is multiplication by \(g^{\prime }(a)\). The partial derivatives are the values of \(Dg(a)\) on the basis vectors \(1\) and \(i\) of \(\mathbb {R}^2 = \mathbb {C}\):

\[ \partial _xg(a) = g^{\prime }(a) \cdot 1 = g^{\prime }(a), \qquad \partial _yg(a) = g^{\prime }(a) \cdot i = ig^{\prime }(a). \]

So \(\partial _x\) and \(\partial _y\) map holomorphic functions to holomorphic functions, acting as \(g \mapsto g^{\prime }\) and \(g \mapsto ig^{\prime }\). Applying this \(a + b\) times, starting from \(g = f\) and using (1), shows that every partial derivative \(\partial _x^a\partial _y^bf\) (in any order of differentiation) exists and equals \(i^bf^{(a + b)}\), which is continuous by (1). Hence \(f\) is \(C^\infty \) by definition, and so are its real and imaginary parts \(u = \operatorname {Re}f\) and \(v = \operatorname {Im}f\), whose partial derivatives are the real and imaginary parts of those of \(f\).

(3) By (2), \(\partial _x^2f + \partial _y^2f = f^{\prime \prime } + i^2f^{\prime \prime } = 0\). The real and imaginary parts of this equation are \(\Delta u = 0\) and \(\Delta v = 0\), where \(\Delta = \partial _x^2 + \partial _y^2\), since \(\partial _x\) and \(\partial _y\) commute with taking real and imaginary parts.

035W Remark 29.4.4

Where does the regularity come from? The proof of Corollary 29.4.3 passes through four steps, and it is worth seeing what each one uses.

  1. Goursat’s theorem (Theorem 29.3.1) uses complex differentiability only at a single point \(z_0\) for each triangle, through the linear approximation \(f(z) = f(z_0) + f^{\prime }(z_0)(z - z_0) + o(|z - z_0|)\). No continuity of \(f^{\prime }\) is needed. This is the crucial step: the classical proof of Cauchy’s theorem by Green’s formula, \(\int _{\partial T}f\, dz = \iint _T(\partial _xf + i\partial _yf)\, i\, dx\, dy\), needs \(f\) to be of class \(C^1\), and would give the result only for \(C^1\) functions.

  2. From vanishing integrals over triangles one gets a primitive on discs (Corollary 29.3.2), and from that the Cauchy integral formula (Theorem 29.4.1), applied to the auxiliary function \(g(z) = (f(z) - f(a))/(z - a)\), which is continuous at \(a\) and holomorphic elsewhere. This is why Goursat’s theorem allows one exceptional point.

  3. The Cauchy formula \(f(w) = \frac1{2\pi i}\int _{|z - a| = r}\frac{f(z)}{z - w}\, dz\) expresses \(f\) inside the disc as an average of its values on the circle against the kernel \(1/(z - w)\), which is infinitely differentiable in \(w\). All the regularity of the kernel passes to \(f\): one may differentiate under the integral sign, or, as in Theorem 29.4.2, expand the kernel in a geometric series and obtain a power series.

  4. Power series are infinitely differentiable (Theorem 27.6.20).

So the equation \(\partial f/\partial \bar z = 0\) (the Cauchy–Riemann equations, see below) forces smoothness. This is the first instance of elliptic regularity: solutions of elliptic differential equations are as smooth as the equation allows, whatever regularity was assumed a priori; Chapter 42 proves this in general, and it is what makes harmonic forms smooth in the Hodge theorem.

It is often convenient to package the two real partial derivatives into the Wirtinger derivatives

\[ \frac{\partial }{\partial z} = \frac12\Bigl(\partial _x - i\partial _y\Bigr), \qquad \frac{\partial }{\partial \bar z} = \frac12\Bigl(\partial _x + i\partial _y\Bigr), \]

defined for real differentiable functions. For \(f = u + iv\) the real and imaginary parts of \(2\, \partial f/\partial \bar z = (\partial _xu - \partial _yv) + i(\partial _xv + \partial _yu)\) are the two Cauchy–Riemann expressions, so a real differentiable \(f\) is holomorphic exactly when \(\partial f/\partial \bar z = 0\), and then \(\partial f/\partial z = f^{\prime }\). On \(C^2\) functions

\[ 4\frac{\partial }{\partial z}\frac{\partial }{\partial \bar z} = (\partial _x - i\partial _y)(\partial _x + i\partial _y) = \partial _x^2 + \partial _y^2 = \Delta , \]

since \(\partial _x\) and \(\partial _y\) commute on \(C^2\) functions (Theorem 27.10.5). This gives a second proof of part (3): \(\Delta f = 4\frac{\partial }{\partial z}\bigl(\frac{\partial f}{\partial \bar z}\bigr) = 0\). The Wirtinger derivatives satisfy the product rule, and \(\partial \bar g/\partial \bar z = \overline{\partial g/\partial z}\), as one checks from the definitions.

035X Example 29.4.5

  1. From \(z^2 = (x^2 - y^2) + i\, 2xy\), the functions \(x^2 - y^2\) and \(2xy\) are harmonic; from \(e^z = e^x\cos y + ie^x\sin y\), so are \(e^x\cos y\) and \(e^x\sin y\). The level curves of \(u\) and \(v\) meet at right angles wherever \(f^{\prime } \neq 0\): by the Cauchy–Riemann equations, \(\nabla u = (\partial _xu, \partial _yu)\) and \(\nabla v = (-\partial _yu, \partial _xu)\) are orthogonal vectors of the same length \(|f^{\prime }|\).

  2. \(\log |f|\) is harmonic where \(f \neq 0\). On a disc \(D \subseteq U\) on which \(f\) has no zeros, \(f^{\prime }/f\) is holomorphic, so it has a primitive \(g\) (Corollary 29.3.2); then \((fe^{-g})^{\prime } = (f^{\prime } - fg^{\prime })e^{-g} = 0\), so \(f = ce^g\) with a constant \(c = e^{c_0} \neq 0\), and \(\log |f| = \operatorname {Re}(g + c_0)\) is harmonic. For example \(\log |z| = \frac12\log (x^2 + y^2)\) is harmonic on \(\mathbb {C}^\times \), although it is not the real part of a holomorphic function \(g\) on all of \(\mathbb {C}^\times \): by the Cauchy–Riemann equations \(g^{\prime } = \partial _x\operatorname {Re}g - i\partial _y\operatorname {Re}g\), which for \(\operatorname {Re}g = \log |z|\) is \((x - iy)/(x^2 + y^2) = 1/z\), and \(1/z\) has no primitive on \(\mathbb {C}^\times \), its integral over the unit circle being \(2\pi i\) (Lemma 29.2.8).

  3. \(|f|^2\) is not harmonic, but subharmonic. With the product rule, \(\frac{\partial }{\partial \bar z}(f\bar f) = f\, \overline{f^{\prime }}\), because \(\partial f/\partial \bar z = 0\) and \(\partial \bar f/\partial \bar z = \overline{f^{\prime }}\); differentiating again, \(\frac{\partial }{\partial z}\bigl(f\, \overline{f^{\prime }}\bigr) = f^{\prime }\, \overline{f^{\prime }}\), since \(\overline{f^{\prime }}\) has \(\partial /\partial z\) equal to \(\overline{\partial f^{\prime }/\partial \bar z} = 0\). Hence \(\Delta |f|^2 = 4|f^{\prime }|^2 \geq 0\). For \(f(z) = z\) this is \(\Delta (x^2 + y^2) = 4\).

035Y Counterexample 29.4.6

The Cauchy–Riemann equations at a point do not imply complex differentiability there; Proposition 29.2.3 also requires real differentiability. Let \(f(x + iy) = \sqrt{|xy|}\), so \(u = \sqrt{|xy|}\) and \(v = 0\). Since \(u\) vanishes on both axes, \(\partial _xu(0) = \partial _yu(0) = 0\), and the Cauchy–Riemann equations hold at \(0\). But for \(h = t(1 + i)\) with \(t \in \mathbb {R}^\times \), \((f(h) - f(0))/h = |t|/(t(1 + i))\), which is \(\frac1{1 + i}\) for \(t {\gt} 0\) and \(-\frac1{1 + i}\) for \(t {\lt} 0\); so \(f^{\prime }(0)\) does not exist. Existence of the partial derivatives only concerns the two directions of the axes, while complex differentiability concerns all directions at once.

014M Corollary 29.4.7

  1. (Liouville) A bounded entire function is constant; more generally, if \(|f(z)| \leq C(1 + |z|)^m\) for an entire \(f\), then \(f\) is a polynomial of degree at most \(m\).

  2. (Fundamental theorem of algebra) Every nonconstant polynomial over \(\mathbb {C}\) has a root (cf. Chapter 9).

  3. (Morera) A continuous function on an open set whose integral over the boundary of every closed triangle contained in the set vanishes is holomorphic.

  4. (Weierstrass) If holomorphic functions \(f_n\) on \(U\) converge uniformly on compact subsets to \(f\), then \(f\) is holomorphic and \(f_n^{(k)} \to f^{(k)}\) uniformly on compact subsets.

Proof â–¼

(1) By the Cauchy estimates with \(r \to \infty \), \(f^{(n)}(a) = 0\) for \(n {\gt} m\) and all \(a\). (2) If \(p\) has no root, \(1/p\) is entire and tends to \(0\) at infinity, hence is bounded and constant. (3) Locally on a disc the hypothesis gives a primitive \(F\) as in Corollary 29.3.2, and \(F\) is holomorphic, hence so is \(F' = f\) by Theorem 29.4.2. (4) The uniform limit is continuous and satisfies Morera’s condition by Theorem 29.3.1 and uniform convergence; convergence of derivatives follows from the Cauchy integral formula for \(f^{(k)}\) on circles inside \(U\).

014N Theorem 29.4.8 Identity theorem

Let \(f\) be holomorphic on a domain \(U\). If the zero set of \(f\) has a limit point in \(U\), then \(f \equiv 0\). Consequently two holomorphic functions agreeing on a set with a limit point in \(U\) are equal, and the zeros of a nonzero \(f\) are isolated, each of finite order \(m\) with \(f(z) = (z - a)^mg(z)\), \(g\) holomorphic and \(g(a) \neq 0\).

Proof â–¼

Let \(A\) be the set of \(a \in U\) with \(f^{(n)}(a) = 0\) for all \(n\); it is closed in \(U\), and open because \(f\) vanishes identically near such a point by Theorem 29.4.2. If \(z_k \to a\) with \(z_k \neq a\) and \(f(z_k) = 0\), and if some derivative at \(a\) were nonzero, then with \(m\) minimal, \(f(z) = (z - a)^mg(z)\) with \(g(a) \neq 0\) by the power series expansion, so \(f\) would have no zeros near \(a\) other than \(a\). Hence \(a \in A\), and \(A = U\) by connectedness.

014O Theorem 29.4.9 Maximum modulus principle

Let \(f\) be holomorphic on a domain \(U\). If \(|f|\) has a local maximum in \(U\), then \(f\) is constant. If moreover \(U\) is bounded and \(f\) extends continuously to \(\overline{U}\), then \(|f|\) attains its maximum on \(\partial U\).

Proof â–¼

Theorem 29.4.1 on circles gives the mean value property \(f(a) = \int _0^1f(a + re^{2\pi it})\, dt\). If \(|f| \leq |f(a)|\) on \(D(a, R)\), then for \(r {\lt} R\), \(|f(a)| \leq \int _0^1|f(a + re^{2\pi it})|\, dt \leq |f(a)|\), so \(|f| = |f(a)|\) on every such circle by continuity, and \(|f|\) is constant on \(D(a, R)\). Then \(f\bar f\) is constant; if \(f(a) \neq 0\) this makes \(\bar f = c/f\) holomorphic, so both \(f\) and \(\bar f\) satisfy the Cauchy–Riemann equations and \(f' = 0\) on the disc. In either case \(f\) is constant on \(D(a, R)\), hence on \(U\) by Theorem 29.4.8. The last statement follows from compactness of \(\overline{U}\).
