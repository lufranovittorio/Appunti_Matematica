5.4 Upward Löwenheim–Skolem and categoricity02FV

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

02FW Theorem 5.4.1 Upward Löwenheim–Skolem

Let \(T\) be an \(\mathcal{L}\)-theory with an infinite model, and \(\kappa \geq \| \mathcal{L}\| \) a cardinal. Then \(T\) has a model of cardinality exactly \(\kappa \).

Proof â–¼

Add new constant symbols \(c_\alpha \), \(\alpha {\lt} \kappa \), to get a language \(\mathcal{L}'\) with \(\| \mathcal{L}'\| = \kappa \) (Corollary 3.7.6), and let \(\Gamma = T \cup \{ c_\alpha \neq c_\beta : \alpha {\lt} \beta {\lt} \kappa \} \). A finite subset of \(\Gamma \) mentions finitely many new constants; interpreting them as distinct elements of an infinite model of \(T\) (and the other new constants arbitrarily) satisfies it. By Theorem 5.3.5, \(\Gamma \) has a model \(\mathcal{B}\), and \(\alpha \mapsto c_\alpha ^{\mathcal{B}}\) is injective, so \(|\mathcal{B}| \geq \kappa \). By Theorem 5.2.5 applied to \(\mathcal{L}'\), \(\mathcal{B}\) has an elementary substructure \(\mathcal{A}\) of cardinality \(\kappa \), which satisfies \(\Gamma \) like \(\mathcal{B}\); its reduct to \(\mathcal{L}\) is a model of \(T\) of cardinality \(\kappa \).

So a first-order theory with an infinite model never determines it up to isomorphism: it has models of arbitrarily large cardinality. Example 2.7.5 exhibited a different phenomenon, a non-standard model of the same (countable) cardinality.

02FX Definition 5.4.2

Let \(\kappa \) be an infinite cardinal. A theory \(T\) is \(\kappa \)-categorical if it has a model of cardinality \(\kappa \) and any two models of cardinality \(\kappa \) are isomorphic. It is complete if for every sentence \(\sigma \), either \(\sigma \) is true in all models of \(T\) or \(\neg \sigma \) is. For countable languages this agrees with Definition 2.8.2, by the completeness theorem (Corollary 2.6.2).

02FY Theorem 5.4.3 Łoś–Vaught test

Let \(T\) be a satisfiable theory without finite models that is \(\kappa \)-categorical for some \(\kappa \geq \| \mathcal{L}\| \). Then \(T\) is complete.

Proof â–¼

Suppose \(\sigma \) is a sentence such that neither \(\sigma \) nor \(\neg \sigma \) holds in all models of \(T\). Then \(T \cup \{ \neg \sigma \} \) and \(T \cup \{ \sigma \} \) have models, which are infinite, so by Theorem 5.4.1 they have models \(\mathcal{A}\) and \(\mathcal{B}\) of cardinality \(\kappa \). These are models of \(T\), hence isomorphic, but \(\mathcal{B} \models \sigma \) and \(\mathcal{A} \not\models \sigma \), contradicting Proposition 2.4.11.

02FZ Definition 5.4.4

The theory \(T_{\mathrm{DLO}}\) of dense linear orders without endpoints, in the language of orders, consists of the sentences \(\forall x\, \neg (x {\lt} x)\), \(\forall x\forall y\forall z\, ((x {\lt} y \wedge y {\lt} z) \to x {\lt} z)\), \(\forall x\forall y\, (x {\lt} y \vee x = y \vee y {\lt} x)\), \(\forall x\forall y\, (x {\lt} y \to \exists z\, (x {\lt} z \wedge z {\lt} y))\), \(\forall x\exists y\, (y {\lt} x)\) and \(\forall x\exists y\, (x {\lt} y)\).

Its models are infinite: a model is nonempty and has no largest element. Examples are \((\mathbb {Q}, {\lt})\), \((\mathbb {R}, {\lt})\) and every open interval of \(\mathbb {R}\).

02G0 Theorem 5.4.5 Cantor

Any two countable dense linear orders without endpoints are isomorphic.

Proof â–¼

Let \(A\) and \(B\) be such orders, enumerated as \(a_0, a_1, \ldots \) and \(b_0, b_1, \ldots \) (they are countably infinite). Extension step: if \(p\) is an order-preserving bijection from a finite subset of \(A\) onto a finite subset of \(B\) and \(a \in A\), there is \(b \in B\) such that \(p \cup \{ (a, b)\} \) is still order-preserving and injective. Indeed, if \(a \in \operatorname {dom}p\) take \(b = p(a)\); otherwise the elements of \(\operatorname {dom}p\) smaller than \(a\) have images smaller than those of the elements larger than \(a\), and we need \(b\) strictly between these two finite sets of images, which exists because \(B\) is dense and has no endpoints (and is arbitrary if \(p = \emptyset \)). The same holds with the roles of \(A\) and \(B\) exchanged. Starting from \(p_0 = \emptyset \), let \(p_{2k+1}\) extend \(p_{2k}\) with \(a_k\) in its domain, and \(p_{2k+2}\) extend \(p_{2k+1}\) with \(b_k\) in its image (back and forth). The union of the \(p_k\) is an order-preserving bijection \(A \to B\).

02G1 Corollary 5.4.6

\(T_{\mathrm{DLO}}\) is \(\aleph _0\)-categorical and complete. In particular \((\mathbb {Q}, {\lt}) \equiv (\mathbb {R}, {\lt})\), and a sentence in the language of orders holds in \((\mathbb {Q}, {\lt})\) if and only if it holds in every dense linear order without endpoints.

Proof â–¼

By Theorem 5.4.5, \(T_{\mathrm{DLO}}\) is \(\aleph _0\)-categorical, its language is finite, and it has no finite models; apply Theorem 5.4.3 with \(\kappa = \aleph _0\).

02G2 Counterexample 5.4.7

\(T_{\mathrm{DLO}}\) is not \(2^{\aleph _0}\)-categorical. The orders \((\mathbb {R}, {\lt})\) and \((\mathbb {R}\setminus \{ 0\} , {\lt})\) are dense without endpoints and have cardinality \(2^{\aleph _0}\), but they are not isomorphic: in \(\mathbb {R}\setminus \{ 0\} \) the set of negative numbers is nonempty and bounded above (by \(1\)) but has no least upper bound, since every upper bound \(u\) is positive and \(u/2\) is a smaller one, while in \(\mathbb {R}\) every nonempty subset bounded above has a least upper bound (Theorem 4.8.3); an order isomorphism would preserve this property. By contrast, the theory of algebraically closed fields of a fixed characteristic is \(\kappa \)-categorical for every uncountable \(\kappa \) (such a field is determined by its transcendence degree, which equals its cardinality), hence complete by the Łoś–Vaught test; see [Mar02, Section 2.2] and Chapter 9.
