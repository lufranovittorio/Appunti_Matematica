29.7 The Riemann sphere033J

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

Many statements of complex analysis become simpler once the point \(\infty \) is added to the plane: a pole is a point where a function takes the value \(\infty \), lines are circles through \(\infty \), and the rational functions are exactly the functions that are meromorphic everywhere, \(\infty \) included. The resulting space is a sphere, the first example of a compact Riemann surface; its symmetries, the Möbius transformations, form the group \(\mathrm{PSL}_2(\mathbb {C})\), which reappears in hyperbolic geometry, in the theory of modular forms and in the classification of Riemann surfaces.

29.7.1 The sphere and stereographic projection033K

033L Definition 29.7.1

The Riemann sphere is the one-point compactification \(\hat{\mathbb {C}} = \mathbb {C}\cup \{ \infty \} \) of \(\mathbb {C}\) (Definition 18.9.2). The neighbourhoods of \(\infty \) are the sets containing \(\{ |z| {\gt} R\} \cup \{ \infty \} \) for some \(R\), so \(z_n \to \infty \) in \(\hat{\mathbb {C}}\) if and only if \(|z_n| \to \infty \).

Identifying \(\mathbb {C}\) with \(\mathbb {R}^2\) by \(z = x + iy \mapsto (x, y)\), Example 18.9.4 gives a homeomorphism \(\tau \colon \hat{\mathbb {C}} \to S^2 \subseteq \mathbb {R}^3\),

\[ \tau (z) = \frac{(2x, 2y, |z|^2 - 1)}{|z|^2 + 1} = \frac{(2\operatorname {Re}z, 2\operatorname {Im}z, |z|^2 - 1)}{|z|^2 + 1}, \qquad \tau (\infty ) = N = (0, 0, 1), \]

whose inverse is the stereographic projection from the north pole \(N\): the point \(\tau (z)\) is the second intersection of \(S^2\) with the line through \(N\) and \((x, y, 0)\). The unit circle is mapped to the equator, the unit disc \(\mathbb {D}\) to the southern hemisphere, \(0\) to the south pole and \(\infty \) to the north pole.

033M Proposition 29.7.2

  1. (Chordal distance) For \(z, w \in \mathbb {C}\),

    \[ \| \tau (z) - \tau (w)\| = \frac{2|z - w|}{\sqrt{(1 + |z|^2)(1 + |w|^2)}}, \qquad \| \tau (z) - N\| = \frac{2}{\sqrt{1 + |z|^2}}. \]

  2. The map \(z \mapsto 1/z\), with \(0 \mapsto \infty \mapsto 0\), corresponds to the rotation by the angle \(\pi \) about the \(x_1\)-axis: \(\tau (1/z) = (x_1, -x_2, -x_3)\) if \(\tau (z) = (x_1, x_2, x_3)\). In particular it preserves the chordal distance.

Proof â–¼

(1) Since \(\| \tau (z)\| = \| \tau (w)\| = 1\), \(\| \tau (z) - \tau (w)\| ^2 = 2 - 2\langle \tau (z), \tau (w)\rangle \), and

\[ 2 - 2\langle \tau (z), \tau (w)\rangle = 2\, \frac{(|z|^2 + 1)(|w|^2 + 1) - 4\operatorname {Re}(z\bar w) - (|z|^2 - 1)(|w|^2 - 1)}{(|z|^2 + 1)(|w|^2 + 1)} = \frac{4(|z|^2 + |w|^2 - 2\operatorname {Re}(z\bar w))}{(|z|^2 + 1)(|w|^2 + 1)}, \]

which is \(4|z - w|^2/((1 + |z|^2)(1 + |w|^2))\). The second formula is in Example 18.9.4.

(2) For \(z \neq 0\), \(1/z = \bar z/|z|^2\) has real part \(x/|z|^2\) and imaginary part \(-y/|z|^2\), so

\[ \tau (1/z) = \frac{(2x/|z|^2, -2y/|z|^2, 1/|z|^2 - 1)}{1/|z|^2 + 1} = \frac{(2x, -2y, 1 - |z|^2)}{1 + |z|^2}, \]

which is \((x_1, -x_2, -x_3)\); and \(\tau \) maps \(0\) and \(\infty \) to the south and north poles, which the rotation exchanges.

A generalised circle in \(\hat{\mathbb {C}}\) is either a circle in \(\mathbb {C}\) or a set \(L \cup \{ \infty \} \) with \(L\) a line in \(\mathbb {C}\). Every generalised circle is the set of solutions in \(\hat{\mathbb {C}}\) of an equation

\begin{equation} A|z|^2 + 2\beta x + 2\gamma y + C = 0, \qquad A, \beta , \gamma , C \in \mathbb {R}, \quad \beta ^2 + \gamma ^2 {\gt} AC, \end{equation}
29.1

where \(\infty \) counts as a solution when \(A = 0\): for \(A \neq 0\) this is the circle with centre \(-(\beta + i\gamma )/A\) and radius \(\sqrt{\beta ^2 + \gamma ^2 - AC}/|A|\), and for \(A = 0\) a line. Through any three distinct points of \(\hat{\mathbb {C}}\) passes exactly one generalised circle: the circle through three non-collinear points of \(\mathbb {C}\), the line through three collinear ones, and the line through the two finite points if one of them is \(\infty \).

033O Proposition 29.7.3

The map \(\tau \) induces a bijection between the generalised circles in \(\hat{\mathbb {C}}\) and the circles on \(S^2\), that is, the intersections of \(S^2\) with planes at distance less than \(1\) from the origin. The lines correspond to the circles through \(N\).

Proof â–¼

A plane is \(\{ a x_1 + bx_2 + cx_3 = d\} \) with \(a^2 + b^2 + c^2 = 1\), and it meets \(S^2\) in a circle exactly when \(|d| {\lt} 1\). The point \(\tau (z)\), \(z \in \mathbb {C}\), lies on it if and only if \(2ax + 2by + c(|z|^2 - 1) = d(|z|^2 + 1)\), that is,

\[ (c - d)|z|^2 + 2ax + 2by - (c + d) = 0, \]

an equation of the form 29.1 with \(A = c - d\), \(\beta = a\), \(\gamma = b\), \(C = -(c + d)\), for which \(\beta ^2 + \gamma ^2 - AC = a^2 + b^2 + c^2 - d^2 = 1 - d^2 {\gt} 0\). The point \(N = \tau (\infty )\) lies on the plane if and only if \(c = d\), that is, \(A = 0\). Conversely, given \((A, \beta , \gamma , C)\) with \(\beta ^2 + \gamma ^2 {\gt} AC\), the numbers \(a = s\beta \), \(b = s\gamma \), \(c = s(A - C)/2\), \(d = -s(A + C)/2\) satisfy the equations above, and \(a^2 + b^2 + c^2 - d^2 = s^2(\beta ^2 + \gamma ^2 - AC) {\gt} 0\); choosing \(s {\gt} 0\) with \(a^2 + b^2 + c^2 = 1\) gives a plane with \(1 - d^2 {\gt} 0\) whose circle corresponds to the given generalised circle.

Stereographic projection also preserves angles between curves; equivalently, the round metric of \(S^2\) pulls back to the conformal metric \(4|dz|^2/(1 + |z|^2)^2\) (Chapter 40).

29.7.2 Meromorphic functions on the sphere033P

The behaviour of a function at \(\infty \) is defined through the coordinate \(w = 1/z\).

033Q Definition 29.7.4

Let \(f\) be holomorphic on \(\{ |z| {\gt} R\} \), and \(g(w) = f(1/w)\) on \(0 {\lt} |w| {\lt} 1/R\). Then \(f\) is holomorphic at \(\infty \), has a pole, a zero or an essential singularity at \(\infty \), if \(g\) has the corresponding property at \(0\), and \(\operatorname {ord}_\infty f = \operatorname {ord}_0g\). In terms of the Laurent expansion \(f(z) = \sum _{n \in \mathbb {Z}}c_nz^n\) on \(|z| {\gt} R\) (Theorem 29.5.1), where \(g(w) = \sum _nc_nw^{-n}\): \(f\) is holomorphic at \(\infty \) if \(c_n = 0\) for \(n {\gt} 0\), and has a pole of order \(m\) if \(c_m \neq 0\) and \(c_n = 0\) for \(n {\gt} m\). The residue at \(\infty \) is

\[ \operatorname {Res}_\infty f = -\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int _{|z| = \rho }f(z)\, dz = -c_{-1} \qquad (\rho {\gt} R), \]

the circle traversed counterclockwise. A function \(f\) on \(\hat{\mathbb {C}}\) is meromorphic if it is meromorphic on \(\mathbb {C}\) and holomorphic or has a pole at \(\infty \); it is regarded as a map \(\hat{\mathbb {C}} \to \hat{\mathbb {C}}\) by sending the poles to \(\infty \), which is continuous by Proposition 29.5.3(2).

The residue at \(\infty \) is not the residue of \(g\) at \(0\): it belongs to the differential \(f(z)\, dz = -f(1/w)w^{-2}\, dw\), and \(\operatorname {Res}_\infty f = \operatorname {Res}_0(-g(w)/w^2)\). For instance \(f(z) = 1/z\) is holomorphic at \(\infty \), with a zero of order \(1\) there, but \(\operatorname {Res}_\infty (1/z) = -1\). The minus sign is there because the circle \(|z| = \rho \), traversed counterclockwise, goes around \(\infty \) clockwise.

A rational function \(R = p/q\), with \(p, q\) coprime polynomials and \(q \neq 0\), is meromorphic on \(\hat{\mathbb {C}}\): near \(\infty \), \(R(z) = z^{\deg p - \deg q}h(1/z)\) with \(h\) holomorphic near \(0\) and \(h(0) \neq 0\) (the ratio of the leading coefficients), so

\begin{equation} \operatorname {ord}_\infty \frac pq = \deg q - \deg p. \end{equation}
29.2

The degree of \(R\) is \(\deg R = \max (\deg p, \deg q)\).

033S Theorem 29.7.5

The meromorphic functions on \(\hat{\mathbb {C}}\) are exactly the rational functions. More precisely, if \(f\) is meromorphic on \(\hat{\mathbb {C}}\), with poles \(a_1, \ldots , a_k\) in \(\mathbb {C}\), principal parts \(P_j(z) = \sum _{n=1}^{m_j}c^{(j)}_{-n}(z - a_j)^{-n}\) there, and principal part \(P_\infty (z) = \sum _{n=1}^mc_nz^n\) at \(\infty \) (the positive part of its Laurent expansion at \(\infty \)), then

\[ f = c + P_\infty + P_1 + \cdots + P_k \]

for a constant \(c\). This is the partial fraction decomposition of \(f\), and it is unique.

Proof â–¼

Let \(f = \sum _nc_nz^n\) on \(|z| {\gt} R\). Since \(f\) is not essential at \(\infty \), \(c_n = 0\) for large \(n\), so \(P_\infty \) is a polynomial. The poles of \(f\) in \(\mathbb {C}\) lie in the compact disc \(|z| \leq R\), where \(f\) is holomorphic outside a discrete closed set of poles, so there are finitely many, \(a_1, \ldots , a_k\). Each \(P_j\) is a rational function, holomorphic on \(\hat{\mathbb {C}} \setminus \{ a_j\} \) and vanishing at \(\infty \). The function \(h = f - P_\infty - \sum _jP_j\) has removable singularities at the \(a_j\), so it extends to an entire function. As \(z \to \infty \), \(f(z) - P_\infty (z) = \sum _{n \leq 0}c_nz^n \to c_0\) and \(P_j(z) \to 0\), so \(h\) is bounded outside a large disc, hence bounded on \(\mathbb {C}\), and constant by Liouville’s theorem (Corollary 29.4.7). So \(f\) is rational. Uniqueness: the principal parts, and hence \(c\), are determined by \(f\).

So a meromorphic function on \(\hat{\mathbb {C}}\) is determined, up to an additive constant, by its principal parts, and up to a multiplicative constant by its zeros and poles: if \(f\) and \(g\) have the same zeros and poles with the same orders, \(f/g\) is meromorphic on \(\hat{\mathbb {C}}\) with only removable singularities, so it has no principal parts and is constant by the theorem. This is the simplest instance of the interplay of local data and global holomorphic functions studied for general compact Riemann surfaces in Chapter 59.

033T Proposition 29.7.6

Let \(R\) be a nonconstant rational function of degree \(d\). For every \(w \in \hat{\mathbb {C}}\), the equation \(R(z) = w\) has exactly \(d\) solutions in \(\hat{\mathbb {C}}\), counted with multiplicity; here the multiplicity of a solution \(a\) is \(\operatorname {ord}_a(R - w)\) if \(a\) and \(w\) are finite, and is defined through the coordinates \(1/z\) near \(z = \infty \) and \(1/R\) near \(w = \infty \) otherwise. Consequently \(\sum _{a \in \hat{\mathbb {C}}}\operatorname {ord}_aR = 0\): a rational function has as many zeros as poles on \(\hat{\mathbb {C}}\), counted with multiplicity.

Proof â–¼

Write \(R = p/q\) with \(p\), \(q\) coprime. Let \(w \in \mathbb {C}\). The finite solutions of \(R(z) = w\) are the roots of \(p - wq\): at a root \(a\) of \(p - wq\) we have \(q(a) \neq 0\), as otherwise \(p(a) = 0\) too, and then \(\operatorname {ord}_a(R - w) = \operatorname {ord}_a(p - wq)\). By the fundamental theorem of algebra, these multiplicities add up to \(e = \deg (p - wq) \leq d\). The point \(\infty \) is a solution exactly when \(R - w = (p - wq)/q\) vanishes at \(\infty \), with multiplicity \(\deg q - e\) by 29.2. If \(\deg p {\gt} \deg q\), then \(e = \deg p = d\) and \(R(\infty ) = \infty \neq w\). If \(\deg p \leq \deg q = d\), then either \(e = d\), and \(R - w\) does not vanish at \(\infty \); or \(e {\lt} d\), and \(\infty \) is a solution of multiplicity \(d - e\). In all cases the total is \(d\). For \(w = \infty \), the solutions are the poles: the roots of \(q\), with multiplicities adding up to \(\deg q\), and \(\infty \) if \(\deg p {\gt} \deg q\), with multiplicity \(\deg p - \deg q\); the total is again \(d\). Finally \(\sum _a\operatorname {ord}_aR\) is the number of zeros minus the number of poles, \(d - d = 0\).

033U Proposition 29.7.7

For every rational function \(R\), \(\sum _{a \in \hat{\mathbb {C}}}\operatorname {Res}_aR = 0\), the sum running over \(\infty \) and the poles of \(R\) in \(\mathbb {C}\).

Proof â–¼

Choose \(\rho \) larger than the absolute values of all poles of \(R\) in \(\mathbb {C}\). By the residue theorem in the simply connected set \(\mathbb {C}\) (Theorem 29.5.8), \(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int _{|z| = \rho }R(z)\, dz = \sum _{a \in \mathbb {C}}\operatorname {Res}_aR\), and the left side is \(-\operatorname {Res}_\infty R\) by definition.

033V Example 29.7.8

  1. The residue at \(\infty \) often computes a contour integral at once. For \(n \geq 2\), \(\int _{|z| = 2}\frac{dz}{z^n - 1} = 0\), because \(\frac{1}{z^n - 1} = z^{-n}(1 + z^{-n} + z^{-2n} + \cdots )\) for \(|z| {\gt} 1\) has \(c_{-1} = 0\); computing the \(n\) residues at the roots of unity would be longer. Similarly \(\int _{|z| = 2}\frac{z^{n-1}\, dz}{z^n - 1} = 2\pi i\), which is also \(2\pi i\) times the number of roots of \(z^n - 1\) in the disc, by the argument principle (Theorem 29.5.10).

  2. Applying the proposition to \(R(z) = \frac{1}{z(z - 1)}\): the residues at \(0\) and \(1\) are \(-1\) and \(1\), and indeed \(R = z^{-2}(1 + z^{-1} + \cdots )\) has \(\operatorname {Res}_\infty R = 0\).

  3. A polynomial of degree \(d \geq 1\) is a rational function of degree \(d\) with a single pole, of order \(d\), at \(\infty \); Proposition 29.7.6 says it takes every value \(d\) times in \(\mathbb {C}\), which is the fundamental theorem of algebra.

29.7.3 Möbius transformations033W

033X Definition 29.7.9

For \(A = \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{smallmatrix}\bigr) \in \mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb {C})\), the Möbius transformation \(M_A \colon \hat{\mathbb {C}} \to \hat{\mathbb {C}}\) is

\[ M_A(z) = \frac{az + b}{cz + d}, \]

with \(M_A(-d/c) = \infty \) and \(M_A(\infty ) = a/c\) if \(c \neq 0\), and \(M_A(\infty ) = \infty \) if \(c = 0\).

It is convenient to write points of \(\hat{\mathbb {C}}\) in homogeneous coordinates: the nonzero vector \((z_1, z_2) \in \mathbb {C}^2\) represents \(z_1/z_2 \in \hat{\mathbb {C}}\) (with \(z_1/0 = \infty \)), and two vectors represent the same point if and only if they are proportional. So \(\hat{\mathbb {C}}\) is the set of lines through \(0\) in \(\mathbb {C}^2\), the complex projective line \(\mathbb {P}^1(\mathbb {C})\), and \(M_A\) is the map induced by the linear map \(A\): if \((z_1, z_2)\) represents \(z\), then \(A(z_1, z_2) = (az_1 + bz_2, cz_1 + dz_2)\) represents \(M_A(z)\). (The cases with \(\infty \) in the definition are exactly what this description gives.)

033Y Proposition 29.7.10

  1. \(M_AM_B = M_{AB}\), and \(M_A = \mathrm{id}\) if and only if \(A\) is a scalar matrix. So the Möbius transformations form a group, isomorphic to \(\mathrm{PGL}_2(\mathbb {C}) = \mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb {C})/\mathbb {C}^*\), and also to \(\mathrm{PSL}_2(\mathbb {C}) = \mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb {C})/\{ \pm 1\} \).

  2. Each \(M_A\) is a homeomorphism of \(\hat{\mathbb {C}}\) and a rational function of degree \(1\); its derivative \(\det A/(cz + d)^2\) never vanishes on \(\mathbb {C}\setminus \{ -d/c\} \). Conversely every rational function of degree \(1\), and every bijective meromorphic function \(\hat{\mathbb {C}} \to \hat{\mathbb {C}}\), is a Möbius transformation.

  3. The Möbius transformations are generated by the translations \(z \mapsto z + b\), the maps \(z \mapsto az\) (\(a \neq 0\)) and the inversion \(z \mapsto 1/z\).

  4. Möbius transformations map generalised circles to generalised circles.

Proof â–¼

(1) The first statement is the description in homogeneous coordinates: \(AB\) induces the composite of the maps induced by \(B\) and \(A\). If \(M_A = \mathrm{id}\), every line in \(\mathbb {C}^2\) is mapped to itself, so every nonzero vector is an eigenvector of \(A\); then \(e_1\), \(e_2\) and \(e_1 + e_2\) are eigenvectors, which forces the two eigenvalues for \(e_1\), \(e_2\) to agree, and \(A\) is scalar. So \(A \mapsto M_A\) induces an isomorphism from \(\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb {C})/\mathbb {C}^*\) onto the group of Möbius transformations. Every \(A\) is a scalar multiple of a matrix of determinant \(1\) (divide by a square root of \(\det A\)), and a scalar matrix of determinant \(1\) is \(\pm 1\), which gives the second description.

(2) \(M_A\) is bijective with inverse \(M_{A^{-1}}\). It is continuous on \(\hat{\mathbb {C}}\): away from \(-d/c\) and \(\infty \) this is clear, \(|M_A(z)| \to \infty \) as \(z \to -d/c\) since the numerator does not vanish there (\(a(-d/c) + b = -\det A/c\)), and \(M_A(z) \to a/c\) (or \(\infty \) if \(c = 0\)) as \(z \to \infty \). A continuous bijection of the compact Hausdorff space \(\hat{\mathbb {C}}\) is a homeomorphism. The polynomials \(az + b\) and \(cz + d\) are coprime, since \(ad - bc \neq 0\), so \(M_A\) has degree \(1\), and the formula for the derivative is the quotient rule. Conversely, a rational function \((az + b)/(cz + d)\) of degree \(1\) in lowest terms has \(ad - bc \neq 0\), since otherwise the numerator and denominator would be proportional. If \(f\) is a meromorphic bijection of \(\hat{\mathbb {C}}\), it is rational (Theorem 29.7.5) of some degree \(d \geq 1\), and by Proposition 29.7.6 each value has a single preimage, of multiplicity \(d\). If \(d \geq 2\), then \(f^{\prime }\) would vanish at every point of \(\mathbb {C}\) that is not a pole and not mapped to \(\infty \), which is impossible for a nonconstant function. So \(d = 1\).

(3) If \(c = 0\), \(M_A(z) = (a/d)z + b/d\). If \(c \neq 0\), \(M_A(z) = \frac ac - \frac{\det A}{c(cz + d)}\), the composite of \(z \mapsto cz + d\), \(z \mapsto 1/z\), multiplication by \(-\det A/c\) and translation by \(a/c\).

(4) By (3) it suffices to check the generators. Translations and the maps \(z \mapsto az\) are similarities of the plane, which map circles to circles and lines to lines, and fix \(\infty \). The inversion corresponds under \(\tau \) to a rotation of \(S^2\) (Proposition 29.7.2(2)), which maps plane sections of \(S^2\) to plane sections; by Proposition 29.7.3 it maps generalised circles to generalised circles.

The proof of (4) by the sphere avoids a computation; directly, substituting \(z = 1/u\) in 29.1 and multiplying by \(|u|^2\) gives \(A + 2\beta \operatorname {Re}u - 2\gamma \operatorname {Im}u + C|u|^2 = 0\), again of the same form. Under inversion, circles through \(0\) become lines, and lines not through \(0\) become circles through \(0\).

033Z Theorem 29.7.11

Given distinct points \(z_1, z_2, z_3 \in \hat{\mathbb {C}}\) and distinct points \(w_1, w_2, w_3 \in \hat{\mathbb {C}}\), there is exactly one Möbius transformation \(M\) with \(M(z_i) = w_i\) for \(i = 1, 2, 3\).

Proof â–¼

If \(z_1, z_2, z_3 \in \mathbb {C}\), the map

\[ S(z) = \frac{(z - z_1)(z_2 - z_3)}{(z - z_3)(z_2 - z_1)} \]

is Möbius, with \(S(z_1) = 0\), \(S(z_2) = 1\), \(S(z_3) = \infty \). If one of the \(z_i\) is \(\infty \), delete the two factors containing it: \(S(z) = \frac{z_2 - z_3}{z - z_3}\), \(\frac{z - z_1}{z - z_3}\) or \(\frac{z - z_1}{z_2 - z_1}\) for \(z_1\), \(z_2\) or \(z_3 = \infty \). Doing the same for the \(w_i\) gives \(T\), and \(M = T^{-1}S\) has the required property. For uniqueness it suffices to show that a Möbius map fixing \(0\), \(1\), \(\infty \) is the identity: from \(M(\infty ) = \infty \), \(c = 0\), so \(M(z) = \alpha z + \beta \); then \(M(0) = 0\) gives \(\beta = 0\) and \(M(1) = 1\) gives \(\alpha = 1\).

0340 Definition 29.7.12

For distinct \(z_1, z_2, z_3 \in \hat{\mathbb {C}}\) and \(z \in \hat{\mathbb {C}}\), the cross-ratio \((z, z_1, z_2, z_3)\) is \(S(z)\), for the unique Möbius \(S\) with \(S(z_1) = 0\), \(S(z_2) = 1\), \(S(z_3) = \infty \); for finite points

\[ (z, z_1, z_2, z_3) = \frac{(z - z_1)(z_2 - z_3)}{(z - z_3)(z_2 - z_1)}. \]
0341 Corollary 29.7.13

  1. For every Möbius transformation \(M\), \((Mz, Mz_1, Mz_2, Mz_3) = (z, z_1, z_2, z_3)\).

  2. Four distinct points \(z, z_1, z_2, z_3\) lie on a generalised circle if and only if \((z, z_1, z_2, z_3)\) is real.

Proof â–¼

(1) \(SM^{-1}\) sends \(Mz_1\), \(Mz_2\), \(Mz_3\) to \(0\), \(1\), \(\infty \), so it is the map defining the left side, and its value at \(Mz\) is \(S(z)\). (2) Let \(C\) be the generalised circle through \(z_1\), \(z_2\), \(z_3\). By Proposition 29.7.10(4), applied to \(S\) and \(S^{-1}\), \(S\) maps \(C\) onto the generalised circle through \(0\), \(1\), \(\infty \), which is \(\mathbb {R}\cup \{ \infty \} \). So \(z \in C\) if and only if \(S(z) \in \mathbb {R}\cup \{ \infty \} \), and \(S(z) \neq \infty \) since \(z \neq z_3\).

29.7.4 Fixed points and conjugacy classes0342

A point \(z = z_1/z_2\) is fixed by \(M_A\) if and only if \((z_1, z_2)\) is an eigenvector of \(A\). Since \(A\) has one or two eigenlines unless it is scalar, a Möbius transformation other than the identity has one or two fixed points in \(\hat{\mathbb {C}}\). Normalising \(\det A = 1\), the trace \(\operatorname {tr}A\) is determined by \(M_A\) up to sign, so \((\operatorname {tr}A)^2\) is an invariant of \(M_A\), unchanged under conjugation \(M \mapsto NMN^{-1}\).

0343 Theorem 29.7.14

Let \(M = M_A \neq \mathrm{id}\) with \(\det A = 1\), and \(t = \operatorname {tr}A\).

  1. If \(t^2 = 4\), \(M\) has exactly one fixed point and is conjugate to the translation \(z \mapsto z + 1\); \(M\) is called parabolic.

  2. If \(t^2 \neq 4\), \(M\) has exactly two fixed points and is conjugate to \(z \mapsto kz\) for some \(k \neq 0, 1\), determined up to \(k \leftrightarrow k^{-1}\) by \(k + 2 + k^{-1} = t^2\). Then \(M\) is called elliptic if \(|k| = 1\), equivalently \(t \in (-2, 2)\); hyperbolic if \(k {\gt} 0\), equivalently \(t \in \mathbb {R}\) with \(|t| {\gt} 2\); and loxodromic otherwise, equivalently \(t \notin \mathbb {R}\).

Two Möbius transformations other than the identity are conjugate if and only if they have the same invariant \(t^2\).

Proof â–¼

Conjugating \(M_A\) by \(M_P\) replaces \(A\) by \(PAP^{-1}\). By the Jordan normal form (Theorem 11.8.4), a non-scalar \(A \in \mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb {C})\) is conjugate either to \(\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} \lambda & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda ^{-1} \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\) with \(\lambda \neq \lambda ^{-1}\), or to \(\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} \lambda & 1 \\ 0 & \lambda \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\) with \(\lambda ^2 = 1\); a diagonal matrix with \(\lambda = \lambda ^{-1}\) would be scalar. In the second case \(t = 2\lambda = \pm 2\), \(M\) is conjugate to \(z \mapsto z \pm 1\), and \(z \mapsto z - 1\) is conjugate to \(z \mapsto z + 1\) by \(z \mapsto -z\); the only fixed point of a translation is \(\infty \). In the first case \(M\) is conjugate to \(z \mapsto kz\) with \(k = \lambda ^2 \neq 1\), which fixes \(0\) and \(\infty \), and \(t^2 = (\lambda + \lambda ^{-1})^2 = k + 2 + k^{-1}\); here \(t^2 \neq 4\) since \(k \neq 1\). Exchanging the eigenvectors replaces \(k\) by \(k^{-1}\). The eigenvalues \(\lambda \), \(\lambda ^{-1}\) are the roots of \(X^2 - tX + 1\), and \(k = \lambda ^2\). If \(t \in (-2, 2)\), the roots are non-real and conjugate with product \(1\), so \(|\lambda | = 1\) and \(|k| = 1\); conversely \(|\lambda | = 1\) gives \(t = \lambda + \bar\lambda = 2\operatorname {Re}\lambda \in [-2, 2]\), and \(t \neq \pm 2\) as \(t^2 \neq 4\). If \(t\) is real with \(|t| {\gt} 2\), the roots are real, so \(k {\gt} 0\); conversely \(k {\gt} 0\) means \(\lambda \in \mathbb {R}\), so \(t = \lambda + \lambda ^{-1}\) is real, and \(|t| {\gt} 2\) since \(\lambda \neq \pm 1\). As \(t^2 \neq 4\), a real \(t\) falls in one of these two cases, so the remaining, loxodromic, case is \(t \notin \mathbb {R}\). Finally, transformations with the same \(t^2\) are conjugate, since both are conjugate to the same normal form (\(z \mapsto z + 1\), or \(z \mapsto kz\) with \(k\) determined up to inversion, and \(z \mapsto k^{-1}z\) is conjugate to \(z \mapsto kz\) by \(z \mapsto 1/z\)).

The names describe the dynamics. A hyperbolic map \(z \mapsto kz\), \(k {\gt} 1\), pushes every point away from the repelling fixed point \(0\) towards the attracting fixed point \(\infty \) along rays; an elliptic map \(z \mapsto e^{i\theta }z\) rotates around its two fixed points, and corresponds to a rotation of the sphere about the axis through them; a loxodromic map spirals from one fixed point to the other; and a parabolic map moves points along circles tangent at the single fixed point. For example \(z \mapsto 1/z\), with matrix \(i\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\) of determinant \(1\) and trace \(0\), is elliptic of order \(2\) with fixed points \(\pm 1\), in accordance with Proposition 29.7.2(2).

29.7.5 The disc, the half-plane and the sphere0344

Three subgroups of the Möbius group correspond to the three simply connected Riemann surfaces of the uniformisation theorem (Chapter 44): the sphere, the plane and the disc.

0345 Proposition 29.7.15

Let \(\mathbb {H} = \{ \operatorname {Im}z {\gt} 0\} \).

  1. The Cayley transform \(\kappa (z) = \frac{z - i}{z + i}\) maps \(\mathbb {H}\) biholomorphically onto \(\mathbb {D}\), and \(\mathbb {R}\cup \{ \infty \} \) onto the unit circle.

  2. For \(A \in \mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb {R})\), \(\operatorname {Im}M_A(z) = \operatorname {Im}z/|cz + d|^2\), so \(M_A\) maps \(\mathbb {H}\) onto itself.

  3. Every biholomorphic self-map of \(\mathbb {H}\) is \(M_A\) for some \(A \in \mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb {R})\), unique up to sign; so \(\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb {H}) \cong \mathrm{PSL}_2(\mathbb {R})\).

Proof â–¼

(1) \(|\kappa (z)| {\lt} 1\) if and only if \(|z - i| {\lt} |z + i|\), that is, \(z\) is closer to \(i\) than to \(-i\), that is, \(\operatorname {Im}z {\gt} 0\); similarly \(|\kappa (z)| = 1\) exactly on \(\mathbb {R}\), and \(\kappa (\infty ) = 1\). Since \(\kappa \) is a bijection of \(\hat{\mathbb {C}}\), it maps \(\mathbb {H}\) onto \(\mathbb {D}\), and it is holomorphic with holomorphic inverse \(M_{A^{-1}}\).

(2) \((az + b)(c\bar z + d) = ac|z|^2 + adz + bc\bar z + bd\) has imaginary part \((ad - bc)\operatorname {Im}z = \operatorname {Im}z\), and \(\operatorname {Im}M_A(z) = \operatorname {Im}\bigl((az + b)(c\bar z + d)\bigr)/|cz + d|^2\). So \(M_A\) maps \(\mathbb {H}\) into itself, and so does its inverse \(M_{A^{-1}}\), \(A^{-1} \in \mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb {R})\).

(3) Let \(f\) be a biholomorphic self-map of \(\mathbb {H}\). Then \(\kappa f\kappa ^{-1}\) is a biholomorphic self-map of \(\mathbb {D}\), hence a Möbius transformation by Proposition 29.6.2, and so \(f = M\) is Möbius. As a homeomorphism of \(\hat{\mathbb {C}}\) mapping \(\mathbb {H}\) onto itself, \(M\) maps the boundary \(\mathbb {R}\cup \{ \infty \} \) of \(\mathbb {H}\) onto itself. Choose three distinct real points \(z_i\); the \(w_i = M(z_i)\) lie in \(\mathbb {R}\cup \{ \infty \} \), and in the proof of Theorem 29.7.11 the maps \(S\) and \(T\) have real coefficients, so \(M = T^{-1}S = M_A\) with \(A \in \mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb {R})\). By the computation in (2), \(\operatorname {Im}M_A(z) = \det A\operatorname {Im}z/|cz + d|^2\), so \(\det A {\gt} 0\), and dividing \(A\) by \(\sqrt{\det A}\) gives \(A \in \mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb {R})\). Finally \(M_A = M_B\) with \(A, B \in \mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb {R})\) forces \(A = \pm B\) by Proposition 29.7.10(1).

So the three groups are: all of \(\mathrm{PSL}_2(\mathbb {C})\) for the sphere (Proposition 29.7.10(2)); the affine maps \(z \mapsto az + b\), the Möbius maps fixing \(\infty \), for the plane (Exercise 29.8.2); and \(\mathrm{PSL}_2(\mathbb {R}) \cong \mathrm{PSU}(1, 1)\) for the half-plane or the disc. The subgroup \(\mathrm{PSL}_2(\mathbb {Z})\) acting on \(\mathbb {H}\) gives the moduli space of elliptic curves (Chapter 51).

The rotations of the sphere form a fourth, compact, subgroup. In homogeneous coordinates the chordal distance of Proposition 29.7.2 reads

\[ \| \tau (z) - \tau (w)\| = \frac{2|z_1w_2 - z_2w_1|}{\| (z_1, z_2)\| \, \| (w_1, w_2)\| } \qquad (z = z_1/z_2, \ w = w_1/w_2), \]

as one sees by taking \(z_2 = w_2 = 1\) (both sides are unchanged by rescaling either vector, and the case of \(\infty \) follows by continuity). The numerator is \(|\det |\) of the matrix with columns \((z_1, z_2)\) and \((w_1, w_2)\), so a matrix \(A \in \mathrm{SU}(2)\), which preserves determinants and norms, gives a Möbius transformation \(M_A\) preserving the chordal distance, an isometry of \(S^2\). These isometries are rotations, and every rotation arises in this way; this is the double covering \(\mathrm{SU}(2) \to \mathrm{SO}(3)\) of Chapter 34. The matrices in \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\) are those of the form \(\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} a & -\bar b \\ b & \bar a \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\) with \(|a|^2 + |b|^2 = 1\); for instance \(z \mapsto e^{i\theta }z\) is the rotation by \(\theta \) about the vertical axis.

0346 Remark 29.7.16

  1. Riemann surfaces. With the two charts \(z\) and \(1/z\), \(\hat{\mathbb {C}}\) is a compact Riemann surface, biholomorphic to the projective line \(\mathbb {C}\mathbb {P}^1\); meromorphic functions on \(\hat{\mathbb {C}}\) are the holomorphic maps \(\hat{\mathbb {C}} \to \hat{\mathbb {C}}\) other than the constant \(\infty \), and the Möbius transformations are its automorphisms (Chapter 44). It is the only compact Riemann surface of genus \(0\).

  2. Riemann–Hurwitz. A rational function of degree \(d\) has \(2d - 2\) critical points on \(\hat{\mathbb {C}}\), counted with multiplicity; for a polynomial of degree \(d\) these are the \(d - 1\) zeros of \(p^{\prime }\) and the point \(\infty \), of multiplicity \(d - 1\). This is the Riemann–Hurwitz formula for maps \(\hat{\mathbb {C}} \to \hat{\mathbb {C}}\) [For81].

  3. Iteration. Iterating a rational function of degree \(d \geq 2\) on \(\hat{\mathbb {C}}\) is the subject of complex dynamics, with its Julia and Fatou sets; the family \(z \mapsto z^2 + c\) gives the Mandelbrot set. For Möbius transformations iteration is simple, by Theorem 29.7.14: the iterates of \(z \mapsto kz\) are \(z \mapsto k^nz\), and those of \(z \mapsto z + 1\) are \(z \mapsto z + n\).
