12.8 Weyl’s theorem02QL

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

For \(\mathfrak {sl}_2\), complete reducibility was proved with the Casimir operator \(C = ef + fe + \frac12h^2\), which commutes with the action and separates the irreducible representations (Theorem 12.6.3). The same element exists for every semisimple Lie algebra; it is built from a nondegenerate invariant form, and it gives complete reducibility in general. In this section \(\mathfrak {g}\) is semisimple and \(k\) has characteristic \(0\).

02QM Lemma 12.8.1 Casimir element

Let \(\varphi \colon \mathfrak {g} \to \mathfrak {gl}(V)\) be a faithful (injective) representation. Then the trace form \(\beta = \beta _\varphi \) is nondegenerate. Let \(x_1, \ldots , x_n\) be a basis of \(\mathfrak {g}\) and \(y_1, \ldots , y_n\) the dual basis, \(\beta (x_i, y_j) = \delta _{ij}\), and put

\[ c_\varphi = \sum _{i=1}^n\varphi (x_i)\varphi (y_i) \in \operatorname {End}(V). \]

Then \(c_\varphi \) commutes with \(\varphi (x)\) for all \(x \in \mathfrak {g}\), and \(\operatorname {tr}c_\varphi = \dim \mathfrak {g}\).

Proof â–¼

The radical \(S\) of \(\beta \) is an ideal (Lemma 12.7.2(3)), and \(\operatorname {tr}(\varphi (x)\varphi (y)) = 0\) for \(x, y \in S\); by Cartan’s criterion (Theorem 12.7.6) \(\varphi (S) \cong S\) is solvable, so \(S = 0\). For \(x \in \mathfrak {g}\) write \([x, x_i] = \sum _ja_{ij}x_j\) and \([x, y_i] = \sum _jb_{ij}y_j\). By invariance, \(a_{il} = \beta ([x, x_i], y_l) = -\beta ([x_i, x], y_l) = -\beta (x_i, [x, y_l]) = -b_{li}\). Using \([X, YZ] = [X, Y]Z + Y[X, Z]\) in \(\operatorname {End}(V)\),

\[ [\varphi (x), c_\varphi ] = \sum _i\bigl([\varphi (x), \varphi (x_i)]\varphi (y_i) + \varphi (x_i)[\varphi (x), \varphi (y_i)]\bigr) = \sum _{i,j}a_{ij}\varphi (x_j)\varphi (y_i) + \sum _{i,j}b_{ij}\varphi (x_i)\varphi (y_j), \]

and renaming the indices in the second sum, it becomes \(\sum _{i,j}b_{ji}\varphi (x_j)\varphi (y_i) = -\sum _{i,j}a_{ij}\varphi (x_j)\varphi (y_i)\). Finally \(\operatorname {tr}c_\varphi = \sum _i\beta (x_i, y_i) = n\).

If \(\varphi \) is not faithful, its kernel is an ideal, so \(\mathfrak {g} = \ker \varphi \oplus \mathfrak {g}^{\prime }\) where \(\mathfrak {g}^{\prime }\) is the sum of the other simple ideals (Theorem 12.7.10); \(\varphi |_{\mathfrak {g}^{\prime }}\) is faithful with the same image, and we define \(c_\varphi = c_{\varphi |\mathfrak {g}^{\prime }}\). It commutes with \(\varphi (\mathfrak {g})\), and its trace is \(\dim \varphi (\mathfrak {g})\). For \(\mathfrak {sl}_2\) acting on \(k^2\) the trace form is \(\operatorname {tr}(xy)\), the dual basis of \(e, f, h\) is \(f, e, \frac12h\), and \(c_\varphi = ef + fe + \frac12h^2\) is the Casimir operator of Theorem 12.6.3.

02QN Theorem 12.8.2 Weyl

Every finite-dimensional representation of a semisimple Lie algebra over a field of characteristic \(0\) is completely reducible.

Proof â–¼

It suffices to show that every subrepresentation \(W\) of a representation \(V\) has a complementary subrepresentation; complete reducibility then follows by induction on \(\dim V\). First note that \(\mathfrak {g}\) acts by \(0\) on every one-dimensional representation, since \(\mathfrak {gl}_1\) is abelian and \(\mathfrak {g} = [\mathfrak {g}, \mathfrak {g}]\).

Step 1: \(W\) of codimension \(1\). We argue by induction on \(\dim V\). By the remark just made, \(\mathfrak {g}V \subseteq W\). If \(\varphi = 0\), any complement will do. Suppose first that \(W\) is irreducible, and let \(c = c_\varphi \). It maps \(V\) into \(W\), being a sum of products of elements of \(\varphi (\mathfrak {g})\), so \(\operatorname {tr}(c|_W) = \operatorname {tr}c = \dim \varphi (\mathfrak {g}) \neq 0\) (Lemma 12.7.2(4)). Since \(c\) commutes with \(\mathfrak {g}\), \(\ker (c|_W)\) is a subrepresentation of \(W\), not equal to \(W\), hence \(0\). So \(\ker c \cap W = 0\), while \(\dim \ker c \geq \dim V - \dim W = 1\) because \(c(V) \subseteq W\); hence \(V = W \oplus \ker c\), and \(\ker c\) is a subrepresentation. If \(W\) is not irreducible, let \(0 \neq W^{\prime } \subsetneq W\) be a subrepresentation. By induction, applied to \(W/W^{\prime } \subseteq V/W^{\prime }\), there is a subrepresentation \(\widetilde{W} \supseteq W^{\prime }\) with \(V/W^{\prime } = W/W^{\prime } \oplus \widetilde{W}/W^{\prime }\) and \(\dim \widetilde{W}/W^{\prime } = 1\). By induction again, applied to \(W^{\prime } \subseteq \widetilde{W}\) (of codimension \(1\), and \(\dim \widetilde{W} {\lt} \dim V\)), \(\widetilde{W} = W^{\prime } \oplus X\) with \(X\) a subrepresentation of dimension \(1\). Then \(X \cap W \subseteq \widetilde{W} \cap W = W^{\prime }\), so \(X \cap W = X \cap W^{\prime } = 0\), and \(V = W \oplus X\).

Step 2: general \(W \neq 0\). The space \(\operatorname {Hom}_k(V, W)\) is a representation with \((x \cdot f)(v) = xf(v) - f(xv)\) (it is \(V^* \otimes W\), Definition 12.4.3). Let \(\mathcal{V}\) be the subspace of maps \(f\) whose restriction to \(W\) is a scalar multiple \(\lambda _f\mathrm{id}_W\), and \(\mathcal{W} \subseteq \mathcal{V}\) those with \(f|_W = 0\). For \(f \in \mathcal{V}\) and \(w \in W\), \((x \cdot f)(w) = \lambda _fxw - \lambda _fxw = 0\), so \(\mathfrak {g}\mathcal{V} \subseteq \mathcal{W}\); in particular both are subrepresentations, and \(\mathcal{W}\) has codimension \(1\) in \(\mathcal{V}\), because \(f \mapsto \lambda _f\) is onto (extend \(\mathrm{id}_W\) by a linear projection). By Step 1, \(\mathcal{V} = \mathcal{W} \oplus kf\) with \(kf\) a subrepresentation, on which \(\mathfrak {g}\) acts by \(0\). We may take \(\lambda _f = 1\). Then \(x \cdot f = 0\) says \(xf(v) = f(xv)\): \(f \colon V \to W\) is a homomorphism of representations which is the identity on \(W\), and \(\ker f\) is a subrepresentation complementary to \(W\).

02QO Counterexample 12.8.3

Semisimplicity is needed. The one-dimensional Lie algebra \(k\) acts on \(k^2\) by \(t \mapsto \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & t \\ 0 & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\); the line \(ke_1\) is a subrepresentation, and a complementary one would be a second line killed by the nilpotent matrix, whose kernel is \(ke_1\). The same happens for the adjoint representation of the solvable Lie algebra with \([x, y] = y\): the ideal \(ky\) has no complementary ideal, as a complement would be a line \(k(x + cy)\) with \([y, x + cy] = -y\) in it.

02QP Corollary 12.8.4 Preservation of the Jordan decomposition

Let \(k\) be algebraically closed.

  1. If \(\mathfrak {g} \subseteq \mathfrak {gl}(V)\) is a semisimple Lie subalgebra and \(x \in \mathfrak {g}\), the semisimple and nilpotent parts \(s\), \(n\) of the endomorphism \(x\) lie in \(\mathfrak {g}\), and they are the parts \(x_s\), \(x_n\) of the abstract Jordan decomposition.

  2. If \(\varphi \colon \mathfrak {g} \to \mathfrak {gl}(V)\) is a representation of a semisimple Lie algebra, then \(\varphi (x_s)\) and \(\varphi (x_n)\) are the semisimple and nilpotent parts of \(\varphi (x)\). In particular a semisimple element acts diagonalisably in every finite-dimensional representation.

Proof â–¼

(1) By Lemma 12.7.4, \(\operatorname {ad}s\) and \(\operatorname {ad}n\) are polynomials without constant term in \(\operatorname {ad}x\), which maps \(\mathfrak {g}\) into itself; so \(s\) and \(n\) lie in the normaliser \(N = \{ y \in \mathfrak {gl}(V) : [y, \mathfrak {g}] \subseteq \mathfrak {g}\} \), a Lie subalgebra. For each subrepresentation \(W \subseteq V\) let \(L_W = \{ y \in \mathfrak {gl}(V) : y(W) \subseteq W,\ \operatorname {tr}(y|_W) = 0\} \), also a Lie subalgebra. It contains \(\mathfrak {g} = [\mathfrak {g}, \mathfrak {g}]\), as the restrictions of commutators are commutators, and it contains \(s\) and \(n\): these are polynomials in \(x\), so they preserve \(W\), \(n|_W\) is nilpotent, so of trace \(0\), and \(s|_W = x|_W - n|_W\). Let \(\mathfrak {g}^{\prime } = N \cap \bigcap _WL_W\); it contains \(\mathfrak {g}\), \(s\) and \(n\), and \(\mathfrak {g}\) is an ideal of \(\mathfrak {g}^{\prime }\). By Weyl’s theorem, applied to the representation \(\operatorname {ad}\) of \(\mathfrak {g}\) on \(\mathfrak {g}^{\prime }\), \(\mathfrak {g}^{\prime } = \mathfrak {g} \oplus M\) with \([\mathfrak {g}, M] \subseteq M\); also \([\mathfrak {g}, M] \subseteq \mathfrak {g}\), so \([\mathfrak {g}, M] = 0\). Let \(y \in M\) and let \(W \subseteq V\) be an irreducible subrepresentation. Then \(y|_W\) commutes with \(\mathfrak {g}\), so it is a scalar by Schur’s lemma (Lemma 12.2.3, whose proof applies to Lie algebras), and it has trace \(0\), so \(y|_W = 0\). By Weyl’s theorem \(V\) is a sum of irreducible subrepresentations, so \(y = 0\). Hence \(\mathfrak {g}^{\prime } = \mathfrak {g}\) contains \(s\) and \(n\). Now \(\operatorname {ad}_{\mathfrak {g}}s\) is diagonalisable, as the restriction of the diagonalisable \(\operatorname {ad}s\) to an invariant subspace, \(\operatorname {ad}_{\mathfrak {g}}n\) is nilpotent, and \([s, n] = 0\); by uniqueness in Proposition 12.7.12, \(s = x_s\) and \(n = x_n\).

(2) The image \(\varphi (\mathfrak {g}) \cong \mathfrak {g}/\ker \varphi \) is semisimple (Theorem 12.7.10). \(\mathfrak {g}\) has a basis of eigenvectors of \(\operatorname {ad}x_s\), and \(\varphi \) maps it onto a spanning set of \(\varphi (\mathfrak {g})\) consisting of eigenvectors of \(\operatorname {ad}\varphi (x_s)\); so \(\operatorname {ad}\varphi (x_s)\) is diagonalisable on \(\varphi (\mathfrak {g})\). Similarly \((\operatorname {ad}\varphi (x_n))^N\varphi (y) = \varphi ((\operatorname {ad}x_n)^Ny) = 0\) for large \(N\), and \([\varphi (x_s), \varphi (x_n)] = 0\). By uniqueness, \(\varphi (x) = \varphi (x_s) + \varphi (x_n)\) is the abstract Jordan decomposition in \(\varphi (\mathfrak {g})\), and by (1) it is the Jordan decomposition of the endomorphism \(\varphi (x)\).
