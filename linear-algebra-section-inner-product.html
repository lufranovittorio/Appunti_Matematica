10.9 Inner product spaces and the spectral theorem00JI

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

00JJ Definition 10.9.1

Let \(\mathbb {K} = \mathbb {R}\) or \(\mathbb {C}\). An inner product on a \(\mathbb {K}\)-vector space \(V\) is a map \(\langle -, - \rangle \colon V \times V \to \mathbb {K}\), linear in the first variable, with \(\langle w, v \rangle = \overline{\langle v, w \rangle }\) and \(\langle v, v \rangle {\gt} 0\) for \(v \neq 0\). (Over \(\mathbb {C}\) it is then conjugate-linear in the second variable; such a form is also called a Hermitian inner product.) We write \(\| v\| = \sqrt{\langle v, v \rangle }\). A family is orthonormal if \(\langle v_i, v_j \rangle = \delta _{ij}\).

00JK Proposition 10.9.2

\(|\langle v, w \rangle | \leq \| v\| \| w\| \), and \(\| v + w\| \leq \| v\| + \| w\| \).

Proof â–¼

If \(w = 0\) this is clear. Otherwise let \(u = v - \frac{\langle v, w \rangle }{\| w\| ^2}w\); then \(\langle u, w \rangle = 0\) and \(0 \leq \| u\| ^2 = \| v\| ^2 - |\langle v, w \rangle |^2/\| w\| ^2\). For the triangle inequality, \(\| v + w\| ^2 = \| v\| ^2 + 2\operatorname {Re}\langle v, w \rangle + \| w\| ^2 \leq (\| v\| + \| w\| )^2\).

00JL Theorem 10.9.3 Gram–Schmidt

If \(v_1, v_2, \ldots \) is a finite or countable linearly independent family, there is an orthonormal family \(u_1, u_2, \ldots \) with \(\operatorname {span}(u_1, \ldots , u_j) = \operatorname {span}(v_1, \ldots , v_j)\) for all \(j\). Every finite-dimensional inner product space has an orthonormal basis, and for every finite-dimensional subspace \(W\), \(V = W \oplus W^\perp \), where \(W^\perp = \{ v : \langle v, w \rangle = 0 \text{ for all } w \in W\} \).

Proof â–¼

Define recursively \(u_j' = v_j - \sum _{i{\lt}j}\langle v_j, u_i \rangle u_i\), which is nonzero since \(v_j \notin \operatorname {span}(u_1, \ldots , u_{j-1})\), and \(u_j = u_j'/\| u_j'\| \). For the last claim, with an orthonormal basis \((u_i)\) of \(W\), write \(v = \sum _i \langle v, u_i \rangle u_i + (v - \sum _i \langle v, u_i\rangle u_i)\); the second term lies in \(W^\perp \), and \(W \cap W^\perp = 0\) by positivity.

00JM Definition 10.9.4

Let \(V\) be a finite-dimensional inner product space and \(f \colon V \to V\) linear. The adjoint \(f^\dagger \) is the unique linear map with \(\langle f(v), w \rangle = \langle v, f^\dagger (w) \rangle \) for all \(v, w\). The map \(f\) is self-adjoint if \(f^\dagger = f\), normal if \(ff^\dagger = f^\dagger f\), and unitary (or orthogonal, if \(\mathbb {K} = \mathbb {R}\)) if \(f^\dagger f = \mathrm{id}\). The unitary operators of \(\mathbb {C}^n\) form the unitary group \(\mathrm{U}(n)\), and the orthogonal operators of \(\mathbb {R}^n\) the orthogonal group \(\mathrm{O}(n)\).

00JN Lemma 10.9.5

The adjoint exists and is unique. In an orthonormal basis its matrix is the conjugate transpose of the matrix of \(f\). If \(W\) is \(f\)-invariant then \(W^\perp \) is \(f^\dagger \)-invariant.

Proof â–¼

With an orthonormal basis \((u_i)\), the map \(f^\dagger (w) = \sum _i \langle w, f(u_i) \rangle u_i\) works, and a linear map is determined by the values \(\langle v, f^\dagger (w) \rangle \). Its matrix entries are \(\langle f^\dagger (u_j), u_i \rangle = \overline{\langle f(u_i), u_j\rangle }\). If \(f(W) \subseteq W\) and \(v \in W^\perp \), then \(\langle f^\dagger (v), w \rangle = \langle v, f(w) \rangle = 0\) for \(w \in W\).

00JO Theorem 10.9.6 Spectral theorem

Let \(V\) be a finite-dimensional inner product space.

  1. If \(\mathbb {K} = \mathbb {C}\) and \(f\) is normal, then \(V\) has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of \(f\).

  2. If \(f\) is self-adjoint (over \(\mathbb {R}\) or \(\mathbb {C}\)), then all its eigenvalues are real and \(V\) has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of \(f\).

Proof â–¼

For self-adjoint \(f\) and an eigenvector \(v\) with eigenvalue \(\lambda \), \(\lambda \| v\| ^2 = \langle f v, v \rangle = \langle v, fv \rangle = \bar\lambda \| v\| ^2\), so \(\lambda \in \mathbb {R}\).

(1) By induction on \(\dim V\). By Theorem 9.7.3, \(\chi _f\) has a root, so \(f\) has an eigenvector \(v\). Since \(f\) and \(f^\dagger \) commute, \(f^\dagger \) preserves the eigenspace \(V_\lambda \), and its restriction there has an eigenvector \(u\); then \(u\) is an eigenvector of both \(f\) and \(f^\dagger \). Then \(W = \mathbb {C}u\) is invariant under both, so \(W^\perp \) is invariant under \(f^\dagger \) and \((f^\dagger )^\dagger = f\) (Lemma 10.9.5), and the restriction of \(f\) to \(W^\perp \) is normal. Apply induction and normalise \(u\).

(2) Over \(\mathbb {C}\) this follows from (1). Over \(\mathbb {R}\), choose an orthonormal basis and let \(A\) be the real symmetric matrix of \(f\). As a complex matrix acting on \(\mathbb {C}^n\) with the standard inner product, \(A\) is self-adjoint, so it has an eigenvalue, which is real, and hence a real root \(\lambda \) of \(\chi _f\); so \(f\) has an eigenvector \(u \in V\). The line \(\mathbb {R}u\) and its orthogonal complement are invariant under \(f = f^\dagger \), and we conclude by induction.

00JP Counterexample 10.9.7

Normality is necessary in (1): the matrix \(N\) of Counterexample 10.6.7 is not diagonalisable at all. Over \(\mathbb {R}\), normal is not enough: the rotation of that counterexample is orthogonal, hence normal, but has no real eigenvector.

00JQ Corollary 10.9.8

A real symmetric (or complex Hermitian) matrix \(A\) defines an inner product \(\langle v, w \rangle = w^* A v\) if and only if all its eigenvalues are positive. Every inner product on \(\mathbb {R}^n\) (or \(\mathbb {C}^n\)) is of this form.

Proof â–¼

Diagonalise \(A = U D U^*\) with \(U\) unitary (Theorem 10.9.6); then \(w^*Av = (U^*w)^* D (U^*v)\), which is positive definite iff all diagonal entries of \(D\) are positive. The matrix of an inner product in the standard basis is Hermitian.

02D8 Proposition 10.9.9 Singular value decomposition

Let \(f \colon V \to W\) be a linear map between finite-dimensional inner product spaces over \(\mathbb {K} = \mathbb {R}\) or \(\mathbb {C}\), of rank \(r\). There are orthonormal bases \((v_1, \ldots , v_n)\) of \(V\) and \((w_1, \ldots , w_m)\) of \(W\) and real numbers \(\sigma _1 \geq \cdots \geq \sigma _r {\gt} 0\) with \(f(v_i) = \sigma _iw_i\) for \(i \leq r\) and \(f(v_i) = 0\) for \(i {\gt} r\). The \(\sigma _i^2\) are the nonzero eigenvalues of \(f^\dagger f\), so the singular values \(\sigma _i\) are determined by \(f\); the largest one is the operator norm \(\| f\| = \sup _{\| v\| = 1}\| f(v)\| \).

Proof â–¼

The endomorphism \(f^\dagger f\) of \(V\) is self-adjoint and \(\langle f^\dagger fv, v\rangle = \| f(v)\| ^2 \geq 0\), so by Theorem 10.9.6 there is an orthonormal basis \((v_i)\) of \(V\) with \(f^\dagger fv_i = \lambda _iv_i\) and \(\lambda _i \geq 0\); order it so that \(\lambda _1 \geq \lambda _2 \geq \cdots \). Then \(\langle f(v_i), f(v_j)\rangle = \langle f^\dagger fv_i, v_j\rangle = \lambda _i\delta _{ij}\), so the \(f(v_i)\) are pairwise orthogonal, \(f(v_i) = 0\) exactly when \(\lambda _i = 0\), and the number of \(i\) with \(\lambda _i {\gt} 0\) is the rank \(r\). Put \(\sigma _i = \sqrt{\lambda _i}\) and \(w_i = f(v_i)/\sigma _i\) for \(i \leq r\); these are orthonormal, and we extend them to an orthonormal basis of \(W\) (Theorem 10.9.3). For \(v = \sum _ic_iv_i\) of norm one, \(\| f(v)\| ^2 = \sum _{i \leq r}\sigma _i^2|c_i|^2 \leq \sigma _1^2\), with equality at \(v = v_1\).

02D9 Example 10.9.10

For \(f(x, y) = (x + y, 0)\) on \(\mathbb {R}^2\), \(f^\dagger f\) has matrix \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\) with eigenvalues \(2\) and \(0\) and eigenvectors \((1, 1)/\sqrt2\) and \((1, -1)/\sqrt2\). So \(\sigma _1 = \sqrt2\), \(w_1 = (1, 0)\), and \(\| f\| = \sqrt2\), although the eigenvalues of \(f\) itself are \(1\) and \(0\): for non-normal maps the operator norm is not the largest absolute value of an eigenvalue.
