32.6 Submanifolds017U

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

Most manifolds met in practice sit inside a Euclidean space or another manifold: spheres, surfaces in \(\mathbb {R}^3\), matrix groups such as \(\mathrm{O}(n)\). A \(k\)-dimensional submanifold is a subset that looks, in suitable charts of the ambient manifold, like \(\mathbb {R}^k \times \{ 0\} \subseteq \mathbb {R}^n\). The main tools to produce submanifolds are graphs (Proposition 32.6.4) and level sets of submersions (Theorem 32.6.8).

017V Definition 32.6.1

Let \(f \colon M \to N\) be smooth. It is an immersion if every \(d_pf\) is injective, a submersion if every \(d_pf\) is surjective, and an embedding if it is an immersion that is a homeomorphism onto its image (with the subspace topology). A subset \(S \subseteq M^n\) is a submanifold of dimension \(k\) if every \(p \in S\) lies in a chart \((U, \varphi )\) of \(M\) with

\[ \varphi (U \cap S) = \varphi (U) \cap (\mathbb {R}^k \times \{ 0\} ); \]

such a chart is a slice chart for \(S\). The number \(n - k\) is the codimension of \(S\).

02EH Example 32.6.2

  1. The inclusion \(\mathbb {R}^k \to \mathbb {R}^n\), \(x \mapsto (x, 0)\), is an immersion and an embedding; the projection \(\mathbb {R}^n \to \mathbb {R}^k\) onto the first coordinates is a submersion. The projection \(M \times N \to M\) is a submersion (Proposition 32.4.13).

  2. A curve \(\gamma \colon J \to M\) is an immersion if and only if its velocity never vanishes. So \(t \mapsto (\cos t, \sin t) \in \mathbb {R}^2\) is an immersion, and \(t \mapsto (t^2, t^3)\) is not, since its velocity vanishes at \(t = 0\).

  3. The function \(f(x) = \| x\| ^2\) on \(\mathbb {R}^n\) is a submersion on \(\mathbb {R}^n \setminus \{ 0\} \), since \(d_xf(v) = 2\langle x, v\rangle \), and not at \(0\).

  4. A local diffeomorphism, such as \(e \colon \mathbb {R}\to S^1\) of Example 32.5.6(4), is both an immersion and a submersion.

02EI Lemma 32.6.3

Let \(S \subseteq M^n\) be a submanifold of dimension \(k\), with the subspace topology, and let \(\mathrm{pr} \colon \mathbb {R}^n \to \mathbb {R}^k\) be the projection onto the first \(k\) coordinates. Then \(S\) is a topological \(k\)-manifold, the maps \(\mathrm{pr} \circ \varphi |_{U \cap S}\), for slice charts \((U, \varphi )\), form a smooth atlas of \(S\), and with the smooth structure it generates the inclusion \(S \to M\) is an embedding. In these charts the inclusion is \(x \mapsto (x, 0)\).

Proof â–¼

\(S\) is Hausdorff and second countable, as a subspace of \(M\). For a slice chart, \(\varphi |_{U \cap S}\) is a homeomorphism of the open subset \(U \cap S\) of \(S\) onto \(\varphi (U) \cap (\mathbb {R}^k \times \{ 0\} )\), which is open in \(\mathbb {R}^k \times \{ 0\} \cong \mathbb {R}^k\); so \(\mathrm{pr} \circ \varphi |_{U \cap S}\) is a chart of the topological manifold \(S\). For two slice charts \(\varphi \) and \(\psi \), the transition map of the restricted charts is \(x \mapsto \mathrm{pr}(\psi \circ \varphi ^{-1}(x, 0))\) on the open set \(\{ x \in \mathbb {R}^k : (x, 0) \in \varphi (U \cap V)\} \), which is smooth; so these charts form a smooth atlas. In the restricted chart on \(S\) and the chart \(\varphi \) on \(M\), the inclusion is \(x \mapsto (x, 0)\), which is smooth with injective derivative. So the inclusion is an immersion, and it is a homeomorphism onto its image because \(S\) has the subspace topology.

From now on a submanifold carries this smooth structure. We identify \(T_pS\) with its image under the injective map \(d_p\iota \colon T_pS \to T_pM\), where \(\iota \) is the inclusion; in a slice chart, \(T_pS\) is spanned by \(\partial /\partial x^1|_p, \ldots , \partial /\partial x^k|_p\).

02EJ Proposition 32.6.4

Let \(W \subseteq \mathbb {R}^k\) be open and \(g \colon W \to \mathbb {R}^{n-k}\) smooth. Then the graph \(\Gamma _g = \{ (x, g(x)) : x \in W\} \) is a \(k\)-dimensional submanifold of \(\mathbb {R}^n\), and the projection \(\Gamma _g \to W\) is a diffeomorphism.

Proof â–¼

The map \(\Phi (x, y) = (x, y - g(x))\) is a diffeomorphism of the open set \(W \times \mathbb {R}^{n-k}\) onto itself, with inverse \((x, z) \mapsto (x, z + g(x))\), so it is a chart of \(\mathbb {R}^n\) (Proposition 32.3.3(3)). It maps \(\Gamma _g\) onto \(W \times \{ 0\} \), so it is a slice chart around every point of \(\Gamma _g\). The restricted chart \(\mathrm{pr} \circ \Phi |_{\Gamma _g}\) is the projection \((x, g(x)) \mapsto x\), which is therefore a diffeomorphism onto \(W\).

02EK Proposition 32.6.5

Let \(S \subseteq N\) be a submanifold and \(f \colon M \to N\) a smooth map with \(f(M) \subseteq S\). Then \(f\) is smooth as a map \(M \to S\). The restriction of a smooth map \(M \to N\) to a submanifold of \(M\) is smooth.

Proof â–¼

As a map to \(S\), \(f\) is continuous, since \(S\) has the subspace topology. Let \(p \in M\) and let \((V, \psi )\) be a slice chart at \(f(p)\); choose a chart \((U, \varphi )\) at \(p\) with \(f(U) \subseteq V\). The local representative of \(f \colon M \to S\) in the charts \(\varphi \) and \(\mathrm{pr} \circ \psi |_{V \cap S}\) is \(\mathrm{pr} \circ (\psi \circ f \circ \varphi ^{-1})\), which is smooth. The second statement holds because the restriction is the composite with the smooth inclusion.

02EL Example 32.6.6

The sphere \(S^n\) is an \(n\)-dimensional submanifold of \(\mathbb {R}^{n+1}\). Let \(p \in S^n\) and choose \(i\) with \(p_i \neq 0\), say \(p_i {\gt} 0\). On the open set \(U = \{ x : x_i {\gt} 0, \sum _{j \neq i}x_j^2 {\lt} 1\} \), the sphere is the graph \(x_i = g(x')\) of the smooth function \(g(x') = (1 - \| x'\| ^2)^{1/2}\) of the other coordinates \(x' = (x_j)_{j \neq i}\), which ranges over the open unit ball \(B\) of \(\mathbb {R}^n\). The chart \(x \mapsto (x', x_i - g(x'))\) of Proposition 32.6.4, restricted to \(U\), maps \(U \cap S^n\) onto \(B \times \{ 0\} \) and \(U\) onto a set whose intersection with \(\mathbb {R}^n \times \{ 0\} \) is \(B \times \{ 0\} \), since \(g {\gt} 0\); so it is a slice chart at \(p\). The case \(p_i {\lt} 0\) is the same with the negative square root. The structure obtained in this way agrees with the one given by the stereographic charts (Example 32.2.9(2)): the map \(\sigma _N\) is the restriction to \(S^n \setminus \{ N\} \) of the smooth map \(x \mapsto (x_1, \ldots , x_n)/(1 - x_{n+1})\) on \(\{ x_{n+1} \neq 1\} \), and its inverse \(\tau \) is smooth as a map to \(\mathbb {R}^{n+1}\) with image in \(S^n\); by Proposition 32.6.5 both are smooth for the submanifold structure. So \(\sigma _N\), and likewise \(\sigma _S\), is a diffeomorphism onto \(\mathbb {R}^n\), hence a chart of the submanifold structure (Proposition 32.3.3(3)), and the two structures coincide by Corollary 32.2.4(1).

The next proposition says that, up to a change of coordinates, immersions and submersions look like the linear models of Example 32.6.2(1).

017W Proposition 32.6.7

Let \(f \colon M^m \to N^n\) be smooth and \(p \in M\).

  1. If \(d_pf\) is injective, there are charts around \(p\) and \(f(p)\) in which \(f\) is \(x \mapsto (x, 0)\); if \(d_pf\) is surjective, there are charts in which \(f\) is \((x, y) \mapsto x\). In particular an immersion is injective on a neighbourhood of each point, and a submersion is an open map.

  2. The image of an embedding \(f\) is a submanifold, and \(f\) is a diffeomorphism onto it. Conversely, a submanifold is a smooth manifold for which the inclusion is an embedding (Lemma 32.6.3).

  3. An injective immersion of a compact manifold is an embedding.

Proof â–¼

(1) Choose charts \((U, \varphi )\) at \(p\) and \((V, \psi )\) at \(f(p)\) with \(f(U) \subseteq V\), and let \(F = \psi \circ f \circ \varphi ^{-1}\). By Proposition 32.4.6(3), \(DF(\varphi (p))\) is the matrix of \(d_pf\), so it is injective, respectively surjective, when \(d_pf\) is. If it is surjective, Theorem 26.11.4(1) gives a diffeomorphism \(\theta \) from a neighbourhood of \(\varphi (p)\) onto an open subset of \(\mathbb {R}^m\) with \(F \circ \theta ^{-1}(x^1, \ldots , x^m) = (x^1, \ldots , x^n)\). By Lemma 32.2.6, \(\theta \circ \varphi \), restricted to the preimage of that neighbourhood, is a chart, and in the charts \(\theta \circ \varphi \) and \(\psi \) the local representative of \(f\) is \(F \circ \theta ^{-1}\), the projection. If \(DF(\varphi (p))\) is injective, Theorem 26.11.4(2) gives a diffeomorphism \(\theta \) from a neighbourhood \(\Omega \) of \(F(\varphi (p))\) onto an open subset of \(\mathbb {R}^n\) with \(\theta \circ F(x) = (x, 0)\) near \(\varphi (p)\). Then \(\theta \circ \psi \) is a chart on \(\psi ^{-1}(\Omega )\); shrinking \(U\) so that \(F(\varphi (U)) \subseteq \Omega \) and \(\theta \circ F(x) = (x, 0)\) on \(\varphi (U)\), the local representative in the charts \(\varphi \) and \(\theta \circ \psi \) is \(x \mapsto (x, 0)\). The map \(x \mapsto (x, 0)\) is injective and the projection is open; since charts are homeomorphisms, the last statements follow.

(2) Let \(f \colon M^k \to N^n\) be an embedding and \(p \in M\). By (1) there are charts \((U, \varphi )\) at \(p\) and \((V, \psi )\) at \(f(p)\) with \(f(U) \subseteq V\) and \(\psi \circ f \circ \varphi ^{-1}(x) = (x, 0)\) on \(\varphi (U)\); so \(\psi (f(U)) = \varphi (U) \times \{ 0\} \). This alone does not make \(\psi \) a slice chart, because other parts of \(f(M)\) may pass through \(V\), as happens for a figure eight at its crossing point. But \(f\) is a homeomorphism onto its image, so \(f(U)\) is open in \(f(M)\): there is an open \(W \subseteq N\) with \(f(U) = W \cap f(M)\). Let \(V' = V \cap W \cap \psi ^{-1}(\varphi (U) \times \mathbb {R}^{n-k})\), an open neighbourhood of \(f(p)\). Every point of \(V' \cap f(M)\) lies in \(W \cap f(M) = f(U)\), so \(\psi (V' \cap f(M)) = \psi (V') \cap (\varphi (U) \times \{ 0\} )\). Conversely, if \((x, 0) \in \psi (V')\), then \(x \in \varphi (U)\) by the choice of \(V'\), and \((x, 0) = \psi (f(\varphi ^{-1}(x)))\) lies in \(\psi (V' \cap f(M))\). So \(\psi (V' \cap f(M)) = \psi (V') \cap (\mathbb {R}^k \times \{ 0\} )\), and \(\psi |_{V'}\) is a slice chart. In the charts \(\varphi \) and \(\mathrm{pr} \circ \psi |_{V' \cap f(M)}\), the map \(f \colon M \to f(M)\) is the identity near \(\varphi (p)\); so \(f\) is a bijective local diffeomorphism onto \(f(M)\), hence a diffeomorphism (Theorem 32.4.14).

(3) A continuous injection from a compact space to a Hausdorff space is a homeomorphism onto its image (Proposition 17.6.2(4)).

017X Theorem 32.6.8 Regular value theorem

Let \(f \colon M^n \to N^m\) be smooth and \(q \in N\) a regular value: \(d_pf\) is surjective for every \(p \in f^{-1}(q)\). Then \(f^{-1}(q)\) is a submanifold of dimension \(n - m\), with \(T_p(f^{-1}(q)) = \ker d_pf\).

Proof â–¼

Let \(S = f^{-1}(q)\) and \(p \in S\). By Proposition 32.6.7(1) there are charts \((U, \varphi )\) at \(p\) and \((V, \psi )\) at \(q\) with \(f(U) \subseteq V\) in which \(f\) is \((x^1, \ldots , x^n) \mapsto (x^1, \ldots , x^m)\). With \(c = \psi (q) \in \mathbb {R}^m\), \(\varphi (U \cap S) = \{ x \in \varphi (U) : (x^1, \ldots , x^m) = c\} \). Compose \(\varphi \) with the translation \(x \mapsto x - (c, 0)\) and then with the linear map that moves the last \(n - m\) coordinates to the front (Lemma 32.2.6(2)). In the new chart \(\varphi '\), \(\varphi '(U \cap S) = \varphi '(U) \cap (\mathbb {R}^{n-m} \times \{ 0\} )\): it is a slice chart. So \(S\) is a submanifold of dimension \(n - m\). Since \(f\) is constant on \(S\), the chain rule gives \(d_pf \circ d_p\iota = 0\), so \(T_pS \subseteq \ker d_pf\). Both spaces have dimension \(n - m\), the second by rank–nullity (Theorem 10.2.2), so they are equal.

If \(q\) is not in the image of \(f\), it is a regular value and \(f^{-1}(q) = \emptyset \). For a submanifold \(S\) of \(\mathbb {R}^N\), Proposition 32.4.9 identifies \(T_pS \subseteq T_p\mathbb {R}^N = \mathbb {R}^N\) with the set of velocities \(\gamma '(0)\) of smooth curves \(\gamma \) in \(S\) with \(\gamma (0) = p\), which is the tangent space of elementary geometry.

02HS Proposition 32.6.9 Fibre products

Let \(f \colon M \to P\) and \(g \colon N \to P\) be smooth with \(g\) a submersion. Then

\[ M \times _PN = \{ (m, n) \in M \times N : f(m) = g(n)\} \]

is a submanifold of \(M \times N\) of dimension \(\dim M + \dim N - \dim P\), with \(T_{(m,n)}(M \times _PN) = \{ (u, v) \in T_mM \oplus T_nN : d_mf(u) = d_ng(v)\} \). The projections to \(M\) and \(N\) are smooth, the projection to \(M\) is a submersion, and for every manifold \(L\) the smooth maps \(L \to M \times _PN\) correspond to pairs of smooth maps \(a \colon L \to M\), \(b \colon L \to N\) with \(f \circ a = g \circ b\).

Proof â–¼

Let \((m, n) \in M \times _PN\) and \(p = g(n)\). By Proposition 32.6.7(1) there are charts in which \(g\) is the projection \((x, y) \mapsto x\) from an open subset of \(\mathbb {R}^{\dim P} \times \mathbb {R}^{\dim N - \dim P}\), and a chart \((U, \varphi )\) at \(m\) with \(f(U)\) inside the chart of \(P\); write \(\tilde{f}\) for the local representative of \(f\). In these coordinates the fibre product is \(\{ (z, (x, y)) : x = \tilde{f}(z)\} \), which after reordering the factors as \((z, y, x)\) is the graph of the smooth map \((z, y) \mapsto \tilde{f}(z)\). By Proposition 32.6.4 a graph is a slice in suitable coordinates, so these coordinates give a slice chart of \(M \times N\) at \((m, n)\), and \(M \times _PN\) is a submanifold of dimension \(\dim M + (\dim N - \dim P)\). In the corresponding chart of \(M \times _PN\) the projection to \(M\) is \((z, y) \mapsto z\), a submersion. Differentiating \(f \circ \mathrm{pr}_M = g \circ \mathrm{pr}_N\) on \(M \times _PN\) shows that its tangent space is contained in the space described, which has the same dimension, because \((u, v) \mapsto d_mf(u) - d_ng(v)\) is a surjective linear map \(T_mM \oplus T_nN \to T_pP\) (already \(v \mapsto -d_ng(v)\) is surjective). The universal property follows from Proposition 32.6.5: a pair \((a, b)\) with \(f \circ a = g \circ b\) is a smooth map \(L \to M \times N\) with image in the submanifold \(M \times _PN\).

017Y Example 32.6.10

  1. Spheres and quadrics. \(S^n = f^{-1}(1)\) for \(f(x) = \| x\| ^2\), with \(d_xf(v) = 2\langle x, v\rangle \neq 0\) on \(S^n\); so \(T_xS^n = \ker d_xf = x^\perp \). Similarly, for \(g(x, y, z) = x^2 + y^2 - z^2\) on \(\mathbb {R}^3\) the only critical point is \(0\), so every \(c \neq 0\) is a regular value, and the hyperboloids \(g^{-1}(c)\) are submanifolds of dimension \(2\).

  2. The orthogonal group. Let \(\mathrm{Sym}_n\) be the space of symmetric \(n \times n\) matrices, of dimension \(n(n + 1)/2\), and \(F \colon M_n(\mathbb {R}) \to \mathrm{Sym}_n\), \(F(A) = A^TA\). Then \(\mathrm{O}(n) = F^{-1}(I)\). Since \(F(A + tX) = A^TA + t(A^TX + X^TA) + t^2X^TX\), the differential is \(d_AF(X) = A^TX + X^TA\). For \(A \in \mathrm{O}(n)\) it is surjective onto \(\mathrm{Sym}_n\): given a symmetric \(S\), the matrix \(X = AS/2\) gives \(A^TX + X^TA = S/2 + S/2 = S\). So \(\mathrm{O}(n)\) is a submanifold of dimension \(n^2 - n(n + 1)/2 = n(n - 1)/2\), and its tangent space at \(I\) is \(\ker d_IF = \{ X : X + X^T = 0\} \), the skew-symmetric matrices.

  3. The special linear group. For \(A \in \mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb {R})\), \(\det (A + tX) = \det (A)\det (I + tA^{-1}X)\), and \(\det (I + tY) = 1 + t\operatorname {tr}Y + O(t^2)\) by expanding the determinant; so \(d_A\det (X) = \det (A)\operatorname {tr}(A^{-1}X)\). For \(A \in \mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb {R}) = \det ^{-1}(1)\), taking \(X = A\) gives \(d_A\det (A) = n \neq 0\), so \(1\) is a regular value, \(\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb {R})\) is a submanifold of dimension \(n^2 - 1\), and its tangent space at \(I\) consists of the matrices of trace \(0\).

02EM Example 32.6.11

If \(S \subseteq \mathbb {R}^N\) is a submanifold and \(X \colon S \to \mathbb {R}^N\) is a smooth map with \(X(p) \in T_pS\) for all \(p\), then \(X\) is a vector field on \(S\): in a slice chart \(\psi = (y^1, \ldots , y^N)\) of \(\mathbb {R}^N\), the components of \(X\) in the restricted chart \((y^1, \ldots , y^k)\) of \(S\) are \(X(p)(y^i) = \sum _j X_j(p)\, \partial _j y^i(p)\), which are smooth. On the odd-dimensional sphere \(S^{2m-1} \subseteq \mathbb {R}^{2m}\), the map

\[ X(x) = (-x_2, x_1, -x_4, x_3, \ldots , -x_{2m}, x_{2m-1}) \]

satisfies \(\langle X(x), x\rangle = 0\), so \(X(x) \in T_xS^{2m-1}\) by Example 32.6.10(1), and \(\| X(x)\| = 1\). So odd spheres carry nowhere vanishing vector fields. Even spheres do not, by the hairy ball theorem (Corollary 20.6.6(2)).

0278 Counterexample 32.6.12

At a critical value the level set can fail to be a submanifold, or not. For \(f(x, y) = xy\) on \(\mathbb {R}^2\), \(0\) is a critical value (\(df = 0\) at the origin) and \(f^{-1}(0)\) is the union of the coordinate axes, which is not even a topological manifold (Counterexample 19.5.5). For \(f(x, y) = x^2\), \(0\) is also a critical value, but \(f^{-1}(0)\) is the \(y\)-axis, a submanifold. So the regular value theorem gives a sufficient condition only. Likewise the cone \(C = g^{-1}(0)\) for \(g = x^2 + y^2 - z^2\) is not a \(2\)-dimensional submanifold near its vertex \(0\). Otherwise \(0\) would have a neighbourhood \(D\) in \(C\) homeomorphic to an open ball of \(\mathbb {R}^2\), and \(D \setminus \{ 0\} \) would be connected (Lemma 19.5.4). But \(D \setminus \{ 0\} \) contains points with \(z {\gt} 0\) and points with \(z {\lt} 0\) (the points \((t, 0, \pm t) \in C\) tend to \(0\)), and it is the disjoint union of its open subsets \(\{ z {\gt} 0\} \) and \(\{ z {\lt} 0\} \), because \(z = 0\) on \(C\) only at the vertex.

0279 Lemma 32.6.13

Let \(S \subseteq \mathbb {R}^2\) be a \(1\)-dimensional submanifold and \(p \in S\). Then near \(p\), \(S\) is the graph of a smooth function over one of the coordinate axes: there are \(i \in \{ 1, 2\} \), an open interval \(J\) and a smooth \(g \colon J \to \mathbb {R}\) such that near \(p\), \(S = \{ (s, g(s)) : s \in J\} \) if \(i = 1\), or \(S = \{ (g(s), s) : s \in J\} \) if \(i = 2\).

Proof â–¼

The inclusion \(\iota \colon S \to \mathbb {R}^2\) is an embedding (Proposition 32.6.7(2)), so \(d_p\iota (T_pS)\) is a line, and for some coordinate projection \(\mathrm{pr}_i\) the composite \(\mathrm{pr}_i \circ \iota \colon S \to \mathbb {R}\) has nonzero differential at \(p\). By the inverse function theorem (Theorem 32.4.14) it restricts to a diffeomorphism from a neighbourhood of \(p\) in \(S\) onto an open interval \(J\); composing its inverse with the other coordinate gives \(g\).

027A Counterexample 32.6.14

  1. An injective immersion that is not an embedding. Let \(\beta (t) = (\sin 2t, \sin t)\) for \(t \in (-\pi , \pi )\). Then \(\beta '(t) = (2\cos 2t, \cos t)\) never vanishes, since \(\cos t = 0\) forces \(\cos 2t = -1\). It is injective: if \(\beta (s) = \beta (t)\) with \(s \neq t\) in \((-\pi , \pi )\), then \(\sin s = \sin t\) gives \(s = \pm \pi - t\), so \(\sin 2s = -\sin 2t\), and \(\sin 2s = \sin 2t\) forces \(\sin 2t = 0\), that is \(t \in \{ 0, \pm \pi /2\} \); but then \(s = \pm \pi - t\) is either \(\pm \pi \notin (-\pi , \pi )\) or equal to \(t\), a contradiction. The image, a figure eight, equals \(\beta ([-\pi , \pi ])\) because \(\beta (\pm \pi ) = \beta (0)\), so it is compact; since \((-\pi , \pi )\) is not compact, \(\beta \) is not a homeomorphism onto its image.

  2. A dense immersed line. Let \(\alpha \) be irrational and \(\beta \colon \mathbb {R}\to T^2 = S^1 \times S^1\), \(\beta (t) = (e^{2\pi it}, e^{2\pi i\alpha t})\). It is an immersion, and injective: \(\beta (s) = \beta (t)\) forces \(s - t \in \mathbb {Z}\) and \(\alpha (s - t) \in \mathbb {Z}\), so \(s = t\). The closure of \(\beta (\mathbb {R})\) contains \(\{ 1\} \times S^1\), since the points \(\beta (n) = (1, e^{2\pi i\alpha n})\), \(n \in \mathbb {Z}\), are dense there by Lemma 19.3.4; and it is invariant under multiplication by \(\beta (t)\), since \(\beta \) is a homomorphism and multiplication is a homeomorphism; so it contains \(\{ e^{2\pi it}\} \times S^1\) for all \(t\), and \(\beta (\mathbb {R})\) is dense. For \(z = e^{2\pi it_0}\), \(\beta (\mathbb {R}) \cap (\{ z\} \times S^1) = \beta (t_0 + \mathbb {Z})\) is countable. If \(\beta (\mathbb {R})\) were a submanifold, it would be locally closed, being locally a slice in a chart, and a dense locally closed set is open (it is open in its closure). But a nonempty open subset of \(T^2\) meets some circle \(\{ z\} \times S^1\) in a nonempty open subset of that circle, which is uncountable. So \(\beta (\mathbb {R})\) is not a submanifold.

  3. A smooth homeomorphism onto a non-submanifold. The map \(\gamma (t) = (t^2, t^3)\) is smooth and a homeomorphism of \(\mathbb {R}\) onto the cusp \(C = \{ y^2 = x^3\} \), with inverse \((x, y) \mapsto y^{1/3}\), but \(\gamma '(0) = 0\). The cusp is not a submanifold near \(0\): by Lemma 32.6.13 it would be a graph over one axis near \(0\), but over the \(x\)-axis it has two points \((x, \pm x^{3/2})\) for small \(x {\gt} 0\), and over the \(y\)-axis it is \(x = y^{2/3}\), which is not differentiable at \(0\).

02HT Proposition 32.6.15

Let \(S \subseteq \mathbb {R}^N\) and \(p \in S\), and let \(0 \leq k \leq N\). The following are equivalent.

  1. (Slice) There is a chart \((U, \varphi )\) of \(\mathbb {R}^N\) at \(p\) with \(\varphi (U \cap S) = \varphi (U) \cap (\mathbb {R}^k \times \{ 0\} )\).

  2. (Graph) After permuting the coordinates of \(\mathbb {R}^N\) there are open sets \(\Omega \subseteq \mathbb {R}^k\) and \(W \ni p\) in \(\mathbb {R}^N\) and a smooth \(g \colon \Omega \to \mathbb {R}^{N-k}\) with \(W \cap S = \{ (u, g(u)) : u \in \Omega \} \).

  3. (Parametrisation) There are an open \(\Omega \subseteq \mathbb {R}^k\), an open \(W \ni p\) in \(\mathbb {R}^N\) and a smooth \(x \colon \Omega \to \mathbb {R}^N\) with \(x(\Omega ) = W \cap S\), such that \(x\) is a homeomorphism onto \(W \cap S\) with the subspace topology and \(d_ux\) is injective for every \(u \in \Omega \).

  4. (Regular level set) There are an open \(W \ni p\) in \(\mathbb {R}^N\) and a smooth \(F \colon W \to \mathbb {R}^{N-k}\) with \(W \cap S = F^{-1}(0)\) and \(d_qF\) surjective for every \(q \in W \cap S\).

So \(S\) is a \(k\)-dimensional submanifold of \(\mathbb {R}^N\) if and only if one of these holds at every point of \(S\).

Proof â–¼

(1) \(\Rightarrow \) (4). Let \(F\) be the last \(N - k\) coordinates of \(\varphi \) on \(W = U\); then \(F^{-1}(0) = U \cap S\) and \(d_qF\) is surjective, being the last \(N - k\) rows of the invertible matrix of \(d_q\varphi \).

(4) \(\Rightarrow \) (2). By the implicit function theorem (Theorem 26.11.3), applied after permuting coordinates so that the last \(N - k\) columns of \(DF(p)\) are independent, there are open sets \(\Omega \times B \ni p\) inside \(W\) and a smooth \(g \colon \Omega \to B\) such that, for \((u, v) \in \Omega \times B\), \(F(u, v) = 0\) if and only if \(v = g(u)\).

(2) \(\Rightarrow \) (3). Take \(x(u) = (u, g(u))\), whose differential is injective and whose inverse is the projection to the first \(k\) coordinates, a continuous map.

(3) \(\Rightarrow \) (1). The map \(x \colon \Omega \to \mathbb {R}^N\) is an injective immersion and a homeomorphism onto its image, so it is an embedding, and its image \(W \cap S\) is a \(k\)-dimensional submanifold of \(\mathbb {R}^N\) (Proposition 32.6.7(2)); a slice chart at \(p\) for \(W \cap S\), intersected with \(W\), is one for \(S\).

02HU Remark 32.6.16

A regular surface in \(\mathbb {R}^3\), in the sense of do Carmo [dC76, Section 2.2], is a subset \(S \subseteq \mathbb {R}^3\) such that every \(p \in S\) has a parametrisation: an open \(\Omega \subseteq \mathbb {R}^2\), a neighbourhood \(W\) of \(p\) in \(\mathbb {R}^3\) and a smooth \(x \colon \Omega \to \mathbb {R}^3\) with image \(W \cap S\), which is a homeomorphism onto \(W \cap S\) and has injective differential everywhere. This is condition (3) of Proposition 32.6.15 with \(k = 2\) and \(N = 3\), so regular surfaces are exactly the \(2\)-dimensional submanifolds of \(\mathbb {R}^3\), and the charts of the submanifold structure are the inverses \(x^{-1}\) of the parametrisations (Lemma 32.6.3); the change of parameters between two parametrisations is the transition map of these charts, and is smooth. Both conditions on \(x\) are needed: dropping injectivity of \(d_ux\) allows the cusp, and dropping the requirement that \(x\) be a homeomorphism onto its image allows the figure eight, neither of which is a submanifold (Counterexample 32.6.14).

017Z Definition 32.6.17

A subset \(A\) of a smooth \(n\)-manifold has measure zero if \(\varphi (A \cap U)\) has Lebesgue measure zero for every chart; by the change of variables formula (Theorem 27.10.2) it suffices to check this for the charts of one atlas.

0180 Theorem 32.6.18 Sard

Let \(f \colon M \to N\) be smooth. The set of critical values of \(f\) (the images of points where \(d_pf\) is not surjective) has measure zero in \(N\); in particular regular values are dense. If \(\dim M {\lt} \dim N\), the image of \(f\) has measure zero.

Proof â–¼

The last statement follows from the first, since then every point is critical; alternatively, compose with the projection \(\mathbb {R}^{\dim N} \to \mathbb {R}^{\dim M}\) and use that a smooth map is locally Lipschitz, so it maps sets of measure zero to sets of measure zero and \(\mathbb {R}^{\dim M} \times \{ 0\} \) has measure zero. The general case is [Mil97, Chapter 2] or [Lee13, Chapter 6]; the proof is a careful induction on the order of vanishing of the derivatives, using Taylor’s theorem and a covering argument in charts, together with the fact that a countable union of sets of measure zero has measure zero (Proposition 27.2.2) and that manifolds are covered by countably many charts.
