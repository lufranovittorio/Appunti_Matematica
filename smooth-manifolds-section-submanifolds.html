31.4 Submanifolds017U

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

017V Definition 31.4.1

A smooth \(f \colon M \to N\) is an immersion if every \(d_pf\) is injective, a submersion if every \(d_pf\) is surjective, and an embedding if it is an immersion that is a homeomorphism onto its image. A subset \(S \subseteq M\) is a submanifold of dimension \(k\) if every \(p \in S\) has a chart \(\varphi \colon U \to \mathbb {R}^n\) of \(M\) with \(\varphi (U \cap S) = \varphi (U) \cap (\mathbb {R}^k \times \{ 0\} )\).

017W Proposition 31.4.2

Let \(f \colon M \to N\) be smooth and \(p \in M\).

  1. If \(f\) is an immersion at \(p\), there are charts around \(p\) and \(f(p)\) in which \(f\) is \(x \mapsto (x, 0)\); if \(f\) is a submersion at \(p\), there are charts in which \(f\) is \((x, y) \mapsto x\).

  2. The image of an embedding is a submanifold, and a submanifold is a smooth manifold for which the inclusion is an embedding.

  3. An injective immersion of a compact manifold is an embedding.

Proof â–¼

(1) is Theorem 25.11.4 read in charts. (2) For an embedding \(f\), the local form in (1) shows that \(f(M)\) is locally \(\mathbb {R}^k \times \{ 0\} \) in suitable charts; being a homeomorphism onto its image guarantees that the chart can be shrunk so that it meets \(f(M)\) only in that slice. The converse is clear from the definition of submanifold. (3) A continuous injection from a compact space to a Hausdorff space is a homeomorphism onto its image (Proposition 16.6.2).

017X Theorem 31.4.3 Regular value theorem

Let \(f \colon M^n \to N^m\) be smooth and \(q \in N\) a regular value: \(d_pf\) is surjective for every \(p \in f^{-1}(q)\). Then \(f^{-1}(q)\) is a submanifold of dimension \(n - m\), with \(T_p(f^{-1}(q)) = \ker d_pf\).

Proof â–¼

By Proposition 31.4.2(1) there are charts in which \(f\) is the projection \((x, y) \mapsto x\), in which \(f^{-1}(q)\) is \(\{ 0\} \times \mathbb {R}^{n-m}\). Differentiating \(f|_{f^{-1}(q)}\), which is constant, gives \(T_p(f^{-1}(q)) \subseteq \ker d_pf\), and the dimensions agree.

017Y Example 31.4.4

\(S^n = f^{-1}(1)\) for \(f(x) = \| x\| ^2\), with \(d_xf(v) = 2\langle x, v\rangle \neq 0\) on \(S^n\). The orthogonal group \(O(n) = F^{-1}(I)\) for \(F(A) = A^TA\), viewed as a map to symmetric matrices: \(d_AF(X) = A^TX + X^TA\) is surjective onto symmetric matrices for \(A \in O(n)\) (take \(X = AS/2\)), so \(O(n)\) is a submanifold of dimension \(n^2 - n(n+1)/2 = n(n-1)/2\). Similarly \(\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb {R}) = \det ^{-1}(1)\) has dimension \(n^2 - 1\), since \(d_A\det (X) = \det (A)\operatorname {tr}(A^{-1}X)\).

0278 Counterexample 31.4.5

At a critical value the level set can fail to be a submanifold, or not. For \(f(x, y) = xy\) on \(\mathbb {R}^2\), \(0\) is a critical value (\(df = 0\) at the origin) and \(f^{-1}(0)\) is the union of the coordinate axes, which is not even a topological manifold (Counterexample 18.5.5). For \(f(x, y) = x^2\), \(0\) is also a critical value, but \(f^{-1}(0)\) is the \(y\)-axis, a submanifold. So the regular value theorem gives a sufficient condition only.

0279 Lemma 31.4.6

Let \(S \subseteq \mathbb {R}^2\) be a \(1\)-dimensional submanifold and \(p \in S\). Then near \(p\), \(S\) is the graph of a smooth function over one of the coordinate axes: there are \(i \in \{ 1, 2\} \), an open interval \(J\) and a smooth \(g \colon J \to \mathbb {R}\) such that near \(p\), \(S = \{ (s, g(s)) : s \in J\} \) if \(i = 1\), or \(S = \{ (g(s), s) : s \in J\} \) if \(i = 2\).

Proof â–¼

The inclusion \(\iota \colon S \to \mathbb {R}^2\) is an embedding (Proposition 31.4.2(2)), so \(d_p\iota (T_pS)\) is a line, and for some coordinate projection \(\mathrm{pr}_i\) the composite \(\mathrm{pr}_i \circ \iota \colon S \to \mathbb {R}\) has nonzero differential at \(p\). By the inverse function theorem (Theorem 25.11.2, in charts) it restricts to a diffeomorphism from a neighbourhood of \(p\) in \(S\) onto an open interval \(J\); composing its inverse with the other coordinate gives \(g\).

027A Counterexample 31.4.7

  1. An injective immersion that is not an embedding. Let \(\beta (t) = (\sin 2t, \sin t)\) for \(t \in (-\pi , \pi )\). Then \(\beta '(t) = (2\cos 2t, \cos t)\) never vanishes, since \(\cos t = 0\) forces \(\cos 2t = -1\). It is injective: if \(\beta (s) = \beta (t)\) with \(s \neq t\) in \((-\pi , \pi )\), then \(\sin s = \sin t\) gives \(s = \pm \pi - t\), so \(\sin 2s = -\sin 2t\), and \(\sin 2s = \sin 2t\) forces \(\sin 2t = 0\), that is \(t \in \{ 0, \pm \pi /2\} \); but then \(s = \pm \pi - t\) is either \(\pm \pi \notin (-\pi , \pi )\) or equal to \(t\), a contradiction. The image, a figure eight, equals \(\beta ([-\pi , \pi ])\) because \(\beta (\pm \pi ) = \beta (0)\), so it is compact; since \((-\pi , \pi )\) is not compact, \(\beta \) is not a homeomorphism onto its image.

  2. A dense immersed line. Let \(\alpha \) be irrational and \(\beta \colon \mathbb {R}\to T^2 = S^1 \times S^1\), \(\beta (t) = (e^{2\pi it}, e^{2\pi i\alpha t})\). It is an immersion, and injective: \(\beta (s) = \beta (t)\) forces \(s - t \in \mathbb {Z}\) and \(\alpha (s - t) \in \mathbb {Z}\), so \(s = t\). The closure of \(\beta (\mathbb {R})\) contains \(\{ 1\} \times S^1\), since the points \(\beta (n) = (1, e^{2\pi i\alpha n})\), \(n \in \mathbb {Z}\), are dense there by Lemma 18.3.4; and it is invariant under multiplication by \(\beta (t)\), since \(\beta \) is a homomorphism and multiplication is a homeomorphism; so it contains \(\{ e^{2\pi it}\} \times S^1\) for all \(t\), and \(\beta (\mathbb {R})\) is dense. For \(z = e^{2\pi it_0}\), \(\beta (\mathbb {R}) \cap (\{ z\} \times S^1) = \beta (t_0 + \mathbb {Z})\) is countable. If \(\beta (\mathbb {R})\) were a submanifold, it would be locally closed, being locally a slice in a chart, and a dense locally closed set is open (it is open in its closure). But a nonempty open subset of \(T^2\) meets some circle \(\{ z\} \times S^1\) in a nonempty open subset of that circle, which is uncountable. So \(\beta (\mathbb {R})\) is not a submanifold.

  3. A smooth homeomorphism onto a non-submanifold. The map \(\gamma (t) = (t^2, t^3)\) is smooth and a homeomorphism of \(\mathbb {R}\) onto the cusp \(C = \{ y^2 = x^3\} \), with inverse \((x, y) \mapsto y^{1/3}\), but \(\gamma '(0) = 0\). The cusp is not a submanifold near \(0\): by Lemma 31.4.6 it would be a graph over one axis near \(0\), but over the \(x\)-axis it has two points \((x, \pm x^{3/2})\) for small \(x {\gt} 0\), and over the \(y\)-axis it is \(x = y^{2/3}\), which is not differentiable at \(0\).

017Z Definition 31.4.8

A subset \(A\) of a smooth \(n\)-manifold has measure zero if \(\varphi (A \cap U)\) has Lebesgue measure zero for every chart; by the change of variables formula (Theorem 26.8.2) it suffices to check this for the charts of one atlas.

0180 Theorem 31.4.9 Sard

Let \(f \colon M \to N\) be smooth. The set of critical values of \(f\) (the images of points where \(d_pf\) is not surjective) has measure zero in \(N\); in particular regular values are dense. If \(\dim M {\lt} \dim N\), the image of \(f\) has measure zero.

Proof â–¼

The last statement follows from the first, since then every point is critical; alternatively, compose with the projection \(\mathbb {R}^{\dim N} \to \mathbb {R}^{\dim M}\) and use that a smooth map is locally Lipschitz, so it maps sets of measure zero to sets of measure zero and \(\mathbb {R}^{\dim M} \times \{ 0\} \) has measure zero. The general case is [Mil97, Chapter 2] or [Lee13, Chapter 6]; the proof is a careful induction on the order of vanishing of the derivatives, using Taylor’s theorem and a covering argument in charts, together with the fact that a countable union of sets of measure zero has measure zero (Proposition 26.2.2) and that manifolds are covered by countably many charts.
