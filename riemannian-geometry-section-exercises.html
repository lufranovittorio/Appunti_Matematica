01CR Exercise 39.15.1

Verify that \(\delta = -*d*\) on \(k\)-forms when \(n\) is even, and compute \(\delta \) on \(1\)-forms in \(\mathbb {R}^3\), identifying it with minus the divergence.

01CS Exercise 39.15.2

Show that in dimension \(n = 2k\) the Hodge star on \(k\)-forms depends only on the conformal class of the metric, and that \(*^2 = (-1)^k\) on \(\Omega ^k\).

Solution â–¼

Under \(g \mapsto \lambda g\) with \(\lambda {\gt} 0\), the inner product on \(k\)-forms scales by \(\lambda ^{-k}\) and the volume form by \(\lambda ^{n/2} = \lambda ^k\), so \(*\) is unchanged by the characterisation \(\alpha \wedge *\beta = \langle \alpha ,\beta \rangle \mathrm{vol}\). The sign is \((-1)^{k(n-k)} = (-1)^{k^2} = (-1)^k\).

01CT Exercise 39.15.3

Compute the Laplacian on functions on the round sphere \(S^2\) in spherical coordinates, and verify that the restrictions of linear functions of \(\mathbb {R}^3\) are eigenfunctions with eigenvalue \(2\).

01CU Exercise 39.15.4

Let \(G\) be a compact Lie group with a bi-invariant metric. Show that the geodesics through \(e\) are the one-parameter subgroups, and that \(\nabla _XY = \frac12[X, Y]\) for left-invariant fields.

028C Exercise 39.15.5

Show that the geodesics of the round sphere \(S^n \subseteq \mathbb {R}^{n+1}\) are the great circles \(\gamma (t) = \cos (t)p + \sin (t)v\) with \(|p| = |v| = 1\) and \(v \perp p\) (up to affine reparametrisation).

Solution â–¼

The reflection \(\rho \) of \(\mathbb {R}^{n+1}\) fixing the plane spanned by \(p\) and \(v\) pointwise preserves \(S^n\) and its metric, so it is an isometry, and it maps geodesics to geodesics. The geodesic \(\gamma _v\) with \(\gamma _v(0) = p\), \(\dot\gamma _v(0) = v\) is mapped to a geodesic with the same initial data, hence to itself by uniqueness; so \(\gamma _v\) lies in the fixed set of \(\rho \) on \(S^n\), the great circle through \(p\) and \(v\). Having constant speed \(1\) (Proposition 39.4.4) and starting at \(p\) with velocity \(v\), it is \(\cos (t)p + \sin (t)v\).

028D Exercise 39.15.6

Show that the holonomy group of a Riemannian product \(M_1 \times M_2\) at \((p_1, p_2)\) is \(\mathrm{Hol}_{p_1}(M_1) \times \mathrm{Hol}_{p_2}(M_2)\), acting on \(T_{p_1}M_1 \oplus T_{p_2}M_2\).

Solution â–¼

The Levi-Civita connection of the product metric is the direct sum of those of the factors, since the direct sum connection is metric and torsion-free. So a vector \((v_1, v_2)\) is transported along a curve \((c_1, c_2)\) to \((P_{c_1}v_1, P_{c_2}v_2)\). Loops at \((p_1, p_2)\) are exactly the pairs of loops at \(p_1\) and \(p_2\), which gives the claim.

028E Exercise 39.15.7

Let \((M, g)\) be connected with \(\mathrm{Hol}_p(g) = \{ 1\} \). Show that \(M\) is flat and that \(TM\) is trivial.

Solution â–¼

By Theorem 39.6.2, each vector of an orthonormal basis of \(T_pM\) extends to a parallel vector field \(E_i\); the \(E_i\) are orthonormal everywhere (parallel transport is an isometry), so they trivialise \(TM\). For parallel fields \(R(X, Y)E_i = \nabla _X\nabla _YE_i - \nabla _Y\nabla _XE_i - \nabla _{[X,Y]}E_i = 0\), so \(R = 0\).

02K5 Exercise 39.15.8

Let \(f, h \colon (a, b) \to \mathbb {R}\) be smooth with \(f {\gt} 0\) and \((f^{\prime }, h^{\prime })\) nowhere zero, and let \(S\) be the surface of revolution \(x(t, \theta ) = (f(t)\cos \theta , f(t)\sin \theta , h(t))\), assuming that the profile curve \(t \mapsto (f(t), h(t))\) is injective and a homeomorphism onto its image. Show that the area of \(S\) is \(2\pi \int _a^bf(t)\sqrt{f^{\prime }(t)^2 + h^{\prime }(t)^2}\, dt\).

Solution â–¼

Here \(x_t = (f^{\prime }\cos \theta , f^{\prime }\sin \theta , h^{\prime })\) and \(x_\theta = (-f\sin \theta , f\cos \theta , 0)\), so \(E = f^{\prime 2} + h^{\prime 2}\), \(F = 0\) and \(G = f^2\). By Proposition 39.3.4(2), \(dA = f\sqrt{f^{\prime 2} + h^{\prime 2}}\, dt\, d\theta \) on the image of \((a, b) \times (0, 2\pi )\), and the missing meridian \(\theta = 0\) has area \(0\) (it is a segment in the chart given by \(\theta \in (-\pi , \pi )\)). Integrating over \(\theta \) gives the formula.

02K6 Exercise 39.15.9

Show that the area of the part of the unit sphere \(S^2\) between the planes \(z = c\) and \(z = d\), for \(-1 \leq c {\lt} d \leq 1\), is \(2\pi (d - c)\): it depends only on the distance between the planes (Archimedes).

Solution â–¼

Apart from the poles and a meridian, which have area \(0\), the sphere is the surface of revolution of Exercise 39.15.8 with \(t = z \in (-1, 1)\), \(f(z) = \sqrt{1 - z^2}\) and \(h(z) = z\). Then \(f^{\prime } = -z/\sqrt{1 - z^2}\) and \(f\sqrt{f^{\prime 2} + 1} = \sqrt{1 - z^2} \cdot (1 - z^2)^{-1/2} = 1\), so the area of the zone is \(2\pi \int _c^d1\, dz = 2\pi (d - c)\).

02K7 Exercise 39.15.10

On the upper half plane \(H = \{ z = x + iy : y {\gt} 0\} \) with the metric \((dx^2 + dy^2)/y^2\), show that the Killing fields form a Lie algebra of dimension \(3\), spanned by \(\partial _x\), \(x\partial _x + y\partial _y\) and \((x^2 - y^2)\partial _x + 2xy\, \partial _y\). (Show first that the maps \(z \mapsto (az + b)/(cz + d)\) with \(a, b, c, d\) real and \(ad - bc = 1\) are isometries of \(H\).)

Solution â–¼

For \(\phi (z) = (az + b)/(cz + d)\) one computes \(\operatorname {Im}\phi (z) = y/|cz + d|^2\) and \(\phi ^{\prime }(z) = 1/(cz + d)^2\), so \(\phi \) maps \(H\) to itself. As a map of \(\mathbb {R}^2 = \mathbb {C}\), its differential at \(z\) is multiplication by the complex number \(\phi ^{\prime }(z)\), which multiplies Euclidean lengths by \(|\phi ^{\prime }(z)| = 1/|cz + d|^2\). The hyperbolic length of \(v \in T_zH\) is \(|v|/y\), and that of its image is \(|\phi ^{\prime }(z)||v|/\operatorname {Im}\phi (z) = |v|/y\): \(\phi \) is an isometry. The three fields generate the one-parameter groups \(z \mapsto z + t\), \(z \mapsto e^tz\) and \(z \mapsto z/(1 - tz)\) (for \(z/(1 - tz)\), the derivative in \(t\) at \(t = 0\) is \(z^2 = (x^2 - y^2) + 2ixy\)), which are of the form above (for the last one, with \(a = d = 1\), \(b = 0\), \(c = -t\), defined on all of \(H\) since \(1 - tz \neq 0\) for \(y {\gt} 0\)); so they are Killing fields. They are linearly independent, and by Theorem 39.14.6 the Killing algebra has dimension at most \(2 \cdot 3/2 = 3\).

02KP Exercise 39.15.11

Show that the helicoid and the catenoid of Example 39.12.3 are locally isometric: in the parameters \((u, w)\), with \(v = \sinh w\) on the helicoid, both metrics are \(\cosh ^2w\, (du^2 + dw^2)\).

Solution â–¼

The helicoid has the metric \((1 + v^2)\, du^2 + dv^2\). Substituting \(v = \sinh w\), \(dv = \cosh w\, dw\) and \(1 + v^2 = \cosh ^2w\) gives \(\cosh ^2w\, (du^2 + dw^2)\). The catenoid, with \(v\) renamed \(w\), has \(E = G = \cosh ^2w\) and \(F = 0\). So the map sending the point with parameters \((u, \sinh w)\) on the helicoid to the point with parameters \((u, w)\) on the catenoid preserves the metric: it is a local isometry from the helicoid onto the catenoid, which winds the helicoid infinitely often around the catenoid, as the catenoid is \(2\pi \)-periodic in \(u\).

02KQ Exercise 39.15.12

Show that a minimal surface in \(\mathbb {R}^3\) has Gaussian curvature \(K \leq 0\), and deduce that there is no compact minimal surface in \(\mathbb {R}^3\).

Solution â–¼

If \(H = 0\), the principal curvatures are \(\kappa \) and \(-\kappa \), so \(K = -\kappa ^2 \leq 0\) (Corollary 39.10.8). A compact surface in \(\mathbb {R}^3\) has a point with \(K {\gt} 0\) by Lemma 39.11.10.

02NY Exercise 39.15.13

Let \(F \colon \mathbb {R}^n \to S^n \subseteq \mathbb {R}^{n+1}\), \(F(x) = \bigl(2x, |x|^2 - 1\bigr)/(|x|^2 + 1)\), be the inverse of the stereographic projection from the north pole. Show that the pullback of the round metric is \(4(dx_1^2 + \cdots + dx_n^2)/(1 + |x|^2)^2\), and deduce once more from Corollary 39.8.6(3) that \(S^n\) has constant curvature \(1\).

Solution â–¼

Let \(s = 1 + |x|^2\), so \(F = (2x/s, 1 - 2/s)\) and \(ds = 2\sum _kx_k\, dx_k = 2\, x \cdot dx\). Then \(dF = \bigl(2\, dx/s - 2x\, ds/s^2,\; 2\, ds/s^2\bigr)\), and the pullback metric is

\[ |dF|^2 = \frac{4|dx|^2}{s^2} - \frac{8(x \cdot dx)\, ds}{s^3} + \frac{4|x|^2ds^2}{s^4} + \frac{4ds^2}{s^4} = \frac{4|dx|^2}{s^2} - \frac{16(x \cdot dx)^2}{s^3} + \frac{4s \cdot 4(x \cdot dx)^2}{s^4} = \frac{4|dx|^2}{s^2}, \]

where \(|dx|^2 = \sum _kdx_k^2\) and products of \(1\)-forms mean symmetric products. So \(F\) is an isometry from \(\mathbb {R}^n\) with this metric onto \(S^n\) minus the north pole, and the curvature of \(S^n\) is that of Corollary 39.8.6(3).

02NZ Exercise 39.15.14

Let \(f\) be a positive function on an interval \(I\) and \(g = f(x_n)^2(dx_1^2 + \cdots + dx_n^2)\) on \(\{ x \in \mathbb {R}^n : x_n \in I\} \), \(n \geq 3\). Show that in the frame \(e_i = f^{-1}\partial _i\) the sectional curvatures are

\[ K(e_i, e_j) = -\frac{f^{\prime 2}}{f^4}\quad (i, j {\lt} n), \qquad K(e_i, e_n) = \frac{f^{\prime 2} - ff^{\prime \prime }}{f^4}\quad (i {\lt} n), \]

and that \(g\) has constant curvature if and only if \(f(t) = 1/(a + bt)\) for constants \(a\), \(b\), the curvature being \(-b^2\). Which metric is obtained for \(a = 0\), \(b = 1\)?

Solution â–¼

Apply Proposition 39.8.5 with \(u = \log f(x_n)\): \(u_i = \delta _{in}f^{\prime }/f\), \(u_{ik} = \delta _{in}\delta _{kn}(f^{\prime }/f)^{\prime }\), so \(\alpha _i = \delta _{in}\mu \, dx_n\) with \(\mu = (f^{\prime }/f)^{\prime } - (f^{\prime }/f)^2\), and \(|\nabla u|^2 = (f^{\prime }/f)^2\). For \(i, j {\lt} n\), \(\alpha _i = \alpha _j = 0\) and \(\Omega ^i_j = -(f^{\prime }/f)^2dx_i \wedge dx_j = -(f^{\prime 2}/f^4)\, \omega ^i \wedge \omega ^j\). For \(i {\lt} n\), \(\Omega ^i_n = -\mu \, dx_i \wedge dx_n - (f^{\prime }/f)^2dx_i \wedge dx_n = -(f^{\prime }/f)^{\prime }dx_i \wedge dx_n\), and \(-(f^{\prime }/f)^{\prime }/f^2 = (f^{\prime 2} - ff^{\prime \prime })/f^4\). Since every \(\Omega ^i_j\) is a multiple of \(\omega ^i \wedge \omega ^j\), the curvature is constant \(c\) if and only if both expressions equal \(c\) (Theorem 39.8.2(4)). The first forces \(c = -b^2 \leq 0\) and \(f^{\prime }/f^2 = \pm b\), that is \((1/f)^{\prime } = \mp b\), so \(f = 1/(a + bt)\) after renaming \(b\); and for this \(f\), \(f^{\prime } = -b/(a + bt)^2\), \(f^{\prime \prime } = 2b^2/(a + bt)^3\), \(f^{\prime 2} - ff^{\prime \prime } = -b^2/(a + bt)^4\), so the second expression is \(-b^2\) as well. For \(a = 0\), \(b = 1\) we get \(f = 1/x_n\): the upper half-space model of hyperbolic space.

02O0 Exercise 39.15.15

On \(\mathbb {R}^3\) consider the metric \(g = dx^2 + dy^2 + (dz - x\, dy)^2\) (a left-invariant metric on the Heisenberg group). With the coframe \(\omega ^1 = dx\), \(\omega ^2 = dy\), \(\omega ^3 = dz - x\, dy\), find the connection forms, and show that the sectional curvatures of the planes spanned by pairs of frame vectors are \(K(e_1, e_2) = -\frac34\) and \(K(e_1, e_3) = K(e_2, e_3) = \frac14\). Compute the Ricci and scalar curvatures.

Solution â–¼

\(d\omega ^1 = d\omega ^2 = 0\) and \(d\omega ^3 = -dx \wedge dy = -\omega ^1 \wedge \omega ^2\). Try \(\omega ^1_2 = a\, \omega ^3\), \(\omega ^1_3 = b\, \omega ^2\), \(\omega ^2_3 = c\, \omega ^1\). The first structure equation for \(i = 1, 2, 3\) gives \((a - b)\, \omega ^2 \wedge \omega ^3 = 0\), \(-(a + c)\, \omega ^1 \wedge \omega ^3 = 0\) and \(-(b - c)\, \omega ^1 \wedge \omega ^2 = -\omega ^1 \wedge \omega ^2\), so \(a = b = \frac12\), \(c = -\frac12\); by uniqueness these are the connection forms. Then

\[ \Omega ^1_2 = d\omega ^1_2 + \omega ^1_3 \wedge \omega ^3_2 = -\tfrac 12\omega ^1 \wedge \omega ^2 + \tfrac 12\omega ^2 \wedge \tfrac 12\omega ^1 = -\tfrac 34\omega ^1 \wedge \omega ^2, \]

\(\Omega ^1_3 = d\omega ^1_3 + \omega ^1_2 \wedge \omega ^2_3 = \tfrac 12\omega ^3 \wedge (-\tfrac 12\omega ^1) = \tfrac 14\omega ^1 \wedge \omega ^3\) and \(\Omega ^2_3 = d\omega ^2_3 + \omega ^2_1 \wedge \omega ^1_3 = -\tfrac 12\omega ^3 \wedge \tfrac 12\omega ^2 = \tfrac 14\omega ^2 \wedge \omega ^3\). Each \(\Omega ^i_j\) is a multiple of \(\omega ^i \wedge \omega ^j\), so \(R(e_k, e_l, e_j, e_i) = 0\) unless \(\{ k, l\} = \{ i, j\} \), and \(\mathrm{Ric}(e_i, e_j) = \sum _kR(e_k, e_i, e_j, e_k)\) is diagonal, with \(\mathrm{Ric}(e_1, e_1) = K_{12} + K_{13} = -\frac12\), \(\mathrm{Ric}(e_2, e_2) = -\frac12\), \(\mathrm{Ric}(e_3, e_3) = \frac12\), and scalar curvature \(-\frac12\). So this homogeneous metric has sectional curvatures of both signs.

02O1 Exercise 39.15.16

Let \((\theta ^a)_{a {\lt} n}\) be an orthonormal coframe on an open subset of the round \(S^{n-1}\), \(n \geq 3\), with connection forms \(\theta ^a_b\), so that \(d\theta ^a_b + \sum _c\theta ^a_c \wedge \theta ^c_b = \theta ^a \wedge \theta ^b\). On \((0, L) \times S^{n-1}\) consider \(g = dr^2 + f(r)^2g_{S^{n-1}}\) with \(f {\gt} 0\), and the coframe \(\omega ^a = f\theta ^a\), \(\omega ^n = dr\). Show that \(\omega ^a_b = \theta ^a_b\) and \(\omega ^a_n = f^{\prime }\theta ^a\), and that

\[ K(e_a, e_n) = -\frac{f^{\prime \prime }}{f}, \qquad K(e_a, e_b) = \frac{1 - f^{\prime 2}}{f^2} . \]

Check that \(f(r) = r\), \(\sin r\) and \(\sinh r\) give constant curvature \(0\), \(1\) and \(-1\).

Solution â–¼

\(d\omega ^a = f^{\prime }dr \wedge \theta ^a + f\, d\theta ^a = -\frac{f^{\prime }}{f}\omega ^a \wedge \omega ^n - f\sum _b\theta ^a_b \wedge \theta ^b\), and with the proposed forms \(-\sum _b\omega ^a_b \wedge \omega ^b - \omega ^a_n \wedge \omega ^n = -f\sum _b\theta ^a_b \wedge \theta ^b - \frac{f^{\prime }}{f}\omega ^a \wedge \omega ^n\), which agrees; also \(d\omega ^n = 0\) and \(-\sum _a\omega ^n_a \wedge \omega ^a = \sum _af^{\prime }\theta ^a \wedge f\theta ^a = 0\). So these are the connection forms. Then \(\Omega ^a_b = d\theta ^a_b + \sum _c\theta ^a_c \wedge \theta ^c_b + \omega ^a_n \wedge \omega ^n_b = \theta ^a \wedge \theta ^b - f^{\prime 2}\theta ^a \wedge \theta ^b = \frac{1 - f^{\prime 2}}{f^2}\omega ^a \wedge \omega ^b\), and \(\Omega ^a_n = d(f^{\prime }\theta ^a) + \sum _c\theta ^a_c \wedge f^{\prime }\theta ^c = f^{\prime \prime }dr \wedge \theta ^a + f^{\prime }\bigl(d\theta ^a + \sum _c\theta ^a_c \wedge \theta ^c\bigr) = -\frac{f^{\prime \prime }}{f}\omega ^a \wedge \omega ^n\), using the first structure equation of the sphere. For \(f = r\): \(0\) and \((1 - 1)/r^2 = 0\); for \(f = \sin r\): \(1\) and \((1 - \cos ^2r)/\sin ^2r = 1\); for \(f = \sinh r\): \(-1\) and \((1 - \cosh ^2r)/\sinh ^2r = -1\).

02O2 Exercise 39.15.17

Let \(M\) be a compact oriented \(4\)-manifold with a metric of constant curvature \(\kappa \). Show that \(\chi (M) = 3\kappa ^2\operatorname {vol}(M)/(4\pi ^2)\), and deduce that \(T^4\) and \(S^1 \times S^3\) carry no metric of constant curvature \(\kappa \neq 0\). Check the formula directly for the product \(S^2 \times S^2\) of two unit spheres, which does not have constant curvature: compute \(\mathrm{Pf}(\Omega )\) in a product frame and show that \(\frac{1}{4\pi ^2}\int \mathrm{Pf}(\Omega ) = 4 = \chi (S^2 \times S^2)\).

Solution â–¼

The first statement is Corollary 39.8.9(2) with \(m = 2\), \((2m-1)!! = 3\). By the Künneth formula \(\chi (T^4) = \chi (S^1)^4 = 0\) and \(\chi (S^1 \times S^3) = 0\), while \(3\kappa ^2\operatorname {vol}/(4\pi ^2) {\gt} 0\) for \(\kappa \neq 0\). For \(S^2 \times S^2\) take orthonormal coframes \(\omega ^1, \omega ^2\) of the first factor and \(\omega ^3, \omega ^4\) of the second, pulled back. The connection forms of the factors, placed in two diagonal blocks, are skew-symmetric and satisfy the first structure equation, so they are the connection forms of the product. The curvature is block diagonal, \(\Omega ^1_2 = \omega ^1 \wedge \omega ^2\), \(\Omega ^3_4 = \omega ^3 \wedge \omega ^4\) and \(\Omega ^1_3 = \Omega ^1_4 = \Omega ^2_3 = \Omega ^2_4 = 0\), so \(\mathrm{Pf}(\Omega ) = \Omega ^1_2 \wedge \Omega ^3_4 = dA_1 \wedge dA_2\), with integral \((4\pi )^2\), and \(16\pi ^2/(4\pi ^2) = 4 = \chi (S^2)^2\). (The plane spanned by \(e_1\) and \(e_3\) has curvature \(0\), the one spanned by \(e_1\) and \(e_2\) curvature \(1\).)

02O8 Exercise 39.15.18

This exercise relates three descriptions of hyperbolic space, each with its natural chart: the upper half-space \(H^n = \{ x \in \mathbb {R}^n : x_n {\gt} 0\} \) with the metric \(|dx|^2/x_n^2\), where \(|dx|^2 = dx_1^2 + \cdots + dx_n^2\) and the chart is the inclusion \(H^n \subseteq \mathbb {R}^n\); the ball \(B^n = \{ |y| {\lt} 1\} \) with the metric \(4|dy|^2/(1 - |y|^2)^2\), where the chart is the identity of \(B^n\); and the hyperboloid \(L = \{ x \in \mathbb {R}^{n+1} : x_1^2 + \cdots + x_n^2 - x_{n+1}^2 = -1,\ x_{n+1} {\gt} 0\} \), where the chart is the projection \(p(x) = (x_1, \ldots , x_n)\).

  1. (\(n = 2\), starting from the half-plane.) Show that the Cayley map \(C(z) = i\frac{1 + z}{1 - z}\) is a diffeomorphism from the unit disc onto the upper half-plane, with inverse \(w \mapsto \frac{w - i}{w + i}\), and compute \(C^*\bigl(|dw|^2/(\operatorname {Im}w)^2\bigr)\), finding the metric \(4|dz|^2/(1 - |z|^2)^2\) of the disc. So the disc model is the half-plane model transported by \(C\).

  2. (Any \(n\).) Let \(\sigma (x) = -e_n + 2(x + e_n)/|x + e_n|^2\), the inversion in the sphere of radius \(\sqrt2\) centred at \(-e_n\). Show that \(\sigma \) maps \(H^n\) diffeomorphically onto \(B^n\) and that \(\sigma ^*\bigl(4|dy|^2/(1 - |y|^2)^2\bigr) = |dx|^2/x_n^2\).

  3. Show that \(p\) maps \(L\) diffeomorphically onto \(\mathbb {R}^n\), that the restriction to \(L\) of the Lorentz form \(dx_1^2 + \cdots + dx_n^2 - dx_{n+1}^2\) is positive definite, and that in the chart \(p\), with coordinates \(u = p(x)\), it is \(|du|^2 - (u \cdot du)^2/(1 + |u|^2)\), where \(u \cdot du = \sum _iu_i\, du_i\).

  4. Show that the map \(y \mapsto x = \bigl(2y, 1 + |y|^2\bigr)/(1 - |y|^2)\) is a diffeomorphism from \(B^n\) onto \(L\), inverse to \(x \mapsto (x_1, \ldots , x_n)/(1 + x_{n+1})\) (the projection from the point \((0, \ldots , 0, -1)\)), which pulls the Lorentz metric of \(L\) back to \(4|dy|^2/(1 - |y|^2)^2\). Conclude that the three models are isometric, and that all have constant curvature \(-1\).

Solution â–¼

(1) \(C\) is holomorphic on \(\{ z \neq 1\} \) with \(C^{\prime }(z) = 2i/(1 - z)^2\). Writing \(\frac{1 + z}{1 - z} = \frac{(1 + z)(1 - \bar z)}{|1 - z|^2}\) and \((1 + z)(1 - \bar z) = 1 - |z|^2 + (z - \bar z)\), we get \(\operatorname {Im}C(z) = \operatorname {Re}\frac{1 + z}{1 - z} = \frac{1 - |z|^2}{|1 - z|^2}\), which is positive exactly for \(|z| {\lt} 1\). Solving \(w = i(1 + z)/(1 - z)\) for \(z\) gives \(z = (w - i)/(w + i)\), defined on the half-plane, and \(|(w - i)/(w + i)| {\lt} 1\) there since \(w\) is closer to \(i\) than to \(-i\); so \(C\) is a bijection onto the half-plane with holomorphic, hence smooth, inverse. The differential of a holomorphic map is multiplication by the complex derivative, which by the Cauchy–Riemann equations (Proposition 28.2.2) multiplies lengths by its modulus; so \(C^*|dw|^2 = |C^{\prime }(z)|^2|dz|^2 = 4|dz|^2/|1 - z|^4\), and

\[ C^*\frac{|dw|^2}{(\operatorname {Im}w)^2} = \frac{4|dz|^2}{|1 - z|^4} \cdot \frac{|1 - z|^4}{(1 - |z|^2)^2} = \frac{4|dz|^2}{(1 - |z|^2)^2} . \]

(2) Write \(v = x + e_n\). The inversion \(J(x) = c + r^2(x - c)/|x - c|^2\), here with \(c = -e_n\) and \(r^2 = 2\), is an involution of \(\mathbb {R}^n \setminus \{ c\} \), and its differential is \(dJ = \frac{r^2}{|v|^2}\bigl(I - 2\frac{vv^T}{|v|^2}\bigr)\), a reflection times \(r^2/|v|^2\) (compare Example 34.2.7); so \(J^*|dy|^2 = r^4|dx|^2/|v|^4 = 4|dx|^2/|x + e_n|^4\). Moreover

\[ |\sigma (x)|^2 = 1 - \frac{4\langle v, e_n\rangle }{|v|^2} + \frac{4}{|v|^2} = 1 - \frac{4x_n}{|x + e_n|^2}, \qquad \text{so}\qquad 1 - |\sigma (x)|^2 = \frac{4x_n}{|x + e_n|^2} . \]

Hence \(\sigma \) maps \(H^n\) into \(B^n\), and, being an involution of \(\mathbb {R}^n \setminus \{ -e_n\} \) mapping \(\{ x_n {\gt} 0\} \) into \(\{ |y| {\lt} 1\} \) and (by the same formula read backwards) \(\{ |y| {\lt} 1\} \) into \(\{ x_n {\gt} 0\} \), it is a diffeomorphism \(H^n \to B^n\). Finally \(\sigma ^*\bigl(4|dy|^2/(1 - |y|^2)^2\bigr) = 4 \cdot \frac{4|dx|^2}{|x + e_n|^4} \cdot \frac{|x + e_n|^4}{16x_n^2} = \frac{|dx|^2}{x_n^2}\).

(3) The inverse of \(p|_L\) is \(u \mapsto (u, (1 + |u|^2)^{1/2})\), smooth on \(\mathbb {R}^n\), so \(p\) is a diffeomorphism and \(p\) is a chart of the submanifold \(L\). On \(L\), \(x_{n+1} = (1 + |u|^2)^{1/2}\) and \(dx_{n+1} = (u \cdot du)/(1 + |u|^2)^{1/2}\), so the Lorentz form restricts to \(|du|^2 - (u \cdot du)^2/(1 + |u|^2)\). By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, \((u \cdot \xi )^2 \leq |u|^2|\xi |^2 {\lt} (1 + |u|^2)|\xi |^2\) for \(\xi \neq 0\), so this is positive definite.

(4) Let \(t = 1 - |y|^2\), so \(x^{\prime } = (x_1, \ldots , x_n) = 2y/t\) and \(x_{n+1} = (1 + |y|^2)/t = 2/t - 1\). Then \(|x^{\prime }|^2 - x_{n+1}^2 = (4|y|^2 - (1 + |y|^2)^2)/t^2 = -1\) and \(x_{n+1} {\gt} 0\), so the point is on \(L\); and \(x^{\prime }/(1 + x_{n+1}) = (2y/t)/(2/t) = y\), which shows that the two maps are inverse to each other (the second one maps \(L\) into \(B^n\), as \(|x^{\prime }|^2 = x_{n+1}^2 - 1 {\lt} (1 + x_{n+1})^2\)). With \(dt = -2\, y \cdot dy\),

\[ \begin{aligned} |dx^{\prime }|^2 - dx_{n+1}^2 & = \Bigl|\frac{2\, dy}{t} - \frac{2y\, dt}{t^2}\Bigr|^2 - \frac{4\, dt^2}{t^4} = \frac{4|dy|^2}{t^2} - \frac{8(y \cdot dy)\, dt}{t^3} + \frac{4(|y|^2 - 1)\, dt^2}{t^4}\\ & = \frac{4|dy|^2}{t^2} + \frac{16(y \cdot dy)^2}{t^3} - \frac{16(y \cdot dy)^2}{t^3} = \frac{4|dy|^2}{(1 - |y|^2)^2} . \end{aligned} \]

So the ball, the half-space and the hyperboloid are isometric (by (2) and (4)), and they have constant curvature \(-1\) by Corollary 39.8.6.
