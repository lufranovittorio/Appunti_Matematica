17.6 Compactness00S5

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

00S6 Definition 17.6.1

An open cover of a space \(X\) is a family of open sets whose union is \(X\). The space \(X\) is compact if every open cover has a finite subcover. A subset is compact if it is compact as a subspace, that is, if every cover of it by open subsets of \(X\) has a finite subcover.

00S7 Proposition 17.6.2

  1. A closed subset of a compact space is compact.

  2. In a Hausdorff space, disjoint compact subsets \(K\), \(L\) have disjoint open neighbourhoods; in particular compact subsets are closed.

  3. Continuous images of compact spaces are compact.

  4. A continuous map from a compact space to a Hausdorff space is closed. If it is bijective, it is a homeomorphism; if it is injective, it is an embedding; if it is surjective, it is a quotient map.

Proof â–¼

(1) An open cover of \(C \subseteq X\), together with \(X \setminus C\), covers \(X\). (2) First let \(L = \{ y\} \). For each \(k \in K\) choose disjoint open \(U_k \ni k\) and \(V_k \ni y\); finitely many \(U_{k_1}, \ldots , U_{k_m}\) cover \(K\), and \(U = \bigcup _j U_{k_j}\) and \(V = \bigcap _j V_{k_j}\) are disjoint. For general \(L\), do this for each \(y \in L\) and use a finite subcover of \(L\) by the resulting \(V\)’s in the same way. (3) Preimages of an open cover of \(f(X)\) cover \(X\). (4) Closed subsets are compact by (1), their images are compact by (3), hence closed by (2). A closed continuous bijection has continuous inverse; for an injection apply this to \(X \to f(X)\), and for a surjection use Proposition 17.4.9.

00S8 Proposition 17.6.3

If \(X\) and \(Y\) are compact, so is \(X \times Y\).

Proof â–¼

Let \(\mathcal{W}\) be an open cover of \(X \times Y\) and fix \(y \in Y\). For each \(x \in X\) choose \(W_x \in \mathcal{W}\) containing \((x, y)\) and a basic open set \(U_x \times V_x\) with \((x, y) \in U_x \times V_x \subseteq W_x\). Finitely many \(U_{x_1}, \ldots , U_{x_m}\) cover \(X\); let \(N_y = \bigcap _j V_{x_j}\). Then \(X \times N_y\) is covered by \(W_{x_1}, \ldots , W_{x_m}\) (the tube lemma). Finitely many \(N_y\) cover \(Y\), and the corresponding finitely many members of \(\mathcal{W}\) cover \(X \times Y\).

00S9 Theorem 17.6.4 Heine–Borel

Every closed bounded interval \([a, b] \subseteq \mathbb {R}\) is compact. A subset of \(\mathbb {R}^n\) (or \(\mathbb {C}^n = \mathbb {R}^{2n}\)) is compact if and only if it is closed and bounded.

Proof â–¼

Let \(\mathcal{U}\) be an open cover of \([a, b]\) and \(S\) the set of \(t \in [a, b]\) such that \([a, t]\) is covered by finitely many members. Then \(a \in S\); let \(s = \sup S\). Choose \(U \in \mathcal{U}\) with \(s \in U\) and \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\) with \((s - \varepsilon , s + \varepsilon ) \cap [a, b] \subseteq U\), and \(t \in S\) with \(t {\gt} s - \varepsilon \). A finite cover of \([a, t]\) together with \(U\) covers \([a, t']\) for \(t' = \min (s + \varepsilon /2, b)\), so \(t' \in S\) and \(t' \leq s\). Hence \(s = b \in S\).

For the second statement, a compact \(K \subseteq \mathbb {R}^n\) is closed by Proposition 17.6.2(2) and bounded, since the balls \(B(0, m)\) cover it. A closed bounded set lies in some cube \([-R, R]^n\), which is compact as a finite product of compact spaces by Proposition 17.6.3 (with the product topology, which is the Euclidean one by Proposition 17.4.6(4)); so it is compact by Proposition 17.6.2(1).

00SA Corollary 17.6.5 Extreme value theorem

A continuous function \(f \colon X \to \mathbb {R}\) on a nonempty compact space attains a maximum and a minimum.

Proof â–¼

\(f(X)\) is compact, hence closed and bounded, so it contains its supremum and infimum, which lie in its closure.

00SB Lemma 17.6.6 Alexander subbasis lemma

Let \(\mathcal{S}\) be a subbasis of the topology of \(X\). If every cover of \(X\) by members of \(\mathcal{S}\) has a finite subcover, then \(X\) is compact.

Proof â–¼

Suppose not. The set of open covers of \(X\) without finite subcover is nonempty, and ordered by inclusion every chain in it has an upper bound, its union: a finite subcover of the union would lie in one member of the chain. By Zorn’s lemma (Theorem 3.7.2) there is a maximal open cover \(\mathcal{C}\) without finite subcover. The members of \(\mathcal{C} \cap \mathcal{S}\) do not cover \(X\), since otherwise they would have a finite subcover. Choose \(x\) not covered by them and \(U \in \mathcal{C}\) with \(x \in U\). There are \(S_1, \ldots , S_k \in \mathcal{S}\) with \(x \in S_1 \cap \cdots \cap S_k \subseteq U\), and no \(S_j\) belongs to \(\mathcal{C}\). By maximality, \(\mathcal{C} \cup \{ S_j\} \) has a finite subcover, so \(X = S_j \cup Y_j\) with \(Y_j\) a finite union of members of \(\mathcal{C}\). Then \(X = (S_1 \cap \cdots \cap S_k) \cup Y_1 \cup \cdots \cup Y_k \subseteq U \cup Y_1 \cup \cdots \cup Y_k\) is covered by finitely many members of \(\mathcal{C}\), a contradiction. (If \(k = 0\), then \(U = X\) is itself a finite subcover.)

00SC Theorem 17.6.7 Tychonoff

Every product of compact spaces is compact.

Proof â–¼

Let \(X = \prod _i X_i\). If some \(X_i\) is empty, so is \(X\). Otherwise, by Lemma 17.6.6 it suffices to consider a cover \(\mathcal{S}\) of \(X\) by subbasic sets \(p_i^{-1}(U)\). For each \(i\) let \(\mathcal{U}_i\) be the set of open \(U \subseteq X_i\) with \(p_i^{-1}(U) \in \mathcal{S}\). If for some \(i\) the family \(\mathcal{U}_i\) covers \(X_i\), then a finite subcover of \(\mathcal{U}_i\) gives a finite subcover of \(\mathcal{S}\). Otherwise choose, by the axiom of choice, \(x_i \in X_i\) not covered by \(\mathcal{U}_i\) for each \(i\); then the point \((x_i)_i\) is not covered by \(\mathcal{S}\), a contradiction.

00SD Remark 17.6.8

Tychonoff’s theorem implies the axiom of choice over ZF [Kel50], so the use of choice in its proof cannot be avoided.

00SE Example 17.6.9

The sphere \(S^n\) is compact by Theorem 17.6.4, and so is \(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^n\), a continuous image of \(S^n\). The quotient \([0, 4]/\{ 0, 4\} \) is homeomorphic to \(S^1\). Indeed, let \(Q = \{ x \in \mathbb {R}^2 : \| x\| _\infty = 1\} \) be the boundary of the square. The maps \(x \mapsto x/\| x\| _2\) and \(x \mapsto x/\| x\| _\infty \) are mutually inverse continuous maps \(Q \to S^1\) and \(S^1 \to Q\). The map \([0, 4] \to Q\) running affinely along the four sides, from \((1, -1)\) to \((1, 1)\), \((-1, 1)\), \((-1, -1)\) and back, is a continuous surjection identifying only \(0\) and \(4\); the induced continuous bijection \([0, 4]/\{ 0, 4\} \to Q\) from a compact space to a Hausdorff space is a homeomorphism by Proposition 17.6.2(4).

00SF Counterexample 17.6.10

  1. An infinite set \(X\) with the metric \(d(x, y) = 1\) for \(x \neq y\) is closed and bounded in itself but not compact: its topology is discrete, and the cover by singletons has no finite subcover.

  2. In the cofinite topology on \(\mathbb {N}\) every subset is compact (any member of a cover contains all but finitely many points), and the set of even numbers is compact but not closed. Hausdorffness in Proposition 17.6.2(2) is needed.
