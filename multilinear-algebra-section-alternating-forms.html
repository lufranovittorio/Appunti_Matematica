11.4 Alternating forms00KC

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

In this section \(V\) has finite dimension \(n\).

00KD Proposition 11.4.1

The pairing \(\Lambda ^p(V^*) \times \Lambda ^p(V) \to k\) determined by \(\langle \varphi _1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \varphi _p, v_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge v_p \rangle = \det (\varphi _i(v_j))\) is well defined and perfect. Hence \(\Lambda ^p(V^*) \cong \Lambda ^p(V)^*\), which by Proposition 11.3.2 is the space \(\mathrm{Alt}^p(V)\) of alternating \(p\)-linear forms on \(V\).

Proof â–¼

The determinant is multilinear and alternating both in the \(\varphi _i\) and in the \(v_j\) (using Proposition 10.5.3(2)), so it induces a bilinear pairing. For a basis \((e_i)\) with dual basis \((e_i^*)\), \(\langle e_I^*, e_J \rangle = \delta _{IJ}\), so the pairing is perfect.

00KE Notation 11.4.2

We identify \(\Lambda ^p(V^*)\) with \(\mathrm{Alt}^p(V)\) via Proposition 11.4.1: an element \(\alpha \in \Lambda ^p(V^*)\) is evaluated on vectors as \(\alpha (v_1, \ldots , v_p) = \langle \alpha , v_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge v_p \rangle \). With this convention, \((\varphi _1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \varphi _p)(v_1, \ldots , v_p) = \det (\varphi _i(v_j))\), without a factor \(1/p!\).

00KF Proposition 11.4.3

For \(\alpha \in \Lambda ^p(V^*)\) and \(\beta \in \Lambda ^q(V^*)\),

\[ (\alpha \wedge \beta )(v_1, \ldots , v_{p+q}) = \sum _{\sigma } \operatorname {sgn}(\sigma )\, \alpha (v_{\sigma (1)}, \ldots , v_{\sigma (p)})\, \beta (v_{\sigma (p+1)}, \ldots , v_{\sigma (p+q)}), \]

the sum running over the shuffles: permutations \(\sigma \in S_{p+q}\) with \(\sigma (1) {\lt} \cdots {\lt} \sigma (p)\) and \(\sigma (p+1) {\lt} \cdots {\lt} \sigma (p+q)\).

Proof â–¼

Both sides are bilinear in \((\alpha , \beta )\), so we may take \(\alpha = \varphi _1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \varphi _p\) and \(\beta = \varphi _{p+1} \wedge \cdots \wedge \varphi _{p+q}\). The left side is \(\det M\) with \(M = (\varphi _i(v_j))\). In the Leibniz formula \(\det M = \sum _\pi \operatorname {sgn}(\pi )\prod _j M_{\pi (j) j}\), group the permutations \(\pi \) according to the set \(A = \{ j : \pi (j) \leq p\} \) of columns sent to the first \(p\) rows. For fixed \(A = \{ a_1 {\lt} \cdots {\lt} a_p\} \) with complement \(\{ b_1 {\lt} \cdots {\lt} b_q\} \), let \(\sigma \) be the shuffle with \(\sigma (i) = a_i\) and \(\sigma (p + i) = b_i\). Every such \(\pi \) is uniquely \(\pi = (\pi _1 \times \pi _2) \circ \sigma ^{-1}\) with \(\pi _1 \in S_p\) acting on \(\{ 1, \ldots , p\} \) and \(\pi _2\) on \(\{ p+1, \ldots , p+q\} \), and \(\operatorname {sgn}\pi = \operatorname {sgn}\pi _1 \operatorname {sgn}\pi _2 \operatorname {sgn}\sigma \). Summing over \(\pi _1\) and \(\pi _2\) gives \(\operatorname {sgn}(\sigma )\det (\varphi _i(v_{a_j}))\det (\varphi _{p+i}(v_{b_j}))\), which is the term of \(\sigma \) on the right.

00KG Definition 11.4.4

For \(v \in V\) and \(\alpha \in \Lambda ^p(V^*)\) with \(p \geq 1\), the interior product \(\iota _v\alpha \in \Lambda ^{p-1}(V^*)\) is \((\iota _v\alpha )(v_2, \ldots , v_p) = \alpha (v, v_2, \ldots , v_p)\); for \(p = 0\), \(\iota _v\alpha = 0\).

00KH Proposition 11.4.5

\(\iota _v(\alpha \wedge \beta ) = \iota _v\alpha \wedge \beta + (-1)^p\alpha \wedge \iota _v\beta \) for \(\alpha \in \Lambda ^p(V^*)\), and \(\iota _v \iota _w = -\iota _w\iota _v\).

Proof â–¼

Expanding the determinant along the first column (Laplace expansion) gives \(\iota _v(\varphi _1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \varphi _p) = \sum _{i=1}^p (-1)^{i-1}\varphi _i(v)\, \varphi _1 \wedge \cdots \widehat{\varphi _i} \cdots \wedge \varphi _p\). Applying this formula to a product of decomposable forms and splitting the sum according to whether \(i\) is among the first \(p\) factors gives the first identity. The second holds because \(\alpha (w, v, \ldots ) = -\alpha (v, w, \ldots )\).

00KI Proposition 11.4.6

Suppose \(\operatorname {char} k = 0\), and let \(\omega \in \Lambda ^2(V^*)\) be nondegenerate, \(\dim V = 2m\). Then \(\omega ^m = \omega \wedge \cdots \wedge \omega \neq 0\), and every \(f \in \mathrm{GL}(V)\) with \(\omega (fv, fw) = \omega (v, w)\) has \(\det f = 1\).

Proof â–¼

With a symplectic basis (Theorem 10.8.6) and its dual basis, \(\omega = \sum _i e_i^* \wedge f_i^*\), as both sides take the same values on pairs of basis vectors. The \(2\)-forms \(e_i^* \wedge f_i^*\) commute and square to zero, so \(\omega ^m = m!\, e_1^* \wedge f_1^* \wedge \cdots \wedge e_m^* \wedge f_m^* \neq 0\). The map \(f^* \colon V^* \to V^*\), \(\varphi \mapsto \varphi \circ f\), satisfies \((\Lambda ^2 f^*)(\omega ) = \omega \), hence \((\Lambda ^{2m}f^*)(\omega ^m) = \omega ^m\); but \(\Lambda ^{2m}f^*\) is multiplication by \(\det f^* = \det f\) (Proposition 11.3.3(5) and Proposition 10.4.2(3)).

The same computation, applied to an arbitrary alternating form instead of a nondegenerate one, produces a square root of the determinant of a skew-symmetric matrix. In the rest of this section \(k\) is a field of characteristic \(0\), \(V = k^{2m}\) with standard basis \((e_i)\) and dual basis \((e_i^*)\). To a skew-symmetric matrix \(A \in M_{2m}(k)\), that is \(A^T = -A\), attach the \(2\)-form

\[ \alpha _A = \sum _{i {\lt} j}A_{ij}\, e_i^* \wedge e_j^* = \frac12\sum _{i, j}A_{ij}\, e_i^* \wedge e_j^* \in \Lambda ^2(V^*) . \]

The two expressions agree because \(A_{ii} = 0\) and \(A_{ji}\, e_j^* \wedge e_i^* = A_{ij}\, e_i^* \wedge e_j^*\). As an alternating form (Notation 11.4.2), \(\alpha _A(v, w) = \sum _{i {\lt} j}A_{ij}(v_iw_j - v_jw_i) = \sum _{i, j}A_{ij}v_iw_j = v^TAw\). Since \(\Lambda ^{2m}(V^*)\) is one-dimensional, spanned by \(e_1^* \wedge \cdots \wedge e_{2m}^*\) (Proposition 11.3.3(3)), the following definition makes sense.

02N0 Definition 11.4.7

The Pfaffian of a skew-symmetric matrix \(A \in M_{2m}(k)\) is the scalar \(\mathrm{Pf}(A)\) with

\[ \alpha _A^m = \alpha _A \wedge \cdots \wedge \alpha _A = m!\, \mathrm{Pf}(A)\, e_1^* \wedge \cdots \wedge e_{2m}^* . \]
02N1 Proposition 11.4.8

Let \(A \in M_{2m}(k)\) be skew-symmetric.

  1. \(\displaystyle \mathrm{Pf}(A) = \frac{1}{2^mm!}\sum _{\sigma \in S_{2m}}\operatorname {sgn}(\sigma )\, A_{\sigma (1)\sigma (2)}A_{\sigma (3)\sigma (4)} \cdots A_{\sigma (2m-1)\sigma (2m)}\). So \(\mathrm{Pf}\) is a homogeneous polynomial of degree \(m\) in the entries, with \(\mathrm{Pf}(\lambda A) = \lambda ^m\mathrm{Pf}(A)\); for instance

    \[ \mathrm{Pf}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & a \\ -a & 0 \end{pmatrix} = a, \qquad \mathrm{Pf}(A) = A_{12}A_{34} - A_{13}A_{24} + A_{14}A_{23} \quad (m = 2). \]

  2. \(\mathrm{Pf}(g^TAg) = \det (g)\, \mathrm{Pf}(A)\) for every \(g \in M_{2m}(k)\). In particular \(\mathrm{Pf}\) is invariant under conjugation by \(\mathrm{SO}(2m)\), and changes sign under conjugation by an orthogonal matrix of determinant \(-1\).

  3. If \(A\) is block diagonal with blocks \(\begin{pmatrix} 0 & a_i \\ -a_i & 0 \end{pmatrix}\), \(i = 1, \ldots , m\), then \(\mathrm{Pf}(A) = a_1a_2 \cdots a_m\).

  4. \(\mathrm{Pf}(A)^2 = \det A\).

Proof â–¼

(1) Expanding the \(m\)-th power of \(\alpha _A = \frac12\sum _{i,j}A_{ij}e_i^* \wedge e_j^*\) gives

\[ \alpha _A^m = \frac{1}{2^m}\sum _{i_1, j_1, \ldots , i_m, j_m}A_{i_1j_1} \cdots A_{i_mj_m}\, e_{i_1}^* \wedge e_{j_1}^* \wedge \cdots \wedge e_{i_m}^* \wedge e_{j_m}^* . \]

A wedge product of \(1\)-forms with a repeated factor vanishes, so only the terms in which \((i_1, j_1, \ldots , i_m, j_m) = (\sigma (1), \ldots , \sigma (2m))\) for a permutation \(\sigma \) survive, and for them \(e_{\sigma (1)}^* \wedge \cdots \wedge e_{\sigma (2m)}^* = \operatorname {sgn}(\sigma )\, e_1^* \wedge \cdots \wedge e_{2m}^*\) by Proposition 11.3.3(1). Comparing with the definition gives the formula. For \(m = 1\) the two permutations give \(\frac12(A_{12} - A_{21}) = A_{12}\). In general the \(2^mm!\) permutations that differ by reordering the \(m\) pairs (an even permutation of positions) and by swapping the two entries of some pairs (which changes both \(\operatorname {sgn}\sigma \) and the sign of one factor) give the same term, so \(\mathrm{Pf}(A)\) is the sum over the ways of splitting \(\{ 1, \ldots , 2m\} \) into \(m\) pairs \(\{ a_l {\lt} b_l\} \) of \(\pm \prod _lA_{a_lb_l}\), the sign being that of the permutation \((a_1, b_1, \ldots , a_m, b_m)\). For \(m = 2\) the three splittings \(\{ 12, 34\} \), \(\{ 13, 24\} \), \(\{ 14, 23\} \) give the permutations \(1234\), \(1324\), \(1423\), of signs \(+1\), \(-1\), \(+1\).

(2) Let \(g^* \colon V^* \to V^*\), \(\varphi \mapsto \varphi \circ g\), where \(g\) acts on \(V = k^{2m}\) by matrix multiplication. Then \(g^*e_k^* = \sum _ig_{ki}e_i^*\), because \(e_k^*(ge_i) = g_{ki}\). The induced map \(\Lambda g^*\) on \(\Lambda (V^*)\) is multiplicative, and

\[ (\Lambda ^2g^*)(\alpha _A) = \frac12\sum _{k, l}A_{kl}\Bigl(\sum _ig_{ki}e_i^*\Bigr) \wedge \Bigl(\sum _jg_{lj}e_j^*\Bigr) = \frac12\sum _{i, j}(g^TAg)_{ij}\, e_i^* \wedge e_j^* = \alpha _{g^TAg}, \]

\(g^TAg\) being again skew-symmetric. Taking \(m\)-th powers, \(\alpha _{g^TAg}^m = (\Lambda ^{2m}g^*)(\alpha _A^m) = m!\, \mathrm{Pf}(A)\det (g)\, e_1^* \wedge \cdots \wedge e_{2m}^*\), since \(\Lambda ^{2m}g^*\) is multiplication by \(\det g^* = \det g\) (Proposition 11.3.3(5) and Proposition 10.4.2(3)). For \(g\) orthogonal, \(g^TAg = g^{-1}Ag\).

(3) Here \(\alpha _A = \sum _ia_i\, e_{2i-1}^* \wedge e_{2i}^*\). The \(2\)-forms \(e_{2i-1}^* \wedge e_{2i}^*\) commute and square to zero, so \(\alpha _A^m = m!\, a_1 \cdots a_m\, e_1^* \wedge \cdots \wedge e_{2m}^*\), as in the proof of Proposition 11.4.6.

(4) Suppose first that \(A\) is invertible. Then \(\omega (v, w) = v^TAw\) is a nondegenerate alternating form, and by Theorem 10.8.6 it has a symplectic basis \(e_1^{\prime }, \ldots , e_m^{\prime }, f_1^{\prime }, \ldots , f_m^{\prime }\). Let \(g\) be the matrix with columns \(e_1^{\prime }, f_1^{\prime }, e_2^{\prime }, f_2^{\prime }, \ldots , e_m^{\prime }, f_m^{\prime }\) (in this order). The entries of \(g^TAg\) are the values of \(\omega \) on pairs of these vectors, so \(g^TAg = J\) is block diagonal with blocks \(\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\). By (3), \(\mathrm{Pf}(J) = 1\), and \(\det J = 1\). By (2), \(\det (g)\mathrm{Pf}(A) = 1\), while \(\det (g)^2\det (A) = \det J = 1\); so \(\mathrm{Pf}(A)^2 = \det (g)^{-2} = \det A\). If \(A\) is not invertible, choose \(v \neq 0\) with \(Av = 0\) and an invertible \(g\) with first column \(v\). The first column of \(g^TAg\) is \(g^TAv = 0\), and by skew-symmetry so is its first row; hence \(\alpha _{g^TAg}\) is a \(2\)-form in \(e_2^*, \ldots , e_{2m}^*\) only, and its \(m\)-th power, of degree \(2m\) in \(2m - 1\) linear forms, vanishes. So \(\det (g)\mathrm{Pf}(A) = \mathrm{Pf}(g^TAg) = 0\), and \(\mathrm{Pf}(A) = 0 = \det A\).

Since \(\mathrm{Pf}\) is a polynomial with rational coefficients, the identity in (2) is a polynomial identity in the entries of \(A\) and \(g\); so it remains true when the entries of \(A\) are taken in any commutative \(\mathbb {Q}\)-algebra, for instance the algebra of differential forms of even degree on a manifold. This is how the Pfaffian is applied to curvature matrices in Chapter 38, where it produces the Euler form.

02N2 Example 11.4.9

For the \(4 \times 4\) skew-symmetric matrix with \(A_{12} = 1\), \(A_{13} = 2\), \(A_{14} = 3\), \(A_{23} = 4\), \(A_{24} = 5\), \(A_{34} = 6\), the formula gives \(\mathrm{Pf}(A) = 1 \cdot 6 - 2 \cdot 5 + 3 \cdot 4 = 8\), and indeed \(\det A = 64\). A real skew-symmetric matrix has \(\det A = \mathrm{Pf}(A)^2 \geq 0\); this explains why the determinant of a skew-symmetric matrix of even size is a perfect square, while one of odd size is \(0\) (as \(\det A = \det A^T = \det (-A) = (-1)^n\det A\)).
