12.4 Alternating forms00KC

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

In this section \(V\) has finite dimension \(n\).

00KD Proposition 12.4.1

The pairing \(\Lambda ^p(V^*) \times \Lambda ^p(V) \to k\) determined by \(\langle \varphi _1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \varphi _p, v_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge v_p \rangle = \det (\varphi _i(v_j))\) is well defined and perfect. Hence \(\Lambda ^p(V^*) \cong \Lambda ^p(V)^*\), which by Proposition 12.3.2 is the space \(\mathrm{Alt}^p(V)\) of alternating \(p\)-linear forms on \(V\).

Proof â–¼

The determinant is multilinear and alternating both in the \(\varphi _i\) and in the \(v_j\) (using Proposition 11.5.3(2)), so it induces a bilinear pairing. For a basis \((e_i)\) with dual basis \((e_i^*)\), \(\langle e_I^*, e_J \rangle = \delta _{IJ}\), so the pairing is perfect.

00KE Notation 12.4.2

We identify \(\Lambda ^p(V^*)\) with \(\mathrm{Alt}^p(V)\) via Proposition 12.4.1: an element \(\alpha \in \Lambda ^p(V^*)\) is evaluated on vectors as \(\alpha (v_1, \ldots , v_p) = \langle \alpha , v_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge v_p \rangle \). With this convention, \((\varphi _1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \varphi _p)(v_1, \ldots , v_p) = \det (\varphi _i(v_j))\), without a factor \(1/p!\).

02VF Example 12.4.3

The three descriptions of an alternating form, explicitly. Let \(e_1, \ldots , e_n\) be a basis of \(V\) with dual basis \(e_1^*, \ldots , e_n^*\), and for \(I = \{ i_1 {\lt} \cdots {\lt} i_p\} \) write \(e_I = e_{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge e_{i_p}\) and \(e_I^* = e_{i_1}^* \wedge \cdots \wedge e_{i_p}^*\).

  1. From \(\Lambda ^p(V^*)\) to \(\mathrm{Alt}^p(V)\). An element \(\alpha = \sum _Ia_Ie_I^*\) acts on vectors \(v_j = \sum _iv_{ij}e_i\) by \(\alpha (v_1, \ldots , v_p) = \sum _Ia_I\det (v_{i_kj})_{k,j}\), the sum of the coefficients times the \(p \times p\) minors, with rows \(I\), of the matrix of coordinates of the \(v_j\).

  2. From \(\mathrm{Alt}^p(V)\) to \(\Lambda ^p(V^*)\). An alternating \(p\)-linear map \(A\) is \(\sum _IA(e_{i_1}, \ldots , e_{i_p})\, e_I^*\): the coefficients are the values on increasing tuples of basis vectors. Summing over all tuples instead, \(A = \frac1{p!}\sum _{i_1, \ldots , i_p}A(e_{i_1}, \ldots , e_{i_p})\, e_{i_1}^* \wedge \cdots \wedge e_{i_p}^*\), since each increasing tuple appears \(p!\) times, with the same sign in \(A\) and in the wedge product.

  3. As a linear form on \(\Lambda ^pV\). \(\alpha \) sends \(e_J\) to \(a_J\), by Proposition 12.4.1; so \((e_I^*)\) is the dual basis of \((e_J)\).

For instance, on \(\mathbb {R}^3\) let \(\alpha = e_1^* \wedge e_2^* + 2e_2^* \wedge e_3^*\). As a bilinear map, \(\alpha (v, w) = (v_1w_2 - v_2w_1) + 2(v_2w_3 - v_3w_2) = v^TAw\) with the skew-symmetric matrix \(A\) having \(A_{12} = 1\), \(A_{23} = 2\), \(A_{13} = 0\); as a linear form on \(\Lambda ^2\mathbb {R}^3\) it sends \(e_1 \wedge e_2 \mapsto 1\), \(e_2 \wedge e_3 \mapsto 2\), \(e_1 \wedge e_3 \mapsto 0\); and under the identification \(\Lambda ^2\mathbb {R}^3 \cong \mathbb {R}^3\) of the cross product (\(e_2 \wedge e_3 \mapsto e_1\), \(e_3 \wedge e_1 \mapsto e_2\), \(e_1 \wedge e_2 \mapsto e_3\)) it is the vector \((2, 0, 1)\), with \(\alpha (v, w) = \langle (2, 0, 1), v \times w\rangle \). For differential forms the same dictionary holds at each point with \(e_i^* = dx^i\) and \(e_i = \partial _i\): \(\omega = \sum _I\omega _I\, dx^I\) with \(\omega _I = \omega (\partial _{i_1}, \ldots , \partial _{i_p})\).

Conventions. Some books identify \(\Lambda ^p(V^*)\) with \(\mathrm{Alt}^p(V)\) through the alternation map \(\mathrm{Alt}(T)(v_1, \ldots , v_p) = \frac1{p!}\sum _\sigma \operatorname {sgn}(\sigma )T(v_{\sigma (1)}, \ldots , v_{\sigma (p)})\), which gives \((\varphi _1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \varphi _p)(v_1, \ldots , v_p) = \frac1{p!}\det (\varphi _i(v_j))\). In our convention \(\varphi _1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \varphi _p = p!\, \mathrm{Alt}(\varphi _1 \otimes \cdots \otimes \varphi _p)\), with no factor in the evaluation; for \(p = 2\), \((\varphi \wedge \psi )(v, w) = \varphi (v)\psi (w) - \varphi (w)\psi (v)\) for us, and half of it in the other convention. Formulas such as the invariant formula for \(d\) (Chapter 36) change by such factors between books.

00KF Proposition 12.4.4

For \(\alpha \in \Lambda ^p(V^*)\) and \(\beta \in \Lambda ^q(V^*)\),

\[ (\alpha \wedge \beta )(v_1, \ldots , v_{p+q}) = \sum _{\sigma } \operatorname {sgn}(\sigma )\, \alpha (v_{\sigma (1)}, \ldots , v_{\sigma (p)})\, \beta (v_{\sigma (p+1)}, \ldots , v_{\sigma (p+q)}), \]

the sum running over the shuffles: permutations \(\sigma \in S_{p+q}\) with \(\sigma (1) {\lt} \cdots {\lt} \sigma (p)\) and \(\sigma (p+1) {\lt} \cdots {\lt} \sigma (p+q)\).

Proof â–¼

Both sides are bilinear in \((\alpha , \beta )\), so we may take \(\alpha = \varphi _1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \varphi _p\) and \(\beta = \varphi _{p+1} \wedge \cdots \wedge \varphi _{p+q}\). The left side is \(\det M\) with \(M = (\varphi _i(v_j))\). In the Leibniz formula \(\det M = \sum _\pi \operatorname {sgn}(\pi )\prod _j M_{\pi (j) j}\), group the permutations \(\pi \) according to the set \(A = \{ j : \pi (j) \leq p\} \) of columns sent to the first \(p\) rows. For fixed \(A = \{ a_1 {\lt} \cdots {\lt} a_p\} \) with complement \(\{ b_1 {\lt} \cdots {\lt} b_q\} \), let \(\sigma \) be the shuffle with \(\sigma (i) = a_i\) and \(\sigma (p + i) = b_i\). Every such \(\pi \) is uniquely \(\pi = (\pi _1 \times \pi _2) \circ \sigma ^{-1}\) with \(\pi _1 \in S_p\) acting on \(\{ 1, \ldots , p\} \) and \(\pi _2\) on \(\{ p+1, \ldots , p+q\} \), and \(\operatorname {sgn}\pi = \operatorname {sgn}\pi _1 \operatorname {sgn}\pi _2 \operatorname {sgn}\sigma \). Summing over \(\pi _1\) and \(\pi _2\) gives \(\operatorname {sgn}(\sigma )\det (\varphi _i(v_{a_j}))\det (\varphi _{p+i}(v_{b_j}))\), which is the term of \(\sigma \) on the right.

00KG Definition 12.4.5

For \(v \in V\) and \(\alpha \in \Lambda ^p(V^*)\) with \(p \geq 1\), the interior product \(\iota _v\alpha \in \Lambda ^{p-1}(V^*)\) is \((\iota _v\alpha )(v_2, \ldots , v_p) = \alpha (v, v_2, \ldots , v_p)\); for \(p = 0\), \(\iota _v\alpha = 0\).

00KH Proposition 12.4.6

\(\iota _v(\alpha \wedge \beta ) = \iota _v\alpha \wedge \beta + (-1)^p\alpha \wedge \iota _v\beta \) for \(\alpha \in \Lambda ^p(V^*)\), and \(\iota _v \iota _w = -\iota _w\iota _v\).

Proof â–¼

Expanding the determinant along the first column (Laplace expansion) gives \(\iota _v(\varphi _1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \varphi _p) = \sum _{i=1}^p (-1)^{i-1}\varphi _i(v)\, \varphi _1 \wedge \cdots \widehat{\varphi _i} \cdots \wedge \varphi _p\). Applying this formula to a product of decomposable forms and splitting the sum according to whether \(i\) is among the first \(p\) factors gives the first identity. The second holds because \(\alpha (w, v, \ldots ) = -\alpha (v, w, \ldots )\).

00KI Proposition 12.4.7

Suppose \(\operatorname {char} k = 0\), and let \(\omega \in \Lambda ^2(V^*)\) be nondegenerate, \(\dim V = 2m\). Then \(\omega ^m = \omega \wedge \cdots \wedge \omega \neq 0\), and every \(f \in \mathrm{GL}(V)\) with \(\omega (fv, fw) = \omega (v, w)\) has \(\det f = 1\).

Proof â–¼

With a symplectic basis (Theorem 11.9.6) and its dual basis, \(\omega = \sum _i e_i^* \wedge f_i^*\), as both sides take the same values on pairs of basis vectors. The \(2\)-forms \(e_i^* \wedge f_i^*\) commute and square to zero, so \(\omega ^m = m!\, e_1^* \wedge f_1^* \wedge \cdots \wedge e_m^* \wedge f_m^* \neq 0\). The map \(f^* \colon V^* \to V^*\), \(\varphi \mapsto \varphi \circ f\), satisfies \((\Lambda ^2 f^*)(\omega ) = \omega \), hence \((\Lambda ^{2m}f^*)(\omega ^m) = \omega ^m\); but \(\Lambda ^{2m}f^*\) is multiplication by \(\det f^* = \det f\) (Proposition 12.3.3(5) and Proposition 11.4.2(3)).

The same computation, applied to an arbitrary alternating form instead of a nondegenerate one, produces a square root of the determinant of a skew-symmetric matrix. In the rest of this section \(k\) is a field of characteristic \(0\), \(V = k^{2m}\) with standard basis \((e_i)\) and dual basis \((e_i^*)\). To a skew-symmetric matrix \(A \in M_{2m}(k)\), that is \(A^T = -A\), attach the \(2\)-form

\[ \alpha _A = \sum _{i {\lt} j}A_{ij}\, e_i^* \wedge e_j^* = \frac12\sum _{i, j}A_{ij}\, e_i^* \wedge e_j^* \in \Lambda ^2(V^*) . \]

The two expressions agree because \(A_{ii} = 0\) and \(A_{ji}\, e_j^* \wedge e_i^* = A_{ij}\, e_i^* \wedge e_j^*\). As an alternating form (Notation 12.4.2), \(\alpha _A(v, w) = \sum _{i {\lt} j}A_{ij}(v_iw_j - v_jw_i) = \sum _{i, j}A_{ij}v_iw_j = v^TAw\). Since \(\Lambda ^{2m}(V^*)\) is one-dimensional, spanned by \(e_1^* \wedge \cdots \wedge e_{2m}^*\) (Proposition 12.3.3(3)), the following definition makes sense.

02N0 Definition 12.4.8

The Pfaffian of a skew-symmetric matrix \(A \in M_{2m}(k)\) is the scalar \(\mathrm{Pf}(A)\) with

\[ \alpha _A^m = \alpha _A \wedge \cdots \wedge \alpha _A = m!\, \mathrm{Pf}(A)\, e_1^* \wedge \cdots \wedge e_{2m}^* . \]
02N1 Proposition 12.4.9

Let \(A \in M_{2m}(k)\) be skew-symmetric.

  1. \(\displaystyle \mathrm{Pf}(A) = \frac{1}{2^mm!}\sum _{\sigma \in S_{2m}}\operatorname {sgn}(\sigma )\, A_{\sigma (1)\sigma (2)}A_{\sigma (3)\sigma (4)} \cdots A_{\sigma (2m-1)\sigma (2m)}\). So \(\mathrm{Pf}\) is a homogeneous polynomial of degree \(m\) in the entries, with \(\mathrm{Pf}(\lambda A) = \lambda ^m\mathrm{Pf}(A)\); for instance

    \[ \mathrm{Pf}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & a \\ -a & 0 \end{pmatrix} = a, \qquad \mathrm{Pf}(A) = A_{12}A_{34} - A_{13}A_{24} + A_{14}A_{23} \quad (m = 2). \]

  2. \(\mathrm{Pf}(g^TAg) = \det (g)\, \mathrm{Pf}(A)\) for every \(g \in M_{2m}(k)\). In particular \(\mathrm{Pf}\) is invariant under conjugation by \(\mathrm{SO}(2m)\), and changes sign under conjugation by an orthogonal matrix of determinant \(-1\).

  3. If \(A\) is block diagonal with blocks \(\begin{pmatrix} 0 & a_i \\ -a_i & 0 \end{pmatrix}\), \(i = 1, \ldots , m\), then \(\mathrm{Pf}(A) = a_1a_2 \cdots a_m\).

  4. \(\mathrm{Pf}(A)^2 = \det A\).

Proof â–¼

(1) Expanding the \(m\)-th power of \(\alpha _A = \frac12\sum _{i,j}A_{ij}e_i^* \wedge e_j^*\) gives

\[ \alpha _A^m = \frac{1}{2^m}\sum _{i_1, j_1, \ldots , i_m, j_m}A_{i_1j_1} \cdots A_{i_mj_m}\, e_{i_1}^* \wedge e_{j_1}^* \wedge \cdots \wedge e_{i_m}^* \wedge e_{j_m}^* . \]

A wedge product of \(1\)-forms with a repeated factor vanishes, so only the terms in which \((i_1, j_1, \ldots , i_m, j_m) = (\sigma (1), \ldots , \sigma (2m))\) for a permutation \(\sigma \) survive, and for them \(e_{\sigma (1)}^* \wedge \cdots \wedge e_{\sigma (2m)}^* = \operatorname {sgn}(\sigma )\, e_1^* \wedge \cdots \wedge e_{2m}^*\) by Proposition 12.3.3(1). Comparing with the definition gives the formula. For \(m = 1\) the two permutations give \(\frac12(A_{12} - A_{21}) = A_{12}\). In general the \(2^mm!\) permutations that differ by reordering the \(m\) pairs (an even permutation of positions) and by swapping the two entries of some pairs (which changes both \(\operatorname {sgn}\sigma \) and the sign of one factor) give the same term, so \(\mathrm{Pf}(A)\) is the sum over the ways of splitting \(\{ 1, \ldots , 2m\} \) into \(m\) pairs \(\{ a_l {\lt} b_l\} \) of \(\pm \prod _lA_{a_lb_l}\), the sign being that of the permutation \((a_1, b_1, \ldots , a_m, b_m)\). For \(m = 2\) the three splittings \(\{ 12, 34\} \), \(\{ 13, 24\} \), \(\{ 14, 23\} \) give the permutations \(1234\), \(1324\), \(1423\), of signs \(+1\), \(-1\), \(+1\).

(2) Let \(g^* \colon V^* \to V^*\), \(\varphi \mapsto \varphi \circ g\), where \(g\) acts on \(V = k^{2m}\) by matrix multiplication. Then \(g^*e_k^* = \sum _ig_{ki}e_i^*\), because \(e_k^*(ge_i) = g_{ki}\). The induced map \(\Lambda g^*\) on \(\Lambda (V^*)\) is multiplicative, and

\[ (\Lambda ^2g^*)(\alpha _A) = \frac12\sum _{k, l}A_{kl}\Bigl(\sum _ig_{ki}e_i^*\Bigr) \wedge \Bigl(\sum _jg_{lj}e_j^*\Bigr) = \frac12\sum _{i, j}(g^TAg)_{ij}\, e_i^* \wedge e_j^* = \alpha _{g^TAg}, \]

\(g^TAg\) being again skew-symmetric. Taking \(m\)-th powers, \(\alpha _{g^TAg}^m = (\Lambda ^{2m}g^*)(\alpha _A^m) = m!\, \mathrm{Pf}(A)\det (g)\, e_1^* \wedge \cdots \wedge e_{2m}^*\), since \(\Lambda ^{2m}g^*\) is multiplication by \(\det g^* = \det g\) (Proposition 12.3.3(5) and Proposition 11.4.2(3)). For \(g\) orthogonal, \(g^TAg = g^{-1}Ag\).

(3) Here \(\alpha _A = \sum _ia_i\, e_{2i-1}^* \wedge e_{2i}^*\). The \(2\)-forms \(e_{2i-1}^* \wedge e_{2i}^*\) commute and square to zero, so \(\alpha _A^m = m!\, a_1 \cdots a_m\, e_1^* \wedge \cdots \wedge e_{2m}^*\), as in the proof of Proposition 12.4.7.

(4) Suppose first that \(A\) is invertible. Then \(\omega (v, w) = v^TAw\) is a nondegenerate alternating form, and by Theorem 11.9.6 it has a symplectic basis \(e_1^{\prime }, \ldots , e_m^{\prime }, f_1^{\prime }, \ldots , f_m^{\prime }\). Let \(g\) be the matrix with columns \(e_1^{\prime }, f_1^{\prime }, e_2^{\prime }, f_2^{\prime }, \ldots , e_m^{\prime }, f_m^{\prime }\) (in this order). The entries of \(g^TAg\) are the values of \(\omega \) on pairs of these vectors, so \(g^TAg = J\) is block diagonal with blocks \(\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\). By (3), \(\mathrm{Pf}(J) = 1\), and \(\det J = 1\). By (2), \(\det (g)\mathrm{Pf}(A) = 1\), while \(\det (g)^2\det (A) = \det J = 1\); so \(\mathrm{Pf}(A)^2 = \det (g)^{-2} = \det A\). If \(A\) is not invertible, choose \(v \neq 0\) with \(Av = 0\) and an invertible \(g\) with first column \(v\). The first column of \(g^TAg\) is \(g^TAv = 0\), and by skew-symmetry so is its first row; hence \(\alpha _{g^TAg}\) is a \(2\)-form in \(e_2^*, \ldots , e_{2m}^*\) only, and its \(m\)-th power, of degree \(2m\) in \(2m - 1\) linear forms, vanishes. So \(\det (g)\mathrm{Pf}(A) = \mathrm{Pf}(g^TAg) = 0\), and \(\mathrm{Pf}(A) = 0 = \det A\).

Since \(\mathrm{Pf}\) is a polynomial with rational coefficients, the identity in (2) is a polynomial identity in the entries of \(A\) and \(g\); so it remains true when the entries of \(A\) are taken in any commutative \(\mathbb {Q}\)-algebra, for instance the algebra of differential forms of even degree on a manifold. This is how the Pfaffian is applied to curvature matrices in Chapter 39, where it produces the Euler form.

02N2 Example 12.4.10

For the \(4 \times 4\) skew-symmetric matrix with \(A_{12} = 1\), \(A_{13} = 2\), \(A_{14} = 3\), \(A_{23} = 4\), \(A_{24} = 5\), \(A_{34} = 6\), the formula gives \(\mathrm{Pf}(A) = 1 \cdot 6 - 2 \cdot 5 + 3 \cdot 4 = 8\), and indeed \(\det A = 64\). A real skew-symmetric matrix has \(\det A = \mathrm{Pf}(A)^2 \geq 0\); this explains why the determinant of a skew-symmetric matrix of even size is a perfect square, while one of odd size is \(0\) (as \(\det A = \det A^T = \det (-A) = (-1)^n\det A\)).
