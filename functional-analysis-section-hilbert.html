30.3 Hilbert spaces015G

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

015H Definition 30.3.1

A Hilbert space is a vector space with an inner product (Definition 11.10.1) that is complete for the norm \(\| x\| = \langle x, x \rangle ^{1/2}\). Vectors are orthogonal if \(\langle x, y \rangle = 0\); for \(S \subseteq H\), \(S^\perp = \{ x : \langle x, s \rangle = 0 \ \forall s \in S\} \), a closed subspace.

015I Example 30.3.2

\(L^2(\mu )\) with \(\langle f, g \rangle = \int f\bar g\) (Theorem 28.5.3); \(\ell ^2\); finite-dimensional inner product spaces. The space \(C([0,1])\) with the \(L^2\) inner product is not complete, and \(L^2\) is its completion (Exercise 18.15.5).

015J Theorem 30.3.3

Let \(H\) be a Hilbert space and \(C \subseteq H\) nonempty, closed and convex. Every \(x \in H\) has a unique nearest point \(P_Cx \in C\). If \(M\) is a closed subspace, \(P_M\) is linear with \(\| P_M\| \leq 1\), \(x - P_Mx \in M^\perp \), and \(H = M \oplus M^\perp \). Moreover \((M^\perp )^\perp = M\), and \(M^\perp = 0\) if and only if \(M\) is dense.

Proof â–¼

Let \(d = \inf _{c \in C}\| x - c\| \) and \(c_k \in C\) with \(\| x - c_k\| \to d\). By the parallelogram law, \(\| c_k - c_l\| ^2 = 2\| x - c_k\| ^2 + 2\| x - c_l\| ^2 - 4\| x - \tfrac {c_k + c_l}{2}\| ^2 \leq 2\| x - c_k\| ^2 + 2\| x - c_l\| ^2 - 4d^2 \to 0\), using \(\tfrac {c_k+c_l}{2} \in C\). So \((c_k)\) is Cauchy and converges to some \(c \in C\) with \(\| x - c\| = d\); uniqueness follows from the same identity. For a closed subspace \(M\) and \(m \in M\), \(t \in \mathbb {K}\), the inequality \(\| x - P_Mx - tm\| \geq \| x - P_Mx\| \) forces \(\langle x - P_Mx, m \rangle = 0\). Hence \(x = P_Mx + (x - P_Mx) \in M + M^\perp \), and \(M \cap M^\perp = 0\), which also gives linearity and \(\| P_M\| \leq 1\) (Pythagoras). Finally \(M \subseteq (M^\perp )^\perp \), and if \(x \in (M^\perp )^\perp \) then \(x - P_Mx \in M^\perp \cap (M^\perp )^\perp = 0\). If \(M\) is a subspace, \(\overline{M}^\perp = M^\perp \), so \(M^\perp = 0\) if and only if \(\overline{M} = H\).

015K Theorem 30.3.4 Riesz representation

For every \(\Lambda \in H^*\) there is a unique \(y \in H\) with \(\Lambda (x) = \langle x, y \rangle \) for all \(x\), and \(\| \Lambda \| = \| y\| \). Consequently every bounded operator \(T \colon H \to H\) has a unique adjoint \(T^*\) with \(\langle Tx, y \rangle = \langle x, T^*y \rangle \); it satisfies \(\| T^*\| = \| T\| \) and \((\ker T)^\perp = \overline{\operatorname {im}T^*}\).

Proof â–¼

Existence and uniqueness of \(y\) are Lemma 28.12.1, whose proof used only completeness; the norm equality follows from Cauchy–Schwarz and \(\Lambda (y) = \| y\| ^2\). For fixed \(y\), \(x \mapsto \langle Tx, y \rangle \) is a bounded functional, so it is \(\langle x, T^*y \rangle \) for a unique \(T^*y\), and \(T^*\) is linear and bounded with \(\| T^*\| = \| T\| \) (from \(\| T\| = \sup _{\| x\| , \| y\| \leq 1}|\langle Tx, y \rangle |\), which is symmetric in \(T\) and \(T^*\)). Finally \(x \in \ker T\) if and only if \(\langle x, T^*y \rangle = 0\) for all \(y\), that is, \(x \in (\operatorname {im}T^*)^\perp \); take orthogonal complements.

02TU Proposition 30.3.5 Bessel’s inequality

Let \((e_i)_{i \in I}\) be an orthonormal family in an inner product space \(H\). For every \(x \in H\),

\[ \sum _{i \in I}|\langle x, e_i \rangle |^2 \leq \| x\| ^2; \]

in particular only countably many coefficients \(\langle x, e_i\rangle \) are nonzero, and \(\langle x, e_n\rangle \to 0\) for an orthonormal sequence.

Proof â–¼

For a finite \(J \subseteq I\) let \(y = \sum _{i \in J}\langle x, e_i \rangle e_i\). Then \(\langle x - y, e_j\rangle = 0\) for \(j \in J\), so \(x - y \perp y\) and, by Pythagoras, \(\| x\| ^2 = \| y\| ^2 + \| x - y\| ^2 \geq \| y\| ^2 = \sum _{i \in J}|\langle x, e_i \rangle |^2\). A sum of nonnegative terms is the supremum of its finite partial sums. For each \(k\) at most \(k\| x\| ^2\) indices have \(|\langle x, e_i\rangle |^2 \geq 1/k\), so the set of nonzero coefficients is a countable union of finite sets.

The vector \(y\) in the proof is the best approximation of \(x\) by combinations of \(e_i\), \(i \in J\): \(\| x - y\| ^2 = \| x\| ^2 - \sum _{i \in J}|\langle x, e_i\rangle |^2\), and equality in Bessel’s inequality for all \(x\) is Parseval’s identity, which characterises orthonormal bases.

015L Theorem 30.3.6 Orthonormal bases

Let \((e_i)_{i \in I}\) be an orthonormal family in a Hilbert space \(H\). The following are equivalent: its span is dense; \(x = \sum _i\langle x, e_i \rangle e_i\) for all \(x\) (with at most countably many nonzero terms, in any order); \(\| x\| ^2 = \sum _i|\langle x, e_i \rangle |^2\) for all \(x\) (Parseval). Such a family is an orthonormal basis; every Hilbert space has one, and \(H\) is separable if and only if it has a countable one, in which case \(H \cong \ell ^2(I)\).

Proof â–¼

For finite \(J \subseteq I\), \(P_Jx = \sum _{i \in J}\langle x, e_i \rangle e_i\) is the orthogonal projection onto the span of \((e_i)_{i \in J}\), so \(\sum _{i \in J}|\langle x, e_i \rangle |^2 \leq \| x\| ^2\) (Bessel); hence at most countably many coefficients are nonzero and, by completeness, \(\sum _i\langle x, e_i \rangle e_i\) converges (its partial sums are Cauchy by Pythagoras), to the projection of \(x\) onto the closed span \(M\). So the three conditions each say that \(M = H\), using \(\| x\| ^2 = \| P_Mx\| ^2 + \| x - P_Mx\| ^2\). Zorn’s lemma gives a maximal orthonormal family, whose closed span \(M\) satisfies \(M^\perp = 0\) (a unit vector in \(M^\perp \) would enlarge the family), so \(M = H\). If \(H\) is separable, an orthonormal family is discrete (\(\| e_i - e_j\| = \sqrt2\)) hence countable. The map \(x \mapsto (\langle x, e_i \rangle )\) is then an isometric isomorphism onto \(\ell ^2(I)\).

015M Example 30.3.7

The functions \(e_n(t) = e^{2\pi int}\), \(n \in \mathbb {Z}\), form an orthonormal basis of \(L^2(S^1)\), where \(S^1 = \mathbb {R}/\mathbb {Z}\) with Lebesgue measure: they are orthonormal by direct computation, their span (the trigonometric polynomials) is dense in \(C(S^1)\) for the supremum norm (Corollary 27.14.3), hence in \(L^2\), and \(C(S^1)\) is dense in \(L^2(S^1)\): by Proposition 28.5.4 the continuous functions with compact support in \((0, 1)\) are dense in \(L^2([0, 1])\), and they extend to continuous functions on \(S^1\). So every \(f \in L^2(S^1)\) is the \(L^2\)-limit of its Fourier series \(\sum _n\hat f(n)e_n\) with \(\hat f(n) = \int _0^1f(t)e^{-2\pi int}\, dt\), and \(\| f\| _2^2 = \sum _n|\hat f(n)|^2\).

02TV Example 30.3.8

Orthonormal bases of \(L^2([0, 1])\). Up to the null set \(\{ 1\} \), \([0, 1)\) is the circle \(\mathbb {R}/\mathbb {Z}\), so Example 30.3.7 gives the basis \(e^{2\pi int}\), \(n \in \mathbb {Z}\).

  1. Real Fourier basis. Taking real and imaginary parts, \(1\), \(\sqrt2\cos (2\pi nt)\), \(\sqrt2\sin (2\pi nt)\) (\(n \geq 1\)) is an orthonormal basis of the real space \(L^2([0, 1])\): these functions are orthonormal (\(\int _0^1\cos ^2(2\pi nt)\, dt = \frac12\)), and they span the same space of trigonometric polynomials, over \(\mathbb {C}\), as the \(e^{\pm 2\pi int}\).

  2. Legendre polynomials. Gram–Schmidt (Theorem 11.10.3) applied to \(1, x, x^2, \ldots \) gives orthonormal polynomials \(P_0, P_1, P_2, \ldots \) with \(\deg P_n = n\). With \(\langle f, g\rangle = \int _0^1fg\): \(P_0 = 1\); \(x - \langle x, 1\rangle = x - \frac12\) has norm\(^2\) \(\int _0^1(x - \frac12)^2 = \frac1{12}\), so \(P_1 = \sqrt3(2x - 1)\); and \(x^2 - \langle x^2, P_0\rangle P_0 - \langle x^2, P_1\rangle P_1 = x^2 - \frac13 - \frac{\sqrt3}{6}\sqrt3(2x - 1) = x^2 - x + \frac16\), whose norm\(^2\) is \(\frac1{180}\), so \(P_2 = \sqrt5(6x^2 - 6x + 1)\). They form an orthonormal basis: their span is the space of all polynomials, which is dense in \(C([0, 1])\) for the supremum norm (Corollary 27.14.3), hence in \(L^2\) (since \(\| f\| _2 \leq \| f\| _\infty \) and \(C([0, 1])\) is dense in \(L^2\), Proposition 28.5.4), and Theorem 30.3.6 applies.

  3. The Haar basis. Let \(h = \mathbf{1}_{[0, 1/2)} - \mathbf{1}_{[1/2, 1)}\) and \(h_{n,k}(t) = 2^{n/2}h(2^nt - k)\) for \(n \geq 0\), \(0 \leq k {\lt} 2^n\). Together with \(1\) these are orthonormal: two of them either have disjoint supports, or the support of one lies in an interval where the other is constant, and then the integral of the first vanishes. The \(2^N\) functions \(1\) and \(h_{n,k}\) with \(n {\lt} N\) lie in the \(2^N\)-dimensional space of functions constant on the dyadic intervals \([j2^{-N}, (j + 1)2^{-N})\) and are independent, so they span it. Such step functions approximate continuous functions uniformly, so the span is dense, and this is an orthonormal basis; unlike the Fourier basis it consists of functions with small support, the starting point of wavelets.

02TW Example 30.3.9

Parseval and \(\sum 1/n^2\). For \(f(t) = t\) on \([0, 1)\), \(\hat f(0) = \frac12\) and, integrating by parts, \(\hat f(n) = \int _0^1te^{-2\pi int}\, dt = \bigl[\frac{te^{-2\pi int}}{-2\pi in}\bigr]_0^1 = \frac{i}{2\pi n}\) for \(n \neq 0\). Parseval’s identity gives \(\frac13 = \int _0^1t^2\, dt = \frac14 + \sum _{n \neq 0}\frac{1}{4\pi ^2n^2}\), that is, \(\sum _{n \geq 1}\frac1{n^2} = \frac{\pi ^2}6\). Note that the Fourier series of \(f\) converges to \(f\) in \(L^2\), not pointwise at \(t = 0\), where the \(1\)-periodic extension of \(f\) jumps.
