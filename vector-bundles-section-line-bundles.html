34.4 Line bundles and the first Chern class019F

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

019G Definition 34.4.1

The Picard group \(\operatorname {Pic}^\infty (M)\) is the set of isomorphism classes of smooth complex line bundles with the tensor product; by Proposition 24.6.4 it is a group, with inverse the dual.

For line bundles the transition functions are nowhere vanishing functions \(g_{ij} \colon U_i \cap U_j \to \mathbb {C}^\times \), which commute, and the tensor product multiplies them. So \(\operatorname {Pic}^\infty (M)\) is the first Čech cohomology group of the sheaf of nowhere vanishing smooth functions, and it can be computed with the exponential function.

019H Theorem 34.4.2

Let \(\underline{\mathbb {C}}^\times _M\) be the sheaf of smooth nowhere vanishing complex functions. There are natural isomorphisms

\[ \operatorname {Pic}^\infty (M) \cong H^1(M, \underline{\mathbb {C}}^\times _M) \xrightarrow {\ \sim \ } H^2(M; \mathbb {Z}), \]

the second induced by the connecting homomorphism of the exponential sequence \(0 \to \mathbb {Z}\to \underline{\mathbb {C}}_M \xrightarrow {\exp (2\pi i\, \cdot )} \underline{\mathbb {C}}^\times _M \to 0\). The resulting class \(c_1(L) \in H^2(M; \mathbb {Z})\) is the first Chern class; it satisfies \(c_1(L \otimes L') = c_1(L) + c_1(L')\), \(c_1(f^*L) = f^*c_1(L)\), and \(L\) is trivial if and only if \(c_1(L) = 0\).

Proof â–¼

Line bundles are classified by \(\check{H}^1\) of the sheaf of smooth nonvanishing functions by Proposition 34.2.6(3), and Čech and sheaf cohomology agree in degree one (Remark 25.5.4); the group structures agree because the cocycle of a tensor product is the product of the cocycles. The exponential sequence is exact as a sequence of sheaves: surjectivity holds because a nonvanishing function has a smooth logarithm on any set where it is defined and contractible, for instance a small ball, and the kernel of \(\exp (2\pi i\, \cdot )\) is the constant sheaf \(\mathbb {Z}\) (Theorem 26.7.3). Its long exact sequence reads

\[ H^1(M, \underline{\mathbb {C}}_M) \to H^1(M, \underline{\mathbb {C}}^\times _M) \to H^2(M, \mathbb {Z}_M) \to H^2(M, \underline{\mathbb {C}}_M), \]

and the outer terms vanish because \(\underline{\mathbb {C}}_M\) is a sheaf of modules over \(\mathcal{C}_M\), hence fine, hence acyclic (Corollary 25.4.6); manifolds are paracompact (Proposition 17.10.7). Finally \(H^2(M, \mathbb {Z}_M) \cong H^2(M; \mathbb {Z})\) by Theorem 25.6.1, since manifolds are locally contractible. Additivity and naturality follow from the corresponding properties of the cocycles and of the connecting homomorphism, and triviality of \(L\) means exactly that its class in \(H^1(M, \underline{\mathbb {C}}^\times _M)\) vanishes.

In particular every complex line bundle on a manifold with \(H^2(M; \mathbb {Z}) = 0\) is trivial: on \(\mathbb {R}^n\), on a disc, on \(S^1\), on \(S^3\). On the sphere \(S^2\) the theorem gives \(\operatorname {Pic}^\infty (S^2) \cong \mathbb {Z}\), and in this case the classification can be made completely explicit, by the winding number of a transition function.

For a continuous loop \(c \colon [0, 1] \to \mathbb {C}^\times \), \(c(0) = c(1)\), choose a continuous logarithm \(L\) along it, \(e^{L(t)} = c(t)\); one exists because \(\exp \colon \mathbb {C}\to \mathbb {C}^\times \) is a covering map (local inverses are the branches of the logarithm on slit planes) and paths lift (Proposition 18.4.3). The winding number \(\mathrm{wind}(c) = (L(1) - L(0))/2\pi i\) is an integer, as \(e^{L(1)} = e^{L(0)}\), and does not depend on \(L\), since two lifts differ by a constant in \(2\pi i\mathbb {Z}\). For a function \(g \colon \mathbb {C}^\times \to \mathbb {C}^\times \) we write \(\mathrm{wind}(g)\) for the winding number of \(t \mapsto g(e^{2\pi it})\) along the unit circle.

02M2 Lemma 34.4.3

  1. \(\mathrm{wind}(c_1c_2) = \mathrm{wind}(c_1) + \mathrm{wind}(c_2)\), and \(\mathrm{wind}(z^k) = k\).

  2. Loops that are homotopic through loops in \(\mathbb {C}^\times \) have the same winding number. In particular \(\mathrm{wind}(g) = 0\) if \(g\) extends to a continuous nowhere vanishing function on the closed unit disc, or on \(\{ |z| \geq 1\} \cup \{ \infty \} \subseteq \mathbb {C}\mathbb {P}^1\).

  3. A continuous \(g \colon \mathbb {C}^\times \to \mathbb {C}^\times \) with \(\mathrm{wind}(g) = 0\) has a continuous logarithm on \(\mathbb {C}^\times \), smooth if \(g\) is smooth.

Proof â–¼

(1) If \(L_1\), \(L_2\) are logarithms along \(c_1\), \(c_2\), then \(L_1 + L_2\) is one along \(c_1c_2\). For \(z^k\) along \(e^{2\pi it}\) take \(L(t) = 2\pi ikt\). (2) Let \(H(s, t)\) be a homotopy of loops. Lifting \(H\) through \(\exp \) (Proposition 18.4.3) gives \(\tilde H\) with \(e^{\tilde H} = H\), and \(s \mapsto (\tilde H(s, 1) - \tilde H(s, 0))/2\pi i\) is a continuous integer-valued function, hence constant. If \(g\) extends to the closed unit disc, the loops \(t \mapsto g(\rho e^{2\pi it})\), \(0 \leq \rho \leq 1\), form a homotopy through loops in \(\mathbb {C}^\times \) from the constant loop \(g(0)\) to the given one; if it extends to \(\{ |z| \geq 1\} \cup \{ \infty \} \), use the loops \(t \mapsto g(\sigma ^{-1}e^{2\pi it})\), \(0 {\lt} \sigma \leq 1\), with the constant loop \(g(\infty )\) at \(\sigma = 0\). (3) The function \(G(r, t) = g(re^{2\pi it})\) on \((0, \infty ) \times \mathbb {R}\), which is simply connected, lifts through \(\exp \) to a continuous \(\Lambda \) with \(e^\Lambda = G\) (Proposition 18.6.1). The difference \(\Lambda (r, t + 1) - \Lambda (r, t)\) lies in \(2\pi i\mathbb {Z}\) and depends continuously on \((r, t)\), so it is a constant, equal to \(2\pi i\) times the winding number of the circle of radius \(1\), which is \(0\). So \(\Lambda (r, t)\) depends only on \(re^{2\pi it}\), and defines a continuous logarithm of \(g\) on \(\mathbb {C}^\times \); it is smooth when \(g\) is, because \(\exp \) is a local diffeomorphism.

02M3 Proposition 34.4.4

Let \(L\) be a complex line bundle on \(\mathbb {C}\mathbb {P}^1 = U_0 \cup U_1\), with \(U_0 = \{ [1 : z]\} \cong \mathbb {C}\) and \(U_1 = \{ [w : 1]\} \), \(w = 1/z\) on the overlap. Then \(L\) has frames \(s_0\) over \(U_0\) and \(s_1\) over \(U_1\), and on \(U_0 \cap U_1 \cong \mathbb {C}^\times \) (coordinate \(z\)) we can write \(s_1 = g\, s_0\) with \(g \colon \mathbb {C}^\times \to \mathbb {C}^\times \). The integer

\[ \deg L = \mathrm{wind}(g) \]

does not depend on the frames, \(\deg (L \otimes L^{\prime }) = \deg L + \deg L^{\prime }\), every integer is the degree of some line bundle, and \(L \cong L^{\prime }\) if and only if \(\deg L = \deg L^{\prime }\). So \(\deg \colon \operatorname {Pic}^\infty (\mathbb {C}\mathbb {P}^1) \to \mathbb {Z}\) is an isomorphism.

Proof â–¼

The frames exist because \(U_0\) and \(U_1\) are diffeomorphic to \(\mathbb {R}^2\), over which every complex line bundle is trivial, as \(H^2(\mathbb {R}^2; \mathbb {Z}) = 0\) (Theorem 34.4.2). Other frames are \(s^{\prime }_0 = h_0s_0\) and \(s^{\prime }_1 = h_1s_1\) with \(h_i \colon U_i \to \mathbb {C}^\times \), and then \(g^{\prime } = h_1gh_0^{-1}\); by Lemma 34.4.3(1),(2), \(\mathrm{wind}(g^{\prime }) = \mathrm{wind}(g) + \mathrm{wind}(h_1) - \mathrm{wind}(h_0) = \mathrm{wind}(g)\), as \(h_0\) is defined on all of \(U_0 = \mathbb {C}\) and \(h_1\) on \(U_1 = \mathbb {C}\mathbb {P}^1 \setminus \{ 0\} \), which contains \(\{ |z| \geq 1\} \cup \{ \infty \} \). For the tensor product, \(s_1 \otimes s^{\prime }_1 = gg^{\prime }\, s_0 \otimes s^{\prime }_0\), so degrees add. The bundle with transition function \(g = z^k\), obtained by Proposition 34.2.6(2), has degree \(k\). If \(\deg L = \deg L^{\prime }\), with transition functions \(g\) and \(g^{\prime }\), then \(g^{\prime }/g\) has winding number \(0\), hence a smooth logarithm \(f\) on \(\mathbb {C}^\times \) (Lemma 34.4.3(3)). Choose a partition of unity \(\psi _0 + \psi _1 = 1\) subordinate to \(\{ U_0, U_1\} \) and put \(f_0 = -\psi _1f\) on \(U_0\) and \(f_1 = \psi _0f\) on \(U_1\), each extended by \(0\) where the cut-off vanishes; then \(f_1 - f_0 = f\) on the overlap, and \(h_i = e^{f_i}\) satisfy \(h_1gh_0^{-1} = ge^{f} = g^{\prime }\). So after changing the frames of \(L\) by \(h_0\) and \(h_1\), the two bundles have the same transition function, and they are isomorphic by Proposition 34.2.6(3).

02M4 Example 34.4.5

  1. The tautological bundle has \(s_1 = z^{-1}s_0\) (Example 34.2.7(4)), so \(\deg \mathcal{O}(-1) = -1\), and \(\deg \mathcal{O}(k) = k\).

  2. The tangent bundle \(T\mathbb {C}\mathbb {P}^1\), a complex line bundle with the frames \(s_0 = \partial /\partial z\) and \(s_1 = \partial /\partial w\): from \(z = 1/w\), \(\partial /\partial w = (dz/dw)\, \partial /\partial z = -w^{-2}\, \partial /\partial z = -z^2\, \partial /\partial z\), so \(g = -z^2\) and \(\deg T\mathbb {C}\mathbb {P}^1 = 2\). Hence \(T\mathbb {C}\mathbb {P}^1 \cong \mathcal{O}(2)\), and \(TS^2\) is not trivial, which gives another proof of the hairy ball theorem for \(S^2\). The cotangent bundle has degree \(-2\).

  3. The degree is the first Chern class: under \(H^2(\mathbb {C}\mathbb {P}^1; \mathbb {Z}) \cong \mathbb {Z}\) given by the complex orientation, \(c_1(L) = \deg L\). We do not verify this here by computing the connecting homomorphism; it follows from Chern–Weil theory (Chapter 38), and in Chapter 37 the curvature of a connection on \(\mathcal{O}(-1)\) is computed to integrate to \(-1\). The degree \(2\) of \(T\mathbb {C}\mathbb {P}^1\) is the Euler characteristic of \(S^2\), an instance of the Gauss–Bonnet theorem.

019I Example 34.4.6

\(H^2(S^2; \mathbb {Z}) \cong \mathbb {Z}\), so the complex line bundles on \(S^2 = \mathbb {C}\mathbb {P}^1\) are classified by an integer, the degree; the tautological bundle \(\mathcal{O}(-1)\) has \(c_1 = -1\) with the standard orientation. On \(\mathbb {C}\mathbb {P}^n\), \(H^2 \cong \mathbb {Z}\) is generated by \(c_1(\mathcal{O}(1))\). Every complex line bundle on a surface of genus \(g\) is classified by its degree in \(H^2 \cong \mathbb {Z}\), while on a simply connected manifold with \(H^2 = 0\), such as \(S^3\) or \(S^4\), every complex line bundle is trivial. Real line bundles are classified in the same way by \(H^1(M; \mathbb {Z}/2\mathbb {Z})\), using the sheaf of nonvanishing real functions and the sequence \(0 \to \mathbb {Z}/2 \to \underline{\mathbb {R}}^\times \to \underline{\mathbb {R}}^\times _{{\gt}0} \to 0\); the resulting class is the first Stiefel–Whitney class \(w_1(L)\), which vanishes if and only if \(L\) is orientable.

019J Remark 34.4.7

For bundles of higher rank the classification is not by a single class: isomorphism classes of rank \(r\) complex bundles on \(M\) correspond to homotopy classes of maps \(M \to \mathrm{BU}(r)\), and the Chern classes \(c_k(E) \in H^{2k}(M; \mathbb {Z})\) are pulled back from universal classes on the classifying space. They are constructed and computed in Chapter 38; for line bundles \(c_1\) agrees with the class above.
