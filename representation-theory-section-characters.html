13.3 Characters of finite groups00L6

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

In this section \(G\) is finite, \(k = \mathbb {C}\), and representations are finite-dimensional.

00L7 Definition 13.3.1

The character of a representation \(V\) is \(\chi _V \colon G \to \mathbb {C}\), \(\chi _V(g) = \operatorname {tr}(g|_V)\). A class function is a function \(G \to \mathbb {C}\) constant on conjugacy classes. On class functions put the Hermitian inner product \(\langle \phi , \psi \rangle = \frac{1}{|G|}\sum _{g \in G}\phi (g)\overline{\psi (g)}\).

00L8 Proposition 13.3.2

Characters are class functions, \(\chi _V(e) = \dim V\), \(\chi _{V \oplus W} = \chi _V + \chi _W\), \(\chi _{V \otimes W} = \chi _V\chi _W\), and \(\chi _{V^*}(g) = \overline{\chi _V(g)} = \chi _V(g^{-1})\). Moreover \(\dim V^G = \frac{1}{|G|}\sum _g \chi _V(g)\).

Proof â–¼

The trace is invariant under conjugation, additive on direct sums and multiplicative on tensor products (compute in bases). Each \(g\) has finite order, so \(g|_V\) satisfies \(x^n - 1\) and is diagonalisable with roots of unity \(\lambda _i\) as eigenvalues (Theorem 11.7.6); then \(\chi _{V^*}(g) = \sum \lambda _i^{-1} = \sum \bar\lambda _i\). Finally \(p = \frac{1}{|G|}\sum _g g\) is a projection onto \(V^G\) (it is the identity on \(V^G\) and \(hp = p\) for all \(h\)), and the trace of a projection is the dimension of its image.

00L9 Theorem 13.3.3 Orthogonality

For representations \(V\), \(W\), \(\langle \chi _W, \chi _V \rangle = \dim \operatorname {Hom}_G(V, W)\). Consequently the characters of irreducible representations are orthonormal, a representation is determined up to isomorphism by its character, and \(V\) is irreducible if and only if \(\langle \chi _V, \chi _V \rangle = 1\).

Proof â–¼

\(\operatorname {Hom}_G(V, W) = \operatorname {Hom}_\mathbb {C}(V, W)^G\), and \(\operatorname {Hom}_\mathbb {C}(V, W) \cong V^* \otimes W\) as representations, so by Proposition 13.3.2 its dimension is \(\frac{1}{|G|}\sum _g \overline{\chi _V(g)}\chi _W(g)\). For irreducible \(V\), \(W\), Schur’s lemma gives \(\dim \operatorname {Hom}_G(V, W) = 1\) if \(V \cong W\) and \(0\) otherwise. By Maschke’s theorem \(V \cong \bigoplus _i V_i^{m_i}\) with distinct irreducible \(V_i\), and \(m_i = \langle \chi _V, \chi _{V_i} \rangle \) is determined by \(\chi _V\); also \(\langle \chi _V, \chi _V \rangle = \sum m_i^2\).

00LA Theorem 13.3.4

The characters of the irreducible representations of \(G\) form an orthonormal basis of the space of class functions. In particular the number of isomorphism classes of irreducible representations equals the number of conjugacy classes, and if \(V_1, \ldots , V_r\) are the irreducible representations, then \(\sum _i (\dim V_i)^2 = |G|\).

Proof â–¼

The regular representation has character \(|G|\) at \(e\) and \(0\) elsewhere, so by Theorem 13.3.3 it contains each \(V_i\) with multiplicity \(\langle \chi _{\mathrm{reg}}, \chi _{V_i}\rangle = \dim V_i\); comparing dimensions gives \(\sum (\dim V_i)^2 = |G|\). In particular there are finitely many irreducible representations up to isomorphism.

It remains to show that a class function \(\phi \) orthogonal to all \(\chi _{V_i}\) is zero. For a representation \(V\) let \(T_V = \sum _g \overline{\phi (g)}\, g|_V\). Since \(\phi \) is a class function, \(T_V\) commutes with all \(h|_V\). If \(V\) is irreducible, \(T_V = \lambda \mathrm{id}\) by Schur’s lemma, and taking traces, \(\lambda \dim V = \sum _g \overline{\phi (g)}\chi _V(g) = |G|\langle \chi _V, \phi \rangle = 0\). So \(T_V = 0\) for every irreducible \(V\), hence for every \(V\) by Maschke’s theorem, in particular for the regular representation; but there \(T(e_e) = \sum _g \overline{\phi (g)} e_g\), so \(\phi = 0\).

00LB Example 13.3.5

\(S_3\) has three conjugacy classes: \(\{ e\} \), the three transpositions and the two \(3\)-cycles. Its irreducible representations are the trivial representation, the sign representation and the two-dimensional representation \(U = \{ x \in \mathbb {C}^3 : x_1 + x_2 + x_3 = 0\} \) (a subrepresentation of the permutation representation). Their characters take the values \((1, 1, 1)\), \((1, -1, 1)\) and \((2, 0, -1)\) on the three classes. One checks orthonormality directly: for instance \(\langle \chi _U, \chi _U\rangle = \frac16(4 + 0 + 2 \cdot 1) = 1\).

02AP Proposition 13.3.6

Let \(G\) be a finite abelian group. Every irreducible complex representation of \(G\) is one-dimensional, given by a homomorphism \(\chi \colon G \to \mathbb {C}^\times \), and there are exactly \(|G|\) of them. For \(G = \mathbb {Z}/n\mathbb {Z}\) they are \(\chi _j(k) = e^{2\pi ijk/n}\), \(0 \leq j {\lt} n\).

Proof â–¼

Let \(V\) be irreducible. Each \(g\) commutes with the action of \(G\), so by Schur’s lemma (Lemma 13.2.3) it acts by a scalar; then every line in \(V\) is a subrepresentation, and \(\dim V = 1\). The conjugacy classes of an abelian group are its elements, so by Theorem 13.3.4 there are \(|G|\) irreducible representations. For \(\mathbb {Z}/n\mathbb {Z}\), a homomorphism to \(\mathbb {C}^\times \) is determined by the image \(\zeta \) of \(1\), with \(\zeta ^n = 1\), and the \(n\) choices give the \(\chi _j\).

02TA Corollary 13.3.7

The one-dimensional complex representations of a group \(G\) are the homomorphisms \(G \to \mathbb {C}^\times \), and they all factor through the abelianisation \(G^{\mathrm{ab}} = G/[G, G]\) (Proposition 7.9.2). For finite \(G\) there are exactly \([G : [G, G]]\) of them.

Proof â–¼

A one-dimensional representation is a homomorphism \(G \to \mathrm{GL}_1(\mathbb {C}) = \mathbb {C}^\times \), and \(\mathbb {C}^\times \) is abelian. So they correspond to the homomorphisms \(G^{\mathrm{ab}} \to \mathbb {C}^\times \), which by Proposition 13.3.6, applied to the finite abelian group \(G^{\mathrm{ab}}\), are \(|G^{\mathrm{ab}}|\) in number.

For example \([S_n, S_n] = A_n\) for \(n \geq 2\) (Exercise 7.12.3), so \(S_n\) has exactly two one-dimensional representations, the trivial one and the sign; a perfect group, such as \(A_5\), has only the trivial one. The analogue for Lie algebras: one-dimensional representations of \(\mathfrak {g}\) are the linear forms vanishing on \([\mathfrak {g}, \mathfrak {g}]\), so a Lie algebra with \([\mathfrak {g}, \mathfrak {g}] = \mathfrak {g}\), for instance a semisimple one, has only the trivial one.

02AQ Counterexample 13.3.8

The second statement of Schur’s lemma needs an algebraically closed field. The group \(\mathbb {Z}/4\mathbb {Z}\) acting on \(\mathbb {R}^2\) by rotation by \(\pi /2\), with generator acting by \(J = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\), is irreducible over \(\mathbb {R}\), since \(J\) has no real eigenvector; but its endomorphisms, the matrices commuting with \(J\), are the \(aI + bJ\), a field isomorphic to \(\mathbb {C}\), not \(\mathbb {R}\cdot \mathrm{id}\). Over \(\mathbb {C}\) the representation splits as \(\chi _1 \oplus \chi _3\) in the notation of Proposition 13.3.6.

02AR Definition 13.3.9

Let \(H \subseteq G\) be a subgroup of a finite group and \(W\) a representation of \(H\). The induced representation is \(\operatorname {Ind}_H^GW = \{ f \colon G \to W : f(hx) = hf(x) \text{ for } h \in H, x \in G\} \), with \((gf)(x) = f(xg)\). For a representation \(V\) of \(G\), \(\operatorname {Res}^G_HV\) is \(V\) viewed as a representation of \(H\).

02AS Proposition 13.3.10 Frobenius reciprocity

In the situation of Definition 13.3.9:

  1. \(\operatorname {Hom}_G(V, \operatorname {Ind}_H^GW) \cong \operatorname {Hom}_H(\operatorname {Res}^G_HV, W)\), via \(\varphi \mapsto (v \mapsto \varphi (v)(e))\);

  2. \(\dim \operatorname {Ind}_H^GW = [G : H]\dim W\), and

    \[ \chi _{\operatorname {Ind}W}(g) = \frac{1}{|H|}\sum _{x \in G,\ xgx^{-1} \in H}\chi _W(xgx^{-1}); \]

  3. \(\langle \chi _{\operatorname {Ind}W}, \chi _V\rangle _G = \langle \chi _W, \chi _{\operatorname {Res}V}\rangle _H\).

Proof â–¼

(1) If \(\varphi \) is \(G\)-equivariant, then \(\psi (v) = \varphi (v)(e)\) satisfies \(\psi (hv) = \varphi (hv)(e) = (h\varphi (v))(e) = \varphi (v)(h) = h\varphi (v)(e) = h\psi (v)\). Conversely, for \(\psi \in \operatorname {Hom}_H(V, W)\) put \(\varphi (v)(x) = \psi (xv)\); then \(\varphi (v)(hx) = \psi (hxv) = h\varphi (v)(x)\), so \(\varphi (v) \in \operatorname {Ind}W\), and \(\varphi (gv)(x) = \psi (xgv) = \varphi (v)(xg) = (g\varphi (v))(x)\). The two constructions are inverse, since \(\varphi (v)(x) = (x\varphi (v))(e) = \varphi (xv)(e)\).

(2) Choose representatives \(x_1, \ldots , x_m\) of the cosets \(Hx\), \(m = [G : H]\). An element \(f\) is determined by the values \(f(x_i)\), which are arbitrary, so \(f \mapsto (f(x_i))_i\) is an isomorphism with \(W^m\). For \(g \in G\) write \(x_ig = h_ix_{\sigma (i)}\) with \(h_i \in H\) and \(\sigma \) a permutation; then \((gf)(x_i) = f(x_ig) = h_if(x_{\sigma (i)})\). So in the decomposition \(W^m\), \(g\) maps the \(\sigma (i)\)-th summand to the \(i\)-th by \(h_i\), and only the indices with \(\sigma (i) = i\) contribute to the trace, with \(h_i = x_igx_i^{-1} \in H\). Hence \(\chi _{\operatorname {Ind}W}(g) = \sum _{i : x_igx_i^{-1} \in H}\chi _W(x_igx_i^{-1})\), and since \(\chi _W\) is a class function on \(H\), each coset \(Hx_i\) contributes \(|H|\) equal terms to the sum in the statement.

(3) By Theorem 13.3.3, both sides are dimensions of the spaces in (1): the left side is \(\dim \operatorname {Hom}_G(V, \operatorname {Ind}_H^GW)\) and the right side is \(\dim \operatorname {Hom}_H(\operatorname {Res}^G_HV, W)\).

02AT Counterexample 13.3.11

The character table does not determine the group. Let \(G\) be a group of order \(8\) whose centre \(Z\) has order \(2\) and with \(G/Z \cong (\mathbb {Z}/2\mathbb {Z})^2\); the dihedral group \(D_4\) of symmetries of a square and the quaternion group \(Q_8 = \{ \pm 1, \pm i, \pm j, \pm k\} \) are examples. For \(x \notin Z\) the centraliser contains \(\langle x\rangle Z\), of order \(4\), and is not \(G\), so the class of \(x\) is \(\{ x, zx\} \), \(Z = \{ e, z\} \): there are five classes. For \(x \notin Z\), \(x^2 \in Z\) and every conjugate of \(x\) lies in \(xZ\), because \(G/Z\) is abelian of exponent \(2\). The commutator subgroup is \(Z\) (\(G/Z\) is abelian and \(G\) is not), so the one-dimensional characters are the four characters of \(G/Z\), equal to \(1\) at \(z\); at each non-identity element of \(G/Z\) two of them take the value \(1\) and two the value \(-1\); by Theorem 13.3.4 the fifth irreducible character \(\chi \) has degree \(2\) (\(4 + 2^2 = 8\)), and comparing with the regular character, \(2\chi = \chi _{\mathrm{reg}} - \sum \chi _{1\text{-dim}}\) gives \(\chi (z) = -2\) and \(\chi (x) = 0\) for \(x \notin Z\). So the character tables of \(D_4\) and \(Q_8\) coincide, but the groups are not isomorphic: \(D_4\) has five elements of order \(2\) and \(Q_8\) only one.

13.3.1 Computing character tables0387

The character table of \(G\) lists the values of the irreducible characters \(\chi _1, \ldots , \chi _r\) on representatives \(g_1, \ldots , g_r\) of the conjugacy classes; by Theorem 13.3.4 it is a square matrix. It is usually found without constructing all the representations, by combining a few known characters with the orthogonality relations. Besides the orthogonality of rows (Theorem 13.3.3) there is an orthogonality of columns. We write \(C_G(g) = \{ x : xg = gx\} \) for the centraliser; the class of \(g\) has \(|G|/|C_G(g)|\) elements.

0388 Proposition 13.3.12 Column orthogonality

For \(g, h \in G\),

\[ \sum _{i=1}^r\chi _i(g)\overline{\chi _i(h)} = \begin{cases} |C_G(g)| & \text{if } g \text{ and } h \text{ are conjugate},\\ 0 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases} \]

In particular \(\sum _i\chi _i(e)^2 = |G|\) and \(\sum _i\chi _i(e)\chi _i(g) = 0\) for \(g \neq e\).

Proof â–¼

Let \(c_j = |G|/|C_G(g_j)|\) be the size of the \(j\)-th class, and let \(M\) be the \(r \times r\) matrix with entries \(M_{ij} = \chi _i(g_j)\sqrt{c_j/|G|}\). Since characters are class functions, row orthogonality says \(\sum _jM_{ij}\overline{M_{kj}} = \frac{1}{|G|}\sum _jc_j\chi _i(g_j)\overline{\chi _k(g_j)} = \langle \chi _i, \chi _k\rangle = \delta _{ik}\), so \(M\) is a unitary matrix. Then also \(M^*M = I\), that is, \(\sum _i\overline{M_{ij}}M_{il} = \delta _{jl}\), which is the formula for \(g = g_l\) and \(h = g_j\) after dividing by \(\sqrt{c_jc_l}/|G|\). For \(g = e\) the values \(\chi _i(e)\) are the dimensions, which are real.

Here are the other tools.

  1. Lifting from quotients. If \(N\) is a normal subgroup, every irreducible representation of \(G/N\) composed with \(G \to G/N\) is an irreducible representation of \(G\) (the images of \(G\) and of \(G/N\) in \(\mathrm{GL}(V)\) are the same), with character constant on cosets of \(N\). The one-dimensional characters are the lifts from \(G^{\mathrm{ab}}\) (Corollary 13.3.7).

  2. Twisting. If \(\chi \) is irreducible and \(\lambda \) is one-dimensional, then \(\lambda \chi \) is the character of an irreducible representation, since \(|\lambda (g)| = 1\) gives \(\langle \lambda \chi , \lambda \chi \rangle = \langle \chi , \chi \rangle = 1\).

  3. Permutation characters, by the next proposition.

  4. Real values. If \(g\) is conjugate to \(g^{-1}\), then \(\chi (g) = \chi (g^{-1}) = \overline{\chi (g)}\) is real for every character (Proposition 13.3.2).

0389 Proposition 13.3.13

Let \(G\) act on a finite set \(X\) and let \(\pi (g) = |\{ x \in X : gx = x\} |\) be the character of the permutation representation \(\mathbb {C}[X]\) (which has basis \(X\), permuted by \(G\)). Then \(\langle \pi , 1\rangle \) is the number of orbits of \(G\) on \(X\), and \(\langle \pi , \pi \rangle \) is the number of orbits of \(G\) on \(X \times X\). If the action is doubly transitive, that is, \(|X| \geq 2\) and \(G\) acts transitively on the pairs \((x, y)\) with \(x \neq y\), then \(\pi = 1 + \chi \) with \(\chi \) irreducible.

Proof â–¼

The matrix of \(g\) in the basis \(X\) is a permutation matrix, whose trace is the number of fixed points. By Proposition 13.3.2, \(\langle \pi , 1\rangle = \dim \mathbb {C}[X]^G\), and an element \(\sum _xa_xx\) is invariant exactly when \(x \mapsto a_x\) is constant on orbits; so the invariants have a basis indexed by the orbits. Since \(\pi \) is real, \(\langle \pi , \pi \rangle = \langle \pi ^2, 1\rangle \), and \(\pi (g)^2\) is the number of fixed points of \(g\) on \(X \times X\); so it is the number of orbits on \(X \times X\). For a doubly transitive action there are two orbits on \(X \times X\), the diagonal and its complement, and one on \(X\). Then \(\chi = \pi - 1\) satisfies \(\langle \chi , \chi \rangle = \langle \pi , \pi \rangle - 2\langle \pi , 1\rangle + 1 = 2 - 2 + 1 = 1\), and \(\chi (e) = |X| - 1 {\gt} 0\), so \(\chi \) is an irreducible character by Theorem 13.3.3.

For \(S_n\) acting on \(\{ 1, \ldots , n\} \), which is doubly transitive for \(n \geq 2\), this gives the standard representation of dimension \(n - 1\), with character (number of fixed points)\({} - 1\); for \(n = 3\) it is the representation \(U\) of Example 13.3.5. The alternating group \(A_n\) is doubly transitive on \(\{ 1, \ldots , n\} \) for \(n \geq 4\) (given \(x \neq y\) and \(x^{\prime } \neq y^{\prime }\), a permutation taking \(x, y\) to \(x^{\prime }, y^{\prime }\) can be composed with a transposition of two other points to make it even).

038A Example 13.3.14 The alternating group \(A_4\)

\(A_4\) has order \(12\). Its classes are: \(\{ e\} \); the three double transpositions such as \((12)(34)\); and the eight \(3\)-cycles, which split into two classes of four. Indeed, the centraliser of \((123)\) in \(S_4\) is the group \(\langle (123)\rangle \) of order \(3\) (an element commuting with \((123)\) preserves its set of fixed points \(\{ 4\} \) and permutes \(\{ 1, 2, 3\} \) by a power of the cycle), so its class in \(A_4\) has \(12/3 = 4\) elements; the classes of \((123)\) and \((132) = (123)^{-1}\) are different, as we shall see. The Klein group \(V = \{ e, (12)(34), (13)(24), (14)(23)\} \) is normal, and \(A_4/V\) has order \(3\), so it is cyclic, generated by the image of \((123)\). With \(\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}\), the three characters of \(A_4/V \cong \mathbb {Z}/3\mathbb {Z}\) lift to one-dimensional characters \(1\), \(\lambda \), \(\lambda ^2\) with \(\lambda ((123)) = \omega \) and \(\lambda ((132)) = \omega ^2\). Since \(\omega \neq \omega ^2\), the elements \((123)\) and \((132)\) are not conjugate, so there are four classes and four irreducible characters, with \(\sum \chi _i(e)^2 = 12\); the fourth has degree \(3\). It is the restriction of the standard representation of \(S_4\), irreducible because \(A_4\) is doubly transitive on four points: fixed points minus one gives \((3, -1, 0, 0)\). The table is

\[ \begin{array}{c|cccc}& e & (12)(34) & (123) & (132)\\ \text{class size} & 1 & 3 & 4 & 4\\ \hline 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 & 1\\ \lambda & 1 & 1 & \omega & \omega ^2\\ \lambda ^2 & 1 & 1 & \omega ^2 & \omega \\ \chi _3 & 3 & -1 & 0 & 0 \end{array} \]

As a check, the column of \((12)(34)\) has \(\sum _i|\chi _i|^2 = 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 4 = |C_{A_4}((12)(34))|\) (its centraliser is \(V\)), and the column of \((123)\) has \(1 + 1 + 1 + 0 = 3\). The representation \(\chi _3\) is the action of \(A_4\) as the rotation group of a regular tetrahedron: a rotation by \(\pi \) about an axis through midpoints of opposite edges has trace \(1 + 2\cos \pi = -1\), a rotation by \(2\pi /3\) about an axis through a vertex has trace \(1 + 2\cos (2\pi /3) = 0\).

038B Example 13.3.15 The alternating group \(A_5\) and the golden ratio

\(A_5\) has order \(60\) and five classes: \(\{ e\} \); the \(15\) double transpositions; the \(20\) three-cycles; and the \(24\) five-cycles, which split into two classes of \(12\), represented by \(x = (12345)\) and \(x^2 = (13524)\). (The centraliser of \(x\) in \(S_5\) is \(\langle x\rangle \), of order \(5\) and contained in \(A_5\), so the class of \(x\) in \(A_5\) has \(12\) elements. A permutation \(\sigma \) with \(\sigma x\sigma ^{-1} = x^2\) is \(\sigma = (2354)\), and all others differ from it by elements of \(\langle x\rangle \); they are all odd, so \(x\) and \(x^2\) are not conjugate in \(A_5\). On the other hand \(x^{-1} = (15432) = \tau x\tau ^{-1}\) with \(\tau = (25)(34)\) even, so \(x\) is conjugate to \(x^{-1}\) in \(A_5\), and all characters are real.) The centralisers have orders \(60, 4, 3, 5, 5\).

\(A_5\) is perfect, so the only one-dimensional character is the trivial one. Doubly transitive action on \(\{ 1, \ldots , 5\} \) gives \(\chi _4 = (\text{fixed points}) - 1 = (4, 0, 1, -1, -1)\). The action on the ten two-element subsets of \(\{ 1, \ldots , 5\} \) has permutation character \(\pi = (10, 2, 1, 0, 0)\) (the double transposition \((12)(34)\) fixes \(\{ 1, 2\} \) and \(\{ 3, 4\} \), the \(3\)-cycle \((123)\) fixes \(\{ 4, 5\} \)). Then \(\langle \pi , \pi \rangle = \frac{1}{60}(100 + 15 \cdot 4 + 20 \cdot 1) = 3\), \(\langle \pi , 1\rangle = 1\) and \(\langle \pi , \chi _4\rangle = \frac{1}{60}(40 + 20) = 1\), so \(\chi _5 = \pi - 1 - \chi _4 = (5, 1, -1, 0, 0)\) has \(\langle \chi _5, \chi _5\rangle = 3 - 1 - 1 = 1\) and is irreducible. The remaining two irreducible characters have \(\chi (e)^2 + \chi ^{\prime }(e)^2 = 60 - 1 - 16 - 25 = 18\), so both have degree \(3\); write their values as \((3, a, b, c, d)\) and \((3, a^{\prime }, b^{\prime }, c^{\prime }, d^{\prime })\), real numbers.

Column orthogonality with the column of \(e\) gives \(1 + 0 + 5 + 3(a + a^{\prime }) = 0\), \(1 + 4 - 5 + 3(b + b^{\prime }) = 0\) and \(1 - 4 + 0 + 3(c + c^{\prime }) = 0\), that is, \(a + a^{\prime } = -2\), \(b + b^{\prime } = 0\), \(c + c^{\prime } = 1\), and likewise \(d + d^{\prime } = 1\). The norms of the columns give \(1 + 0 + 1 + a^2 + a^{\prime 2} = 4\), \(1 + 1 + 1 + b^2 + b^{\prime 2} = 3\) and \(1 + 1 + 0 + c^2 + c^{\prime 2} = 5\). Hence \(a^2 + a^{\prime 2} = 2\) with \(a + a^{\prime } = -2\), forcing \(a = a^{\prime } = -1\); \(b = b^{\prime } = 0\); and \(c + c^{\prime } = 1\), \(c^2 + c^{\prime 2} = 3\), so \(cc^{\prime } = -1\) and \(c\), \(c^{\prime }\) are the roots of \(t^2 - t - 1\), namely

\[ \varphi = \frac{1 + \sqrt5}{2} \quad \text{and}\quad 1 - \varphi = \frac{1 - \sqrt5}{2}. \]

The same holds for \(d, d^{\prime }\), and orthogonality of the columns of \(x\) and \(x^2\) gives \(1 + 1 + 0 + cd + c^{\prime }d^{\prime } = 0\), which excludes \(d = c\) (then \(cd + c^{\prime }d^{\prime } = 3\)); so \(d = c^{\prime }\) and \(d^{\prime } = c\). The table is

\[ \begin{array}{c|ccccc}& e & (12)(34) & (123) & (12345) & (13524)\\ \text{class size} & 1 & 15 & 20 & 12 & 12\\ \hline 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 & 1\\ \chi _3 & 3 & -1 & 0 & \varphi & 1 - \varphi \\ \chi _3^{\prime } & 3 & -1 & 0 & 1 - \varphi & \varphi \\ \chi _4 & 4 & 0 & 1 & -1 & -1\\ \chi _5 & 5 & 1 & -1 & 0 & 0 \end{array} \]

The golden ratio has a geometric meaning: \(A_5\) is the rotation group of the regular icosahedron, and \(\chi _3\) is this three-dimensional representation. The element \(x\) acts as a rotation by \(2\pi /5\) about an axis through two opposite vertices, with trace \(1 + 2\cos (2\pi /5) = 1 + \frac{\sqrt5 - 1}{2} = \varphi \). The representation \(\chi _3^{\prime }\) is obtained from \(\chi _3\) by the field automorphism \(\sqrt5 \mapsto -\sqrt5\), or equivalently by composing with conjugation by an odd permutation, which exchanges the two classes of \(5\)-cycles.
