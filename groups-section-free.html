6.10 Free groups and presentations00DN

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

00DO Definition 6.10.1

Let \(S\) be a set and \(A = S \times \{ 1, -1\} \), whose elements we write \(s\) and \(s^{-1}\); for \(x = s^{\pm 1}\) write \(x^{-1} = s^{\mp 1}\). A word is a finite sequence \(x_1 \cdots x_k\) of elements of \(A\), and it is reduced if no two adjacent letters are of the form \(x x^{-1}\). Let \(F(S)\) be the set of reduced words.

00DP Theorem 6.10.2

There is a unique group structure on \(F(S)\) in which the product of reduced words \(u\) and \(v\) is obtained from the concatenation \(uv\) by repeatedly deleting adjacent pairs \(x x^{-1}\). With the map \(\iota \colon S \to F(S)\) sending \(s\) to the one-letter word, \(F(S)\) is the free group on \(S\): for every group \(G\) and map \(f \colon S \to G\) there is a unique homomorphism \(\bar f \colon F(S) \to G\) with \(\bar f \circ \iota = f\). So \(F\) is left adjoint to the forgetful functor \(\mathsf{Grp} \to \mathsf{Set}\).

Proof â–¼

For \(x \in A\) define \(L_x \colon F(S) \to F(S)\) by \(L_x(w) = xw\) if \(w\) does not begin with \(x^{-1}\), and \(L_x(x^{-1}w') = w'\). Then \(L_x L_{x^{-1}} = \mathrm{id}\) for all \(x\), so each \(L_x\) is a bijection, and \(L_{x^{-1}} = L_x^{-1}\). Let \(\Phi \leq \mathrm{Sym}(F(S))\) be the subgroup generated by the \(L_x\), and \(\epsilon \colon \Phi \to F(S)\), \(\pi \mapsto \pi (\emptyset )\), where \(\emptyset \) is the empty word. By Lemma 6.2.8, every \(\pi \in \Phi \) is a product \(L_{y_1} \cdots L_{y_m}\); deleting adjacent factors \(L_x L_{x^{-1}} = \mathrm{id}\) we may assume \(y_1 \cdots y_m\) is reduced, and then \(\pi (\emptyset ) = y_1 \cdots y_m\). Hence \(\pi \) is determined by \(\pi (\emptyset )\), and every reduced word \(w = x_1 \cdots x_k\) equals \(L_{x_1} \cdots L_{x_k}(\emptyset )\). So \(\epsilon \) is a bijection, and we transport the group structure of \(\Phi \) to \(F(S)\). The product of \(u\) and \(v\) is then \(L_{u_1} \cdots L_{u_k}(v)\), which is computed by the deletion process of the statement.

For \(f \colon S \to G\) extend \(f\) to \(A\) by \(f(s^{-1}) = f(s)^{-1}\) and define \(\bar f(x_1 \cdots x_k) = f(x_1) \cdots f(x_k)\). The value of \(f(x_1)\cdots f(x_k)\) on an arbitrary word does not change when an adjacent pair \(x x^{-1}\) is deleted, and the product in \(F(S)\) is obtained from concatenation by such deletions; so \(\bar f\) is a homomorphism. It is unique because \(\iota (S)\) generates \(F(S)\).

00DQ Definition 6.10.3

Let \(S\) be a set and \(R \subseteq F(S)\). The group with presentation \(\langle S \mid R \rangle \) is \(F(S)/N\), where \(N\) is the smallest normal subgroup containing \(R\) (the intersection of all normal subgroups containing \(R\)). Every group \(G\) has a presentation: take \(S = G\) and \(R\) the kernel of \(F(G) \to G\).

00DR Proposition 6.10.4

Let \(G = \langle S \mid R \rangle \) and let \(f \colon S \to H\) be a map to a group such that \(\bar f(r) = e\) for all \(r \in R\). Then there is a unique homomorphism \(G \to H\) sending the class of each \(s\) to \(f(s)\).

Proof â–¼

\(\ker \bar f\) is a normal subgroup containing \(R\), hence containing \(N\); apply Theorem 6.4.3. Uniqueness holds because the classes of \(S\) generate \(G\).

00DS Example 6.10.5

  1. \(\langle a \mid a^n \rangle \cong \mathbb {Z}/n\mathbb {Z}\): the homomorphism \(F(\{ a\} ) \to \mathbb {Z}/n\mathbb {Z}\), \(a \mapsto 1\), kills \(a^n\) and so induces a surjection \(\langle a \mid a^n \rangle \to \mathbb {Z}/n\mathbb {Z}\); the source is generated by the class of \(a\), which has order dividing \(n\), so it has at most \(n\) elements.

  2. \(\langle a, b \mid aba^{-1}b^{-1} \rangle \cong \mathbb {Z}^2\): the map \(a \mapsto (1, 0)\), \(b \mapsto (0, 1)\) induces a surjection, and a homomorphism \(\mathbb {Z}^2 \to \langle a, b \mid aba^{-1}b^{-1} \rangle \) inverse to it exists because the classes of \(a\) and \(b\) commute.

  3. \(D_n \cong \langle r, s \mid r^n, s^2, srsr \rangle \), by a similar argument.

00DT Definition 6.10.6

Let \(\alpha _1 \colon H \to G_1\) and \(\alpha _2 \colon H \to G_2\) be homomorphisms. The amalgamated free product \(G_1 *_H G_2\) is the group with generators the disjoint union of the sets \(G_1\) and \(G_2\) and relations \(x y z^{-1}\) whenever \(xy = z\) holds in \(G_1\) or in \(G_2\), and \(\alpha _1(h)\alpha _2(h)^{-1}\) for \(h \in H\). If \(H\) is trivial, it is the free product \(G_1 * G_2\).

00DU Proposition 6.10.7

With the evident homomorphisms \(G_i \to G_1 *_H G_2\), the amalgamated free product is a pushout of \(G_1 \leftarrow H \rightarrow G_2\) in \(\mathsf{Grp}\). In particular \(G_1 * G_2\) is a coproduct, and \(\mathsf{Grp}\) has all colimits.

Proof â–¼

The relations of the first kind make the maps \(G_i \to G_1 *_H G_2\) homomorphisms, and those of the second kind make the square commute. Given homomorphisms \(f_i \colon G_i \to K\) with \(f_1\alpha _1 = f_2\alpha _2\), the map on generators given by \(f_1\) and \(f_2\) kills all relations, so it induces a homomorphism by Proposition 6.10.4, which is unique since the images of \(G_1\) and \(G_2\) generate. Coproducts of arbitrary families are constructed in the same way, and coequalisers are quotients by normal subgroups; so \(\mathsf{Grp}\) has all colimits by the dual of Theorem 5.8.6.

00DV Remark 6.10.8

If \(\alpha _1\) and \(\alpha _2\) are injective, the maps \(G_i \to G_1 *_H G_2\) are injective, and every element has a normal form as an alternating product of coset representatives [Ser80, Chapter I, Theorem 1], [LS77, Chapter IV]. For example \(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb {Z}) \cong \mathbb {Z}/4\mathbb {Z}*_{\mathbb {Z}/2\mathbb {Z}} \mathbb {Z}/6\mathbb {Z}\) [Ser80, Chapter I, § 4]. Amalgamated free products compute fundamental groups of unions of spaces via the Seifert–van Kampen theorem (Chapter 17).
