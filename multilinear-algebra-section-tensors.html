12.4 Tensors of type \((r,s)\)03AL

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

The word tensor is used in two related ways. In algebra a tensor is any element of a tensor product. In geometry and physics a tensor is a multilinear quantity attached to a single vector space \(V\) (in geometry, the tangent space at a point), with a certain number of slots for vectors and a certain number of slots for linear forms: an inner product eats two vectors and returns a number, an endomorphism eats a vector and returns a vector, the curvature of a Riemannian manifold eats three vectors and returns a vector. Older texts, and most physics texts, take yet another point of view: a tensor is an array of numbers, one for each choice of basis, which changes in a prescribed way when the basis is changed. This section explains why the three points of view agree, and introduces the operations on tensors (tensor product, contraction, raising and lowering of indices) that are used constantly in differential geometry.

In this section \(V\) has finite dimension \(n\), and \((e_1, \ldots , e_n)\) is a basis of \(V\). Following the custom of differential geometry, we write \(e^i = e_i^*\) for the dual basis of \(V^*\) (Definition 11.4.1), so that \(e^i(e_j) = \delta ^i_j\), where \(\delta ^i_j\) is \(1\) if \(i = j\) and \(0\) otherwise. Indices of basis vectors of \(V\) are written below and indices of basis vectors of \(V^*\) above; the reason for this convention will appear in Proposition 12.4.4.

03AM Definition 12.4.1

Let \(r, s \in \mathbb {N}\). A tensor of type \((r,s)\) on \(V\) is an element of

\[ T^r_s(V) = \underbrace{V \otimes \cdots \otimes V}_{r} \otimes \underbrace{V^* \otimes \cdots \otimes V^*}_{s} = V^{\otimes r} \otimes (V^*)^{\otimes s}, \]

with the convention \(T^0_0(V) = k\). One says that such a tensor is \(r\) times contravariant and \(s\) times covariant. The tensor product of \(S = v_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes v_r \otimes \varphi ^1 \otimes \cdots \otimes \varphi ^s \in T^r_s(V)\) and \(S^{\prime } = v^{\prime }_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes v^{\prime }_{r^{\prime }} \otimes \psi ^1 \otimes \cdots \otimes \psi ^{s^{\prime }} \in T^{r^{\prime }}_{s^{\prime }}(V)\) is

\[ S \otimes S^{\prime } = v_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes v_r \otimes v^{\prime }_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes v^{\prime }_{r^{\prime }} \otimes \varphi ^1 \otimes \cdots \otimes \varphi ^s \otimes \psi ^1 \otimes \cdots \otimes \psi ^{s^{\prime }} \in T^{r + r^{\prime }}_{s + s^{\prime }}(V), \]

extended bilinearly; that is, it is the tensor product of Section 12.2 followed by the isomorphism that moves the factors \(V\) in front of the factors \(V^*\).

The tensor product of tensors is well defined: the expression on the right is multilinear in the \(r + s + r^{\prime } + s^{\prime }\) vectors and forms, so it induces a bilinear map \(T^r_s(V) \times T^{r^{\prime }}_{s^{\prime }}(V) \to T^{r + r^{\prime }}_{s + s^{\prime }}(V)\) by Proposition 12.2.1. It is associative, and it is not commutative: for \(v, w \in V\) independent, \(v \otimes w \neq w \otimes v\) in \(T^2_0(V)\) (Proposition 12.2.1). The small cases are familiar: \(T^0_0(V) = k\) consists of scalars, \(T^1_0(V) = V\) of vectors and \(T^0_1(V) = V^*\) of linear forms, also called covectors. The next proposition identifies the others with multilinear maps.

03AN Proposition 12.4.2

  1. There is a unique isomorphism from \(T^r_s(V)\) to the space of multilinear maps \((V^*)^r \times V^s \to k\) sending \(v_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes v_r \otimes \varphi ^1 \otimes \cdots \otimes \varphi ^s\) to the map

    \[ (\psi ^1, \ldots , \psi ^r, w_1, \ldots , w_s) \longmapsto \psi ^1(v_1) \cdots \psi ^r(v_r)\, \varphi ^1(w_1) \cdots \varphi ^s(w_s). \]

  2. There is a unique isomorphism from \(T^1_s(V)\) to the space of multilinear maps \(V^s \to V\) sending \(v \otimes \varphi ^1 \otimes \cdots \otimes \varphi ^s\) to the map \((w_1, \ldots , w_s) \mapsto \varphi ^1(w_1) \cdots \varphi ^s(w_s)\, v\). For \(s = 1\) this is the isomorphism \(T^1_1(V) \cong \operatorname {End}(V)\) of Proposition 12.2.2.

  3. The tensors \(e_{i_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes e_{i_r} \otimes e^{j_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes e^{j_s}\), for all \(i_1, \ldots , i_r, j_1, \ldots , j_s \in \{ 1, \ldots , n\} \), form a basis of \(T^r_s(V)\). In particular \(\dim T^r_s(V) = n^{r+s}\).

Proof â–¼

(3) The \(e^j\) form a basis of \(V^*\) (Proposition 11.4.2), and Proposition 12.2.1, applied \(r + s - 1\) times, says that the tensor products of basis vectors of the factors form a basis of the tensor product.

(1) By Proposition 12.2.1, multilinear maps \((V^*)^r \times V^s \to k\) are the same as linear maps \((V^*)^{\otimes r} \otimes V^{\otimes s} \to k\), that is, elements of \(U^*\) with \(U = (V^*)^{\otimes r} \otimes V^{\otimes s}\). Both \(T^r_s(V)\) and \(U^*\) have dimension \(n^{r+s}\), by (3) and Proposition 11.4.2. The prescribed map exists, because the displayed expression is multilinear in \((v_1, \ldots , v_r, \varphi ^1, \ldots , \varphi ^s)\), and it is unique, because the decomposable tensors span \(T^r_s(V)\). It sends the basis vector \(e_{i_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes e_{i_r} \otimes e^{j_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes e^{j_s}\) to the linear form on \(U\) that takes the value \(1\) on \(e^{i_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes e^{i_r} \otimes e_{j_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes e_{j_s}\) and \(0\) on the other basis vectors of \(U\) of the same kind. These linear forms are the dual basis of the basis of \(U\) given by (3) (with the roles of \(V\) and \(V^*\) exchanged), so the map sends a basis to a basis and is an isomorphism.

(2) The same argument applies: multilinear maps \(V^s \to V\) are linear maps \(V^{\otimes s} \to V\), a space of dimension \(n^s \cdot n = \dim T^1_s(V)\); the prescribed map exists and is unique as in (1), and it sends \(e_i \otimes e^{j_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes e^{j_s}\) to the linear map \(V^{\otimes s} \to V\) that sends \(e_{j_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes e_{j_s}\) to \(e_i\) and the other basis vectors \(e_{l_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes e_{l_s}\) to \(0\). These maps form a basis of \(\operatorname {Hom}(V^{\otimes s}, V)\) (they are the matrix units in the bases given by (3)), so the map is an isomorphism. For \(s = 1\) it sends \(v \otimes \varphi \) to \(w \mapsto \varphi (w)v\), which is the map of Proposition 12.2.2 composed with the exchange of factors \(V \otimes V^* \cong V^* \otimes V\).

From now on we identify a tensor of type \((r,s)\) with the corresponding multilinear map of (1), and a tensor of type \((1,s)\) also with the multilinear map \(V^s \to V\) of (2); the two are related by \(T(\psi , w_1, \ldots , w_s) = \psi (T(w_1, \ldots , w_s))\), as one checks on decomposable tensors. So a bilinear form on \(V\) is the same as a tensor of type \((0,2)\), and an endomorphism the same as a tensor of type \((1,1)\).

03AO Definition 12.4.3

The components of \(T \in T^r_s(V)\) in the basis \((e_i)\) are the numbers

\[ T^{i_1 \ldots i_r}_{j_1 \ldots j_s} = T(e^{i_1}, \ldots , e^{i_r}, e_{j_1}, \ldots , e_{j_s}), \]

so that, by Proposition 12.4.2,

\[ T = \sum _{i_1, \ldots , i_r, j_1, \ldots , j_s} T^{i_1 \ldots i_r}_{j_1 \ldots j_s}\; e_{i_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes e_{i_r} \otimes e^{j_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes e^{j_s}. \]

The second formula holds because both sides take the same value on every tuple \((e^{i_1}, \ldots , e^{i_r}, e_{j_1}, \ldots , e_{j_s})\) of basis vectors, and a multilinear map is determined by these values. The components of a vector \(v = \sum _iv^ie_i\) are its coordinates \(v^i = e^i(v)\), with the index up; those of a linear form \(\varphi = \sum _j\varphi _je^j\) are \(\varphi _j = \varphi (e_j)\), with the index down. The components of an endomorphism \(A\), viewed as a tensor of type \((1,1)\), are \(A^i_j = e^i(Ae_j)\), the entries of its matrix (row \(i\), column \(j\)); the components of a bilinear form \(B\) are \(B_{ij} = B(e_i, e_j)\), the entries of its Gram matrix (Definition 11.9.1).

03AP Proposition 12.4.4 Transformation law

Let \((e^{\prime }_1, \ldots , e^{\prime }_n)\) be a second basis of \(V\), with \(e^{\prime }_j = \sum _iP^i_je_i\) for an invertible matrix \(P = (P^i_j)\) (row \(i\), column \(j\)), and let \(Q = (Q^i_j) = P^{-1}\).

  1. The dual bases are related by \(e^{\prime j} = \sum _iQ^j_ie^i\), and the coordinates of a vector by \(v^{\prime i} = \sum _jQ^i_jv^j\).

  2. The components of \(T \in T^r_s(V)\) in the two bases are related by

    \[ T^{\prime \, i_1 \ldots i_r}_{j_1 \ldots j_s} = \sum _{k_1, \ldots , k_r, l_1, \ldots , l_s} Q^{i_1}_{k_1} \cdots Q^{i_r}_{k_r}\, P^{l_1}_{j_1} \cdots P^{l_s}_{j_s}\; T^{k_1 \ldots k_r}_{l_1 \ldots l_s}. \]

  3. Conversely, suppose that to every basis \(\mathbf{e}\) of \(V\) there is assigned a family of numbers \(T(\mathbf{e})^{i_1 \ldots i_r}_{j_1 \ldots j_s}\), such that the families assigned to any two bases are related by the formula of (2). Then there is a unique \(T \in T^r_s(V)\) whose components in every basis \(\mathbf{e}\) are the numbers \(T(\mathbf{e})^{i_1 \ldots i_r}_{j_1 \ldots j_s}\).

Proof â–¼

(1) The linear form \(\sum _iQ^j_ie^i\) takes on \(e^{\prime }_m\) the value \(\sum _iQ^j_ie^i\bigl(\sum _lP^l_me_l\bigr) = \sum _iQ^j_iP^i_m = (QP)^j_m = \delta ^j_m\), so it is the \(j\)-th vector of the dual basis of \((e^{\prime }_m)\). Hence \(v^{\prime i} = e^{\prime i}(v) = \sum _jQ^i_je^j(v) = \sum _jQ^i_jv^j\).

(2) By definition and (1),

\[ T^{\prime \, i_1 \ldots i_r}_{j_1 \ldots j_s} = T\Bigl(\sum _{k_1}Q^{i_1}_{k_1}e^{k_1}, \ldots , \sum _{k_r}Q^{i_r}_{k_r}e^{k_r}, \sum _{l_1}P^{l_1}_{j_1}e_{l_1}, \ldots , \sum _{l_s}P^{l_s}_{j_s}e_{l_s}\Bigr), \]

and expanding by multilinearity gives the formula.

(3) Uniqueness holds because a tensor is determined by its components in one basis (Definition 12.4.3). For existence, fix a basis \(\mathbf{e}\) and let \(T\) be the tensor with components \(T(\mathbf{e})^{i_1 \ldots i_r}_{j_1 \ldots j_s}\) in \(\mathbf{e}\). For another basis \(\mathbf{e}^{\prime }\), the components of \(T\) in \(\mathbf{e}^{\prime }\) are obtained from those in \(\mathbf{e}\) by the formula of (2), and so are the numbers \(T(\mathbf{e}^{\prime })\) by hypothesis; so they agree.

Part (3) is the justification of the physicists’ definition, “a tensor is something that transforms like a tensor”. Part (1) explains the names. The basis vectors change by \(P\); the coordinates of a vector change by the inverse matrix \(Q\), against the basis, and are called contravariant; the components of a linear form, \(\varphi ^{\prime }_j = \varphi (e^{\prime }_j) = \sum _lP^l_j\varphi _l\), change by \(P\), together with the basis, and are called covariant. A tensor of type \((r,s)\) has \(r\) indices of the first kind, written above, and \(s\) of the second kind, written below. (These traditional names clash with the language of categories: \(V \mapsto V\) is a covariant functor and \(V \mapsto V^*\) a contravariant one, Example 6.5.5, while vectors are called contravariant. The physicists’ names refer to the components, not to the spaces.)

03AQ Example 12.4.5

Let us compare the transformation laws of an endomorphism and of a bilinear form; in both cases the components form an \(n \times n\) matrix.

  1. For an endomorphism \(A\), a tensor of type \((1,1)\), the formula of Proposition 12.4.4 reads \(A^{\prime \, i}_j = \sum _{k,l}Q^i_kA^k_lP^l_j\), that is, \(A^{\prime } = P^{-1}AP\): the matrix changes by similarity, as in Chapter 11. Similar matrices have the same eigenvalues, trace and determinant, so these are invariants of the endomorphism.

  2. For a bilinear form \(B\), a tensor of type \((0,2)\), the formula reads \(B^{\prime }_{ij} = \sum _{k,l}P^k_iP^l_jB_{kl}\), that is, \(B^{\prime } = P^TBP\): the Gram matrix changes by congruence, as in Definition 11.9.1. Congruent matrices need not have the same eigenvalues: for \(B = I\) and \(P = 2I\), \(P^TBP = 4I\). So “the eigenvalues of a bilinear form” make no sense; what survives for real symmetric forms is the signature (Theorem 11.9.5).

The same matrix can therefore mean two different things, and only the position of the indices tells which. With an inner product the two can be converted into each other (Definition 12.4.10 below), and if both bases are orthonormal for an inner product on a real vector space, then \(P\) is orthogonal, \(P^{-1} = P^T\), and the two transformation laws coincide.

03AR Example 12.4.6

  1. The identity of \(V\) is the tensor \(\sum _ie_i \otimes e^i\) of type \((1,1)\); its components are \(\delta ^i_j\) in every basis, as the transformation law confirms: \(\sum _{k,l}Q^i_k\delta ^k_lP^l_j = (QP)^i_j = \delta ^i_j\). Hence \(\sum _ie_i \otimes e^i\) does not depend on the basis, which is not obvious from the formula.

  2. A bilinear map \(\mu \colon V \times V \to V\), for instance the multiplication of a finite-dimensional algebra or the bracket of a Lie algebra, is a tensor of type \((1,2)\). Its components \(c^k_{ij} = e^k(\mu (e_i, e_j))\), defined by \(\mu (e_i, e_j) = \sum _kc^k_{ij}e_k\), are the structure constants of the algebra.

  3. The determinant in the basis \((e_i)\), the alternating form \((w_1, \ldots , w_n) \mapsto \det (e^i(w_j))\), is a tensor of type \((0,n)\). More generally, alternating forms of degree \(p\) are the tensors of type \((0,p)\) that change sign when two arguments are exchanged; Section 12.5 studies them.

  4. In Riemannian geometry, the curvature of a Riemannian manifold assigns to each tangent space a tensor of type \((1,3)\), \((X, Y, Z) \mapsto R(X, Y)Z\), and its Ricci tensor, of type \((0,2)\), is obtained from it by a contraction (Chapter 40).

The most important operation on tensors, after the tensor product, generalises the trace of an endomorphism: a vector slot and a covector slot are paired with each other.

03AS Definition 12.4.7

Let \(r, s \geq 1\), \(1 \leq a \leq r\) and \(1 \leq b \leq s\). The contraction of the \(a\)-th upper index with the \(b\)-th lower index is the linear map \(C^a_b \colon T^r_s(V) \to T^{r-1}_{s-1}(V)\) given by

\[ C^a_b(v_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes v_r \otimes \varphi ^1 \otimes \cdots \otimes \varphi ^s) = \varphi ^b(v_a)\; v_1 \otimes \cdots \widehat{v_a} \cdots \otimes v_r \otimes \varphi ^1 \otimes \cdots \widehat{\varphi ^b} \cdots \otimes \varphi ^s, \]

where the hat means that the factor is omitted.

The map exists and is unique by Proposition 12.2.1, since the right-hand side is multilinear in \((v_1, \ldots , v_r, \varphi ^1, \ldots , \varphi ^s)\). Note that no basis is used in the definition.

03AT Proposition 12.4.8

  1. In components, contraction is summation over a repeated index:

    \[ (C^a_bT)^{i_1 \ldots \widehat{i_a} \ldots i_r}_{j_1 \ldots \widehat{j_b} \ldots j_s} = \sum _{m=1}^nT^{i_1 \ldots m \ldots i_r}_{j_1 \ldots m \ldots j_s}, \]

    with \(m\) in the \(a\)-th upper and the \(b\)-th lower position.

  2. For \(r = s = 1\), \(C^1_1 \colon \operatorname {End}(V) = T^1_1(V) \to k\) is the trace.

  3. For \(T \in T^1_2(V)\), viewed as a bilinear map \(V \times V \to V\), the contraction \(C^1_1T \in T^0_1(V) = V^*\) is the linear form \(w \mapsto \operatorname {tr}\bigl(v \mapsto T(v, w)\bigr)\).

Proof â–¼

(1) Both sides are linear in \(T\), so it suffices to check the formula for a basis vector \(T = e_{k_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes e_{k_r} \otimes e^{l_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes e^{l_s}\), whose components are \(T^{i_1 \ldots i_r}_{j_1 \ldots j_s} = \delta ^{i_1}_{k_1} \cdots \delta ^{i_r}_{k_r}\delta ^{l_1}_{j_1} \cdots \delta ^{l_s}_{j_s}\). By definition \(C^a_bT = e^{l_b}(e_{k_a})\) times the tensor with the \(a\)-th and \(b\)-th factors removed, and \(e^{l_b}(e_{k_a}) = \delta ^{l_b}_{k_a} = \sum _m\delta ^m_{k_a}\delta ^{l_b}_m\). Comparing components gives the formula.

(2) By (1), \(C^1_1A = \sum _mA^m_m\), the sum of the diagonal entries of the matrix of \(A\) (Definition 12.4.3), which is the trace. Alternatively, this is the last statement of Proposition 12.2.2.

(3) By (1), \((C^1_1T)_j = \sum _mT^m_{mj}\). For fixed \(w = e_j\) the endomorphism \(v \mapsto T(v, e_j)\) has matrix entries \(e^i(T(e_l, e_j)) = T^i_{lj}\), so its trace is \(\sum _mT^m_{mj}\).

03AU Remark 12.4.9

Formulas with components contain many sums over indices that occur once above and once below, as in Propositions 12.4.4 and 12.4.8. Physicists and many geometers use the Einstein summation convention: an index that appears once as an upper and once as a lower index is summed over, and the summation sign is omitted. Thus \(v = v^ie_i\), \(A^{\prime \, i}_j = Q^i_kA^k_lP^l_j\), and the trace of \(A\) is \(A^i_i\). The convention has a built-in check: in a correct formula every index that is not summed over appears in the same position on both sides, and each summed index appears exactly once up and once down. A formula such as \(\sum _iv^iv^i\), which is not of this form, is not invariant under change of basis; it becomes invariant only once a bilinear form is used to lower one of the indices, as in \(\sum _{i,j}g_{ij}v^iv^j\). We do not use the convention in this book.

Let \(g\) be a nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form on \(V\) (Definition 11.9.1), for instance an inner product when \(k = \mathbb {R}\). Its components \(g_{ij} = g(e_i, e_j)\) form an invertible symmetric matrix, and we write \(g^{ij}\) for the entries of the inverse matrix, so that \(\sum _jg^{ij}g_{jl} = \delta ^i_l\).

03AV Definition 12.4.10

The isomorphism \(\flat \colon V \to V^*\), \(v \mapsto v^\flat = g(v, -)\), and its inverse \(\sharp \colon V^* \to V\), \(\varphi \mapsto \varphi ^\sharp \), are called lowering and raising of indices. Applying \(\flat \) to the \(a\)-th factor \(V\) of \(T^r_s(V)\), and moving the result to the last position, gives an isomorphism \(T^r_s(V) \to T^{r-1}_{s+1}(V)\); applying \(\sharp \) to a factor \(V^*\) gives an isomorphism \(T^r_s(V) \to T^{r+1}_{s-1}(V)\). The trace with respect to \(g\) of a tensor \(B\) of type \((0,2)\) is \(\operatorname {tr}_gB = C^1_1(B^\sharp )\), the trace of the endomorphism obtained by raising the first index of \(B\).

03AW Proposition 12.4.11

  1. In components, \((v^\flat )_i = \sum _jg_{ij}v^j\) and \((\varphi ^\sharp )^i = \sum _jg^{ij}\varphi _j\).

  2. For \(B\) of type \((0,2)\), the endomorphism \(B^\sharp \) is characterised by \(g(B^\sharp w, v) = B(v, w)\) for all \(v, w\), and \(\operatorname {tr}_gB = \sum _{i,j}g^{ij}B_{ij}\).

  3. If \(k = \mathbb {R}\), \(g\) is an inner product and \((e_i)\) is orthonormal, then \(g_{ij} = g^{ij} = \delta _{ij}\), raising and lowering do not change components, and \(\operatorname {tr}_gB = \sum _iB(e_i, e_i)\).

Proof â–¼

(1) \((v^\flat )_i = v^\flat (e_i) = g(v, e_i) = \sum _jv^jg_{ji}\), and \(g_{ji} = g_{ij}\). So \(\flat \) has matrix \((g_{ij})\) in the bases \((e_i)\) and \((e^i)\), and its inverse \(\sharp \) has matrix \((g^{ij})\).

(2) Write \(B = \sum _{i,j}B_{ij}e^i \otimes e^j\). Raising the first index replaces \(e^i\) by \((e^i)^\sharp = \sum _lg^{li}e_l\), so \(B^\sharp = \sum _{i,j,l}g^{li}B_{ij}\, e_l \otimes e^j\), the endomorphism \(w \mapsto \sum _{i,j,l}g^{li}B_{ij}e^j(w)\, e_l = \sum _iB(e_i, w)(e^i)^\sharp \). Hence \(g(B^\sharp w, v) = \sum _iB(e_i, w)e^i(v) = B(v, w)\); this characterises \(B^\sharp w\) because \(g\) is nondegenerate. By Proposition 12.4.8(1) the trace is \(\sum _m(B^\sharp )^m_m = \sum _{m,i}g^{mi}B_{im}\), which is \(\sum _{i,j}g^{ij}B_{ij}\) since \(g^{mi} = g^{im}\).

(3) The Gram matrix of an orthonormal basis is the identity, which is its own inverse; now apply (1) and (2).

In particular \(\operatorname {tr}_gB\) does not depend on the basis, being a contraction, although the formula \(\sum _iB(e_i, e_i)\) is only valid for orthonormal bases. For \(B = g\) one finds \(\operatorname {tr}_gg = \sum _{i,j}g^{ij}g_{ij} = n\). This is how the Ricci tensor and the scalar curvature of a Riemannian manifold are obtained from its curvature tensor (Chapter 40), and how the Laplacian \(\Delta f = \operatorname {tr}_g\operatorname {Hess}f\) is obtained from the Hessian.

03AX Exercise 12.4.12

Assume \(\operatorname {char}k \neq 2\). Show that every tensor of type \((0,2)\) is uniquely the sum of a symmetric and an alternating one, and that the symmetric ones form a subspace of dimension \(n(n+1)/2\) and the alternating ones a subspace of dimension \(n(n-1)/2\). Show that the symmetric ones are preserved by the transformation law, that is, the notion of symmetry does not depend on the basis.

Solution â–¼

Write \(B = \frac12(B + B^t) + \frac12(B - B^t)\), where \(B^t(v, w) = B(w, v)\); the first summand is symmetric and the second alternating, because \((B - B^t)(v, v) = 0\). If \(B\) is both symmetric and alternating, then \(0 = B(v + w, v + w) = B(v, w) + B(w, v) = 2B(v, w)\), so \(B = 0\); this gives uniqueness. In components, symmetric means \(B_{ij} = B_{ji}\), so a symmetric tensor is determined by its components \(B_{ij}\) with \(i \leq j\), which are arbitrary: dimension \(n + \binom n2 = n(n+1)/2\). Alternating means \(B_{ij} = -B_{ji}\) and \(B_{ii} = 0\), so the components with \(i {\lt} j\) are free: dimension \(\binom n2 = n(n-1)/2\); the two dimensions add up to \(n^2\). Symmetry is defined by the condition \(B(v, w) = B(w, v)\) for all \(v, w\), which does not mention a basis; equivalently, if \(B^T = B\) then \((P^TBP)^T = P^TB^TP = P^TBP\).

03AY Exercise 12.4.13

Let \(A, B \in \operatorname {End}(V) = T^1_1(V)\), so that \(A \otimes B \in T^2_2(V)\). Show that \(C^2_1(A \otimes B) = A \circ B\) and that \(C^1_1C^2_1(A \otimes B) = \operatorname {tr}(AB)\), while \(C^1_1C^1_1(A \otimes B) = \operatorname {tr}A\operatorname {tr}B\). Deduce that \(\operatorname {tr}(AB) = \operatorname {tr}(BA)\).

Solution â–¼

By linearity we may take \(A = v \otimes \varphi \) and \(B = w \otimes \psi \), so \(A \otimes B = v \otimes w \otimes \varphi \otimes \psi \), \(A(x) = \varphi (x)v\) and \(B(x) = \psi (x)w\). Then \(C^2_1(A \otimes B) = \varphi (w)\, v \otimes \psi \), the endomorphism \(x \mapsto \varphi (w)\psi (x)v\); and \((A \circ B)(x) = A(\psi (x)w) = \psi (x)\varphi (w)v\), the same. Applying \(C^1_1\), the trace (Proposition 12.4.8(2)), gives \(\operatorname {tr}(AB)\), which equals \(\varphi (w)\psi (v)\). On the other hand \(C^1_1(A \otimes B) = \varphi (v)\, w \otimes \psi \) and then \(C^1_1\) gives \(\varphi (v)\psi (w) = \operatorname {tr}A\operatorname {tr}B\). Finally \(\operatorname {tr}(BA)\) is computed by the same formula with the roles exchanged, \(\psi (v)\varphi (w)\), which is the same number as \(\operatorname {tr}(AB) = \varphi (w)\psi (v)\); both sides being bilinear in \((A, B)\), the identity holds for all \(A\), \(B\).
