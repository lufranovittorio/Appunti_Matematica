13.2 Representations of groups00L0

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

00L1 Definition 13.2.1

A representation of a group \(G\) is a vector space \(V\) with a homomorphism \(\rho \colon G \to \mathrm{GL}(V)\); we write \(gv\) for \(\rho (g)v\). A homomorphism of representations (or \(G\)-equivariant map) is a linear map \(f\) with \(f(gv) = gf(v)\); the space of such maps is \(\operatorname {Hom}_G(V, W)\). A subrepresentation is a \(G\)-stable subspace. \(V\) is irreducible if \(V \neq 0\) and its only subrepresentations are \(0\) and \(V\), and completely reducible (or semisimple) if it is a direct sum of irreducible subrepresentations. The invariants are \(V^G = \{ v : gv = v \text{ for all } g\} \).

00L2 Example 13.2.2

Given representations \(V\), \(W\): quotients \(V/U\) by subrepresentations, \(V \oplus W\), \(V \otimes W\) with \(g(v \otimes w) = gv \otimes gw\), the dual \(V^*\) with \((g\varphi )(v) = \varphi (g^{-1}v)\), and \(\operatorname {Hom}_k(V, W)\) with \((gf)(v) = g f(g^{-1}v)\) are representations, and \(\operatorname {Hom}_k(V, W)^G = \operatorname {Hom}_G(V, W)\). The trivial representation is \(k\) with \(gv = v\). If \(G\) acts on a finite set \(X\), the permutation representation is \(k^X\) with \(g e_x = e_{gx}\); for \(X = G\) with the left action this is the regular representation.

00L3 Lemma 13.2.3 Schur

Let \(V\), \(W\) be irreducible representations. Every \(f \in \operatorname {Hom}_G(V, W)\) is zero or an isomorphism. If \(k\) is algebraically closed and \(V\) is finite-dimensional, then \(\operatorname {Hom}_G(V, V) = k \cdot \mathrm{id}\).

Proof â–¼

\(\ker f\) and \(\operatorname {im}f\) are subrepresentations, so \(\ker f \in \{ 0, V\} \) and \(\operatorname {im}f \in \{ 0, W\} \). If \(k\) is algebraically closed and \(f \in \operatorname {Hom}_G(V, V)\), \(f\) has an eigenvalue \(\lambda \), and \(f - \lambda \in \operatorname {Hom}_G(V, V)\) is not an isomorphism, hence zero.

00L4 Theorem 13.2.4 Maschke

Let \(G\) be finite with \(\operatorname {char} k \nmid |G|\). Every subrepresentation \(U\) of a representation \(V\) has a \(G\)-stable complement. Hence every finite-dimensional representation is completely reducible.

Proof â–¼

Choose a linear projection \(\pi \colon V \to U\) (a linear map with \(\pi |_U = \mathrm{id}\)), using a complement of \(U\) (Proposition 11.2.3), and put \(\pi _0 = \frac{1}{|G|}\sum _{g \in G} g\pi g^{-1}\). It is \(G\)-equivariant, has image in \(U\), and restricts to the identity on \(U\); so \(\ker \pi _0\) is a \(G\)-stable complement of \(U\). The second statement follows by induction on the dimension.

00L5 Counterexample 13.2.5

Both hypotheses of Maschke’s theorem are needed. The group \(\mathbb {Z}\) acts on \(\mathbb {C}^2\) by \(n \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} 1 & n \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\); the line \(\mathbb {C}e_1\) is stable, but it has no stable complement, since a stable line would be spanned by an eigenvector and \(e_1\) spans the only eigenline. Similarly \(\mathbb {Z}/p\mathbb {Z}\) acts on \(\mathbb {F}_p^2\) by the same matrices (with \(n\) read modulo \(p\)), and the representation is not completely reducible although \(\mathbb {Z}/p\mathbb {Z}\) is finite; here \(p\) divides the order of the group.

13.2.1 Unitary representations03BP

Many representations met in practice do more than act linearly: they preserve lengths and angles. Permutations of the coordinates of \(\mathbb {C}^n\), rotations of the plane, the symmetries of a molecule acting on its wave functions are all of this kind. In quantum mechanics this is a principle: the states of a system are the unit vectors of a complex Hilbert space, and a symmetry of the system acts by operators preserving the inner product, so that transition probabilities \(|\langle v, w\rangle |^2\) are unchanged. Representations by such operators are called unitary. For them complete reducibility is automatic, and for finite groups every complex representation is of this kind. In this subsection \(V\) is a finite-dimensional complex vector space.

03BQ Definition 13.2.6

A representation \(\rho \colon G \to \mathrm{GL}(V)\) is unitary with respect to a Hermitian inner product \(\langle -, - \rangle \) on \(V\) (Definition 11.10.1) if

\[ \langle gv, gw \rangle = \langle v, w \rangle \qquad \text{for all } g \in G \text{ and } v, w \in V, \]

that is, if every \(\rho (g)\) is a unitary operator (Definition 11.10.4), so that \(\rho \) takes values in the unitary group \(\mathrm{U}(V)\). A representation is unitarisable if \(V\) has a Hermitian inner product for which it is unitary.

The two descriptions agree: a linear map \(f\) satisfies \(\langle fv, fw\rangle = \langle v, w\rangle \) for all \(v, w\) if and only if \(\langle f^\dagger fv, w\rangle = \langle v, w\rangle \) for all \(v, w\), that is, \(f^\dagger f = \mathrm{id}\). In an orthonormal basis of \(V\) the matrices of a unitary representation lie in \(\mathrm{U}(n)\), where \(n = \dim V\) (Lemma 11.10.5).

03BR Example 13.2.7

  1. A permutation representation \(\mathbb {C}^X\) (Example 13.2.2) is unitary for the inner product in which the \(e_x\) are orthonormal, since \(g\) permutes this orthonormal basis. In particular the regular representation of a finite group is unitary.

  2. A one-dimensional representation is a homomorphism \(\chi \colon G \to \mathbb {C}^\times \), and it is unitary for some (equivalently, every) inner product on \(\mathbb {C}\) if and only if \(|\chi (g)| = 1\) for all \(g\), since \(\langle \chi (g)v, \chi (g)v\rangle = |\chi (g)|^2\langle v, v\rangle \). So \(\mathbb {R}\to \mathbb {C}^\times \), \(t \mapsto e^{iat}\), is unitary, while \(t \mapsto e^t\) is not unitarisable. For a finite group every \(\chi (g)\) is a root of unity, so every one-dimensional representation is unitary.

  3. The group \(\mathbb {Z}\) acting on \(\mathbb {C}^2\) by \(n \mapsto \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & n \\ 0 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\) (Counterexample 13.2.5) is not unitarisable: by Proposition 13.2.8(1) below a unitarisable representation is completely reducible, and this one is not.

03BS Proposition 13.2.8

Let \(V\) be a unitary representation of a group \(G\).

  1. The orthogonal complement \(U^\perp \) of a subrepresentation \(U\) is a subrepresentation. Hence \(V\) is the orthogonal direct sum of irreducible subrepresentations.

  2. For every \(g \in G\), \(\rho (g)^\dagger = \rho (g^{-1})\), and \(\rho (g)\) is diagonalisable in an orthonormal basis, with eigenvalues of absolute value \(1\). Consequently \(\operatorname {tr}\rho (g^{-1}) = \overline{\operatorname {tr}\rho (g)}\).

  3. The map \(V \to V^*\), \(v \mapsto \langle -, v\rangle \), is a conjugate-linear bijection compatible with the actions of \(G\) (with the dual representation of Example 13.2.2 on \(V^*\)).

Proof â–¼

(1) If \(U\) is stable under \(\rho (g)\), then \(U^\perp \) is stable under \(\rho (g)^\dagger \) (Lemma 11.10.5), and \(\rho (g)^\dagger = \rho (g)^{-1} = \rho (g^{-1})\) since \(\rho (g)\) is unitary. As \(g\) runs through \(G\) so does \(g^{-1}\), so \(U^\perp \) is a subrepresentation. Now induct on \(\dim V\): if \(V\) is not irreducible (and \(V \neq 0\)), choose a subrepresentation \(0 \neq U \neq V\); then \(V = U \oplus U^\perp \) (Theorem 11.10.3), and \(U\) and \(U^\perp \) are unitary representations of smaller dimension.

(2) The first statement was shown in (1). A unitary operator is normal, so by the spectral theorem (Theorem 11.10.6) it has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors; if \(\rho (g)v = \lambda v\) with \(v \neq 0\), then \(\langle v, v\rangle = \langle \rho (g)v, \rho (g)v\rangle = |\lambda |^2\langle v, v\rangle \), so \(|\lambda | = 1\). In such a basis \(\rho (g^{-1})\) is diagonal with entries \(\lambda _i^{-1} = \bar\lambda _i\), so its trace is \(\overline{\operatorname {tr}\rho (g)}\).

(3) The map is conjugate-linear because the inner product is conjugate-linear in the second variable, and it is bijective because a nondegenerate form identifies \(V\) with \(V^*\) in this way (compute in an orthonormal basis \((u_i)\), where \(u_i \mapsto u_i^*\)). For compatibility with \(G\), write \(\varphi _v = \langle -, v\rangle \); then \((g\varphi _v)(w) = \varphi _v(g^{-1}w) = \langle g^{-1}w, v\rangle = \langle w, gv\rangle = \varphi _{gv}(w)\), using that \(g\) preserves the inner product.

03BT Theorem 13.2.9 Unitary trick for finite groups

Every finite-dimensional complex representation \(V\) of a finite group \(G\) is unitarisable: if \(\langle -, - \rangle \) is any Hermitian inner product on \(V\), then

\[ \langle v, w\rangle _G = \frac{1}{|G|}\sum _{g \in G}\langle gv, gw\rangle \]

is a Hermitian inner product for which \(V\) is unitary. Consequently every finite subgroup of \(\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb {C})\) is conjugate to a subgroup of \(\mathrm{U}(n)\).

Proof â–¼

Each summand is linear in \(v\) and satisfies \(\langle gw, gv\rangle = \overline{\langle gv, gw\rangle }\), so \(\langle -, - \rangle _G\) is Hermitian. For \(v \neq 0\) every summand \(\langle gv, gv\rangle \) is positive, since \(gv \neq 0\); so \(\langle v, v\rangle _G {\gt} 0\). For \(h \in G\),

\[ \langle hv, hw\rangle _G = \frac{1}{|G|}\sum _{g \in G}\langle ghv, ghw\rangle = \frac{1}{|G|}\sum _{g^{\prime } \in G}\langle g^{\prime }v, g^{\prime }w\rangle = \langle v, w\rangle _G, \]

because \(g \mapsto gh\) is a bijection of \(G\). For the last statement, let \(G \subseteq \mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb {C})\) be finite, acting on \(\mathbb {C}^n\). Choose an orthonormal basis of \(\mathbb {C}^n\) for \(\langle -, - \rangle _G\) by the Gram–Schmidt process (Theorem 11.10.3), and let \(P\) be the matrix whose columns are its vectors. In this basis the elements of \(G\) act by unitary matrices, that is, \(P^{-1}GP \subseteq \mathrm{U}(n)\).

Together with Proposition 13.2.8(1) this gives a second proof of Maschke’s theorem over \(\mathbb {C}\), with a canonical choice of complement: the orthogonal one. The averaging only uses that one can take the mean of a function over \(G\) in a translation invariant way. It therefore works for compact groups, where the sum is replaced by the integral against Haar measure (Chapter 34), and fails for \(\mathbb {Z}\), as Example 13.2.7(3) shows. The same argument with real inner products shows that every finite subgroup of \(\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb {R})\) is conjugate to a subgroup of \(\mathrm{O}(n)\): the finite groups of real matrices are, up to a change of basis, groups of symmetries of Euclidean space.

For an irreducible representation the invariant inner product is essentially unique.

03BU Proposition 13.2.10

Let \(V\) be an irreducible finite-dimensional complex representation of a group \(G\), unitary for two Hermitian inner products \(\langle -, - \rangle _1\) and \(\langle -, - \rangle _2\). Then \(\langle -, - \rangle _2 = c\, \langle -, - \rangle _1\) for a real number \(c {\gt} 0\).

Proof â–¼

Let \((u_i)\) be an orthonormal basis for \(\langle -, - \rangle _1\) and define a linear map \(A\) by \(Av = \sum _i\langle v, u_i\rangle _2\, u_i\). Writing \(w = \sum _j\langle w, u_j\rangle _1u_j\),

\[ \langle v, w\rangle _2 = \sum _j\overline{\langle w, u_j\rangle _1}\, \langle v, u_j\rangle _2 = \sum _j\langle v, u_j\rangle _2\, \langle u_j, w\rangle _1 = \langle Av, w\rangle _1 \]

for all \(v, w\). The map \(A\) is \(G\)-equivariant: for \(g \in G\), \(\langle Agv, w\rangle _1 = \langle gv, w\rangle _2 = \langle v, g^{-1}w\rangle _2 = \langle Av, g^{-1}w\rangle _1 = \langle gAv, w\rangle _1\) for all \(w\), so \(Agv = gAv\). By Schur’s lemma (Lemma 13.2.3) \(A = c\, \mathrm{id}\) for some \(c \in \mathbb {C}\), and \(c = \langle v, v\rangle _2/\langle v, v\rangle _1 {\gt} 0\) for any \(v \neq 0\).

Irreducibility is needed: on the trivial representation \(\mathbb {C}^2\) every inner product is invariant.

03BV Remark 13.2.11

In analysis and physics the important unitary representations are infinite-dimensional. A unitary representation of a topological group \(G\) on a Hilbert space \(H\) is a homomorphism from \(G\) to the group of unitary operators of \(H\) such that \(g \mapsto gv\) is continuous for every \(v \in H\) (Chapter 30). Examples are the translations \((T_af)(x) = f(x - a)\) of \(\mathbb {R}\) on \(L^2(\mathbb {R})\), and the rotations of the circle on \(L^2(S^1)\). The latter decomposes as the orthogonal Hilbert sum of the one-dimensional representations spanned by the functions \(e^{in\theta }\), \(n \in \mathbb {Z}\): this is the theory of Fourier series. For a compact group \(G\) the Peter–Weyl theorem gives the analogous decomposition of \(L^2(G)\), in which every irreducible representation \(V\) occurs \(\dim V\) times [BtD85, Chapter III]. For \(G = \mathbb {R}\), Stone’s theorem says that every unitary representation is of the form \(t \mapsto e^{itA}\) for a self-adjoint, in general unbounded, operator \(A\) [RS80, Theorem VIII.8]; in quantum mechanics, the time evolution of a system is such a representation and \(A\) is its energy, the Hamiltonian.

03BW Exercise 13.2.12

Let \(S_3\) act on \(\mathbb {C}^3\) by permuting the coordinates, with the standard inner product. Show that the line spanned by \((1, 1, 1)\) and its orthogonal complement \(W = \{ x + y + z = 0\} \) are subrepresentations, and that \(W\) is irreducible.

Solution â–¼

Permutations fix \((1, 1, 1)\), so its span is a subrepresentation, and the representation is unitary (Example 13.2.7(1)), so \(W\) is a subrepresentation by Proposition 13.2.8(1). If \(W\) were reducible, it would contain a stable line spanned by some \(v = (a, b, c) \neq 0\) with \(a + b + c = 0\), and each transposition, having order \(2\), would act on it by \(\pm 1\). If \((1\, 2)v = v\) then \(a = b\) and \(v = (a, a, -2a)\), and \((2\, 3)v = (a, -2a, a) = \pm v\) forces \(a = 0\). If \((1\, 2)v = -v\) then \(b = -a\) and \(c = 0\), so \(v = (a, -a, 0)\), and \((2\, 3)v = (a, 0, -a) = \pm v\) again forces \(a = 0\). Both cases contradict \(v \neq 0\).
