37.3 The de Rham theorem01AH

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

01AI Theorem 37.3.1 de Rham

For every smooth manifold \(M\) there are natural isomorphisms

\[ H^k_{\mathrm{dR}}(M) \cong H^k(M, \mathbb {R}_M) \cong H^k(M; \mathbb {R}), \]

the first to sheaf cohomology of the constant sheaf, the second to singular cohomology with real coefficients. Under these isomorphisms the wedge product corresponds to the cup product, and the isomorphism is induced by integration: the class of a closed \(k\)-form \(\omega \) corresponds to the singular cochain \(\sigma \mapsto \int _{\Delta ^k}\sigma ^*\omega \) on smooth simplices.

Proof â–¼

Let \(\underline{\Omega }^k\) be the sheaf \(U \mapsto \Omega ^k(U)\). The sequence of sheaves

\[ 0 \to \mathbb {R}_M \to \underline{\Omega }^0 \xrightarrow {d} \underline{\Omega }^1 \xrightarrow {d} \cdots \]

is exact: exactness at \(\underline{\Omega }^0\) is Proposition 37.2.2, and exactness at \(\underline{\Omega }^k\) for \(k \geq 1\) holds on stalks, because every point has a basis of convex coordinate neighbourhoods, on which closed forms are exact by the Poincaré lemma (Theorem 37.2.4). Each \(\underline{\Omega }^k\) is a module over the sheaf of smooth functions, hence fine, hence acyclic on the paracompact space \(M\) (Example 26.4.3 and Theorem 26.4.5). So this is an acyclic resolution of \(\mathbb {R}_M\) and Proposition 26.7.1(3) gives \(H^k(M, \mathbb {R}_M) \cong H^k(\Gamma (M, \underline{\Omega }^\bullet )) = H^k_{\mathrm{dR}}(M)\). The second isomorphism is Theorem 26.6.1, since manifolds are locally contractible and paracompact, and it is compatible with products. The description by integration over smooth simplices is the standard comparison of the two resolutions; see [War83, Chapter 5] or [Bre97, Chapter III].

01AJ Corollary 37.3.2

For a compact manifold \(M\) the spaces \(H^k_{\mathrm{dR}}(M)\) are finite-dimensional, they vanish for \(k {\gt} \dim M\), and \(\dim H^k_{\mathrm{dR}}(M) = b_k(M)\), the \(k\)-th Betti number. For a compact oriented \(n\)-manifold, \(H^n_{\mathrm{dR}}(M) \cong \mathbb {R}\) for each connected component, with the isomorphism given by integration.

Proof â–¼

Compact manifolds have finitely generated singular homology [Hat02, Appendix], so Theorem 37.3.1 and the universal coefficient theorem give finite dimension and the identification with Betti numbers; the vanishing above the dimension holds because there are no nonzero forms of degree \({\gt} n\). For the top degree, \(H^n(M; \mathbb {R}) \cong H_0(M; \mathbb {R}) \cong \mathbb {R}\) per component by Poincaré duality (Theorem 23.6.2), and integration is nonzero on the class of a volume form (Corollary 36.4.13).

Explicitly, if \(M_1, \ldots , M_c\) are the connected components of the compact oriented manifold \(M\) (finitely many, as they are open and cover the compact \(M\)), then \(\Omega ^\bullet (M) = \prod _i\Omega ^\bullet (M_i)\), so \(H^n_{\mathrm{dR}}(M) = \bigoplus _iH^n_{\mathrm{dR}}(M_i)\), and the isomorphism of the corollary is

\[ H^n_{\mathrm{dR}}(M) \to \mathbb {R}^c, \qquad [\omega ] \mapsto \Bigl(\int _{M_1}\omega , \ldots , \int _{M_c}\omega \Bigr). \]

So a top degree form on \(M\) is exact if and only if its integral over each component vanishes; the vanishing of the total integral \(\int _M\omega \) is not enough when \(M\) is disconnected. For example, on \(M = S^2 \sqcup S^2\) the form equal to the area form on the first sphere and to minus the area form on the second has total integral \(0\) but is not exact.

02CX Proposition 37.3.3

Let \(M\) be a connected manifold and \(x_0 \in M\). There is an isomorphism

\[ H^1_{\mathrm{dR}}(M) \xrightarrow {\ \sim \ } \operatorname {Hom}(\pi _1(M, x_0), \mathbb {R}), \]

sending \([\omega ]\) to the homomorphism whose value on the class of a piecewise smooth loop \(\gamma \) is the period \(\int _\gamma \omega \). In particular \(H^1_{\mathrm{dR}}(M) = 0\) if \(M\) is simply connected, and a closed \(1\)-form is exact if and only if all its periods vanish.

Proof â–¼

Compose the isomorphism \(H^1_{\mathrm{dR}}(M) \cong H^1(M; \mathbb {R})\) of Theorem 37.3.1, given by integration over smooth simplices, with \(H^1(M; \mathbb {R}) \cong \operatorname {Hom}_\mathbb {R}(H_1(M; \mathbb {R}), \mathbb {R}) = \operatorname {Hom}(H_1(M), \mathbb {R})\) (Theorem 22.2.3 over the field \(\mathbb {R}\)) and with \(\operatorname {Hom}(H_1(M), \mathbb {R}) \cong \operatorname {Hom}(\pi _1(M, x_0), \mathbb {R})\), which holds by the Hurewicz theorem (Theorem 21.9.1) because homomorphisms to the abelian group \(\mathbb {R}\) factor through the abelianisation. The composite is the period map.

The last statement can also be seen directly. If \(\omega = df\), then \(\int _\gamma \omega = f(\gamma (1)) - f(\gamma (0)) = 0\) for loops. Conversely, if all periods vanish, \(f(x) = \int _{x_0}^x\omega \) along any piecewise smooth path is well defined, since two paths differ by a loop, and in a chart around \(x\) that is a ball, integrating along segments shows \(df = \omega \), by the Poincaré lemma on the ball (Theorem 37.2.4): there \(\omega = dg\), and \(f - g\) is constant.

02CY Example 37.3.4

On \(M = \mathbb {R}^2 \setminus \{ 0\} \) the angle form \(\omega = \frac{x\, dy - y\, dx}{x^2 + y^2}\) is closed, and its period on the unit circle is \(2\pi \), so it is not exact. Since \(\pi _1(M) \cong \mathbb {Z}\), Proposition 37.3.3 gives \(H^1_{\mathrm{dR}}(M) = \mathbb {R}[\omega ]\): a closed \(1\)-form \(\eta \) on \(M\) is exact if and only if \(\int _{S^1}\eta = 0\), and in general \(\eta - \frac{1}{2\pi }\big(\int _{S^1}\eta \big)\omega \) is exact. On the simply connected \(\mathbb {R}^3 \setminus \{ 0\} \), by contrast, every closed \(1\)-form is exact.

029X Counterexample 37.3.5

De Rham cohomology of a noncompact manifold can be infinite-dimensional. On \(M = \mathbb {C}\setminus \mathbb {Z}\) let \(\alpha _n\) be the angle form centred at \(n\), the pullback of the angle form of Counterexample 36.2.20 by \(z \mapsto z - n\). It is closed, and its integral over the circle \(\gamma _m\) of radius \(\frac12\) around \(m\) is \(2\pi \) if \(m = n\) and \(0\) otherwise (for \(m \neq n\) the circle lies in a half-plane, where \(\alpha _n\) is exact). If \(\sum _nc_n\alpha _n = d f\) for finitely many nonzero \(c_n\), integrating over \(\gamma _m\) gives \(2\pi c_m = 0\) (Proposition 36.2.19). So the classes \([\alpha _n]\), \(n \in \mathbb {Z}\), are linearly independent in \(H^1_{\mathrm{dR}}(M)\).

029Y Proposition 37.3.6

Let a finite group \(G\) act freely on \(M\) by diffeomorphisms, and \(\pi \colon M \to M/G\) the quotient (Theorem 33.7.1). Then \(\pi ^*\) induces an isomorphism \(H^k_{\mathrm{dR}}(M/G) \cong H^k_{\mathrm{dR}}(M)^G\) onto the classes fixed by \(G\).

Proof â–¼

As \(\pi \) is a local diffeomorphism and \(\pi \circ g = \pi \), \(\pi ^*\) identifies \(\Omega ^\bullet (M/G)\) with the complex \(\Omega ^\bullet (M)^G\) of \(G\)-invariant forms: an invariant form descends, because locally \(\pi \) has smooth inverses that differ by elements of \(G\). The averaging operator \(A = \frac{1}{|G|}\sum _{g \in G}g^*\) commutes with \(d\) and is a projection of \(\Omega ^\bullet (M)\) onto \(\Omega ^\bullet (M)^G\). The map \(H^k(\Omega ^\bullet (M)^G) \to H^k_{\mathrm{dR}}(M)^G\) is injective: if an invariant \(\omega \) equals \(d\eta \), then \(\omega = A\omega = d(A\eta )\) with \(A\eta \) invariant. It is surjective: if \([\omega ]\) is \(G\)-fixed, then \(A\omega \) is invariant, closed, and cohomologous to \(\omega \), since each \(g^*\omega \) is.

029Z Example 37.3.7

\(H^k_{\mathrm{dR}}(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^n) \cong \mathbb {R}\) for \(k = 0\), and for \(k = n\) if \(n\) is odd; it vanishes otherwise. By Proposition 37.3.6 and Example 37.2.10, only the action of the antipodal map \(a\) on \(H^n_{\mathrm{dR}}(S^n) \cong \mathbb {R}\) matters. The volume form of \(S^n\) is \(\iota _X\Omega \) restricted to \(S^n\), where \(\Omega = dx^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge dx^{n+1}\) and \(X = \sum x^i\partial _i\); as \(a\) is the restriction of \(-\mathrm{id}\), \(a^*\Omega = (-1)^{n+1}\Omega \) and \(a\) preserves \(X\), so \(a^*\) acts on \(H^n\) by \((-1)^{n+1}\), and the invariant part is \(H^n\) for \(n\) odd and \(0\) for \(n\) even. In particular \(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^n\) is not orientable for \(n\) even, by Corollary 37.3.2.

02A0 Theorem 37.3.8 Künneth formula

If \(M\) has a finite good cover, the map \(\bigoplus _{p+q=k}H^p_{\mathrm{dR}}(M) \otimes H^q_{\mathrm{dR}}(N) \to H^k_{\mathrm{dR}}(M \times N)\), \([\alpha ] \otimes [\beta ] \mapsto [\mathrm{pr}_M^*\alpha \wedge \mathrm{pr}_N^*\beta ]\), is an isomorphism for every manifold \(N\).

Proof â–¼

Write \(\psi _M\) for the map. It is natural in \(M\) for restrictions to open subsets. If \(M \cong \mathbb {R}^m\), then \(\mathrm{pr}_N \colon M \times N \to N\) is a homotopy equivalence and \(H^\bullet _{\mathrm{dR}}(M) = \mathbb {R}\) in degree \(0\), so \(\psi _M\) is \(\mathrm{pr}_N^*\), an isomorphism by Theorem 37.2.4. For open \(U, V \subseteq M\), tensoring the Mayer–Vietoris sequence of \(U, V\) with the vector space \(H^q_{\mathrm{dR}}(N)\) and summing over \(q\) gives an exact sequence, which \(\psi \) maps to the Mayer–Vietoris sequence of \(U \times N\), \(V \times N\). The squares involving restrictions commute, and so does the one with the connecting maps: with a partition of unity \(\{ \rho _U, \rho _V\} \) on \(U \cup V\), the connecting maps are \([\omega ] \mapsto [d(\rho _V\omega )]\) on both sides, and for closed \(\beta \), \(d(\mathrm{pr}_M^*(\rho _V\omega ) \wedge \mathrm{pr}_N^*\beta ) = \mathrm{pr}_M^*d(\rho _V\omega ) \wedge \mathrm{pr}_N^*\beta \). So if \(\psi \) is an isomorphism for \(U\), \(V\) and \(U \cap V\), it is one for \(U \cup V\) by the five lemma (Lemma 15.4.2). Now let \(U_1, \ldots , U_k\) be a finite good cover of \(M\); we show by induction on \(k\) that \(\psi \) is an isomorphism for every open set covered by \(k\) open sets forming a good cover. For \(k = 1\) this is the case \(\mathbb {R}^m\). For the induction step apply the five lemma to \(W = U_1 \cup \cdots \cup U_{k-1}\) and \(U_k\), noting that \(W \cap U_k\) is covered by the \(k - 1\) sets \(U_i \cap U_k\), which form a good cover.

02A1 Corollary 37.3.9

\(H^\bullet _{\mathrm{dR}}(T^n)\) is the exterior algebra on the classes of \(dx^1, \ldots , dx^n\), so \(b_k(T^n) = \binom nk\); and \(H^\bullet _{\mathrm{dR}}(S^1 \times S^2)\) has dimensions \(1, 1, 1, 1\).

Proof â–¼

By Example 37.2.10, \(H^\bullet (S^1)\) is spanned by \(1\) and \([d\theta ]\), and \(T^n = (S^1)^n\) has a finite good cover; Theorem 37.3.8 and induction give the tensor product of \(n\) copies of \(\mathbb {R}\oplus \mathbb {R}[d\theta ]\), which is the exterior algebra because \(d\theta \wedge d\theta = 0\) and the wedge product is graded commutative. The second statement is the same computation with \(H^\bullet (S^2)\).

03GN Remark 37.3.10

De Rham cohomology is a vector space, and it does not see torsion. The spaces \(\Omega ^k(M)\) are real vector spaces and \(d\) is \(\mathbb {R}\)-linear, so \(H^k_{\mathrm{dR}}(M)\) is a real vector space, not just an abelian group. When it is finite-dimensional, for instance for compact \(M\) (Corollary 37.3.2), a choice of basis gives \(H^k_{\mathrm{dR}}(M) \cong \mathbb {R}^{b_k}\), and the only invariant left is the dimension. A real vector space has no torsion: \(mv = 0\) with \(m \neq 0\) forces \(v = m^{-1}(mv) = 0\). Integer phenomena are therefore lost. By Theorem 37.3.1 and the universal coefficient theorem (Theorem 22.2.3) over the field \(\mathbb {R}\),

\[ H^k_{\mathrm{dR}}(M) \cong H^k(M; \mathbb {R}) \cong \operatorname {Hom}(H_k(M), \mathbb {R}), \]

and \(\operatorname {Hom}(\mathbb {Z}/m\mathbb {Z}, \mathbb {R}) = 0\), since the image of a generator \(x\) would satisfy \(m\varphi (x) = 0\). So if \(H_k(M) \cong \mathbb {Z}^{r} \oplus T\) with \(T\) finite, then \(b_k = r\): de Rham cohomology records the free part of homology and forgets \(T\). For example (Examples 37.2.10 and 37.3.7), \(S^3\) and \(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^3\) have the same de Rham cohomology, \(\mathbb {R}\) in degrees \(0\) and \(3\), although \(H_1(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^3) \cong \mathbb {Z}/2\mathbb {Z}\) while \(H_1(S^3) = 0\); and the Klein bottle has \(H_1 \cong \mathbb {Z}\oplus \mathbb {Z}/2\mathbb {Z}\) (Exercise 21.10.6) and \(H^1_{\mathrm{dR}} \cong \mathbb {R}\) (Example 37.3.12(2)). To see torsion one needs integer coefficients, singular or Čech cohomology with values in \(\mathbb {Z}\). This matters for the Hodge conjecture: its rational form only concerns \(H^{2p}(X; \mathbb {Q})\), while torsion classes in \(H^{2p}(X; \mathbb {Z})\) are responsible for the first counterexamples to the integral version (Chapter 73).

Because the groups are vector spaces, computations can be done by counting dimensions. The basic tool is the following.

03GO Proposition 37.3.11

  1. If \(0 \to V_1 \to V_2 \to \cdots \to V_m \to 0\) is an exact sequence of finite-dimensional vector spaces, then \(\sum _{i=1}^m(-1)^i\dim V_i = 0\).

  2. Let \(M = U \cup V\) with \(U\), \(V\) open. If two of the three manifolds \(M\), \(U \sqcup V\) and \(U \cap V\) have finite-dimensional de Rham cohomology (in all degrees together), then so does the third, and the de Rham Euler characteristics \(\chi (W) = \sum _k(-1)^k\dim H^k_{\mathrm{dR}}(W)\) satisfy

    \[ \chi (M) = \chi (U) + \chi (V) - \chi (U \cap V). \]

  3. A manifold with a finite good cover, a finite open cover \(U_1, \ldots , U_k\) in which every nonempty intersection \(U_{i_1} \cap \cdots \cap U_{i_p}\) is diffeomorphic to \(\mathbb {R}^n\), has finite-dimensional de Rham cohomology.

Here \(H^k_{\mathrm{dR}}(U \sqcup V) = H^k_{\mathrm{dR}}(U) \oplus H^k_{\mathrm{dR}}(V)\), since a form on a disjoint union is a pair of forms. For compact \(M\), \(\chi (M)\) is the Euler characteristic of Chapter 21, because \(\dim H^k_{\mathrm{dR}}(M) = b_k(M)\) (Corollary 37.3.2). The formula in (2) is the analogue of the inclusion–exclusion principle \(|U \cup V| = |U| + |V| - |U \cap V|\) for finite sets: the Euler characteristic behaves like a “number of points”, and for a finite set of points it is exactly that.

Proof â–¼

(1) Put \(V_0 = V_{m+1} = 0\) and let \(f_i \colon V_i \to V_{i+1}\), \(0 \leq i \leq m\), be the maps, so that \(f_0 = 0\) and \(f_m = 0\). By rank–nullity (Theorem 11.2.6), \(\dim V_i = \dim \ker f_i + \dim \operatorname {im}f_i\), and exactness says \(\dim \ker f_i = \dim \operatorname {im}f_{i-1}\). So \(\sum _i(-1)^i\dim V_i = \sum _i(-1)^i(\dim \operatorname {im}f_{i-1} + \dim \operatorname {im}f_i)\), a telescoping sum equal to \(\pm \dim \operatorname {im}f_0 \pm \dim \operatorname {im}f_m = 0\).

(2) First, if \(A \xrightarrow {a} B \xrightarrow {b} C\) is exact with \(A\) and \(C\) finite-dimensional, so is \(B\): by rank–nullity \(\dim B = \dim \ker b + \dim \operatorname {im}b\), and \(\ker b = \operatorname {im}a\) has dimension at most \(\dim A\), while \(\operatorname {im}b \subseteq C\). In the Mayer–Vietoris sequence (Theorem 37.2.9)

\[ \cdots \to H^{k-1}_{\mathrm{dR}}(U \cap V) \to H^k_{\mathrm{dR}}(M) \to H^k_{\mathrm{dR}}(U \sqcup V) \to H^k_{\mathrm{dR}}(U \cap V) \to H^{k+1}_{\mathrm{dR}}(M) \to \cdots \]

each space sits between a space of each of the other two kinds, so this argument shows that the third kind is finite-dimensional. All the spaces vanish above \(\dim M\), so the Mayer–Vietoris sequence is a finite exact sequence of finite-dimensional spaces; apply (1). In it, \(H^k(M)\), \(H^k(U) \oplus H^k(V)\) and \(H^k(U \cap V)\) occupy the positions \(3k + 1\), \(3k + 2\), \(3k + 3\), with signs \((-1)^{k+1}\), \((-1)^k\), \((-1)^{k+1}\) in the alternating sum, which gives \(-\chi (M) + \chi (U) + \chi (V) - \chi (U \cap V) = 0\).

(3) By induction on the number \(k\) of sets in a good cover: for \(k = 1\) the manifold is diffeomorphic to \(\mathbb {R}^n\), whose cohomology is \(\mathbb {R}\) in degree \(0\) by the Poincaré lemma (Theorem 37.2.4). We prove that every open set covered by \(k\) sets forming a good cover has finite-dimensional cohomology. For the induction step write the union as \(W \cup U_k\) with \(W = U_1 \cup \cdots \cup U_{k-1}\); then \(W \cap U_k\) is covered by the (at most) \(k - 1\) nonempty sets \(U_i \cap U_k\), which form a good cover of it because their intersections are intersections of the \(U_j\). By induction \(W\) and \(W \cap U_k\) have finite-dimensional cohomology, and so does \(U_k \cong \mathbb {R}^n\); so (2) applies.

03GP Example 37.3.12

  1. The torus. Write \(T^2 = S^1 \times S^1\) as the union of two open annuli \(U = S^1 \times A\) and \(V = S^1 \times B\), where \(A\) and \(B\) are open arcs covering \(S^1\); then \(U \cap V\) is a disjoint union of two annuli. Each annulus is homotopy equivalent to \(S^1\) (Theorem 37.2.4), which has \(\chi = 1 - 1 = 0\) (Example 37.2.10). So \(\chi (T^2) = 0 + 0 - 0 = 0\). Since \(T^2\) is compact, connected and orientable, \(b_0 = b_2 = 1\) (Proposition 37.2.2 and Corollary 37.3.2), and \(1 - b_1 + 1 = 0\) gives \(b_1 = 2\), as in Corollary 37.3.9, but without computing any map in the sequence. In general, the Euler characteristic of a Mayer–Vietoris decomposition is computed from the pieces alone, while the individual \(b_k\) depend on the maps; dimension counting gives the \(b_k\) as soon as all but one of them are known.

  2. The Klein bottle. The Klein bottle is \(K = T^2/G\) for the group \(G = \{ 1, \tau \} \) generated by the free involution \(\tau (x, y) = (x + \frac12, -y)\) of \(T^2 = \mathbb {R}^2/\mathbb {Z}^2\). By Corollary 37.3.9, \(H^1_{\mathrm{dR}}(T^2)\) has basis \([dx]\), \([dy]\) and \(H^2_{\mathrm{dR}}(T^2)\) basis \([dx \wedge dy]\); and \(\tau ^*dx = dx\), \(\tau ^*dy = -dy\), \(\tau ^*(dx \wedge dy) = -dx \wedge dy\). By Proposition 37.3.6 the invariant classes give \(H^\bullet _{\mathrm{dR}}(K)\): \(\mathbb {R}\), \(\mathbb {R}[dx]\), \(0\) in degrees \(0\), \(1\), \(2\). So \(\chi (K) = 1 - 1 + 0 = 0\), as for the torus, while \(H^2_{\mathrm{dR}}(K) = 0\) shows that \(K\) is not orientable, since a compact connected oriented surface has \(H^2_{\mathrm{dR}} \cong \mathbb {R}\) (Corollary 37.3.2). Compare with \(H_1(K) \cong \mathbb {Z}\oplus \mathbb {Z}/2\mathbb {Z}\) (Exercise 21.10.6): the free part \(\mathbb {Z}\) gives \(b_1 = 1\), and the torsion \(\mathbb {Z}/2\mathbb {Z}\) is invisible (Remark 37.3.10).

  3. Counting with the Euler characteristic. If a compact connected surface \(S\) is the union of open sets whose cohomology is known, (2) of the proposition gives \(\chi (S)\), and then \(b_1 = b_0 + b_2 - \chi (S)\) with \(b_0 = 1\) and \(b_2 = 1\) or \(0\) according as \(S\) is orientable or not. Exercise 37.5.13 does this for the surfaces of genus \(g\).

01AK Theorem 37.3.13 Poincaré duality in de Rham cohomology

Let \(M\) be an oriented \(n\)-manifold. The pairing

\[ H^k_{\mathrm{dR}}(M) \times H^{n-k}_{\mathrm{dR},c}(M) \to \mathbb {R}, \qquad ([\alpha ], [\beta ]) \mapsto \int _M\alpha \wedge \beta , \]

is well defined and nondegenerate if \(M\) has a finite good cover (for instance if \(M\) is compact); in that case \(H^k_{\mathrm{dR}}(M) \cong H^{n-k}_{\mathrm{dR},c}(M)^*\). For compact oriented \(M\) this is the statement that \(\int _M\alpha \wedge \beta \) is a perfect pairing between \(H^k_{\mathrm{dR}}(M)\) and \(H^{n-k}_{\mathrm{dR}}(M)\).

Proof â–¼

The pairing is well defined by Stokes’ theorem: if \(\alpha \) is closed and \(\beta = d\gamma \) has compact support, then \(\alpha \wedge \beta = \pm d(\alpha \wedge \gamma )\) has compact support and integrates to zero. Nondegeneracy is proved by induction on the number of sets in a finite good cover, one in which all nonempty intersections are diffeomorphic to \(\mathbb {R}^n\) (such covers exist on any manifold, and finite ones on compact manifolds), comparing the Mayer–Vietoris sequences of \(H^\bullet _{\mathrm{dR}}\) and \(H^\bullet _{\mathrm{dR},c}\) with the five lemma; see [BT82, Chapter I, Section 5]. Alternatively it follows from Theorem 37.3.1 and topological Poincaré duality (Corollary 23.6.3), since the wedge product corresponds to the cup product.

02A2 Theorem 37.3.14 Cartan–Chevalley–Eilenberg

Let \(G\) be a compact connected Lie group. Every de Rham class of \(G\) has a unique representative that is invariant under left and right translations, and the bi-invariant forms are closed. Consequently \(H^\bullet _{\mathrm{dR}}(G) \cong (\Lambda ^\bullet \mathfrak {g}^*)^{G}\), the space of \(\operatorname {Ad}(G)\)-invariant alternating forms on the Lie algebra.

Proof â–¼

\(G \times G\) acts on \(G\) by \((a, b) \cdot x = axb^{-1}\); let \(\mu \) be the Haar probability measure on the compact connected group \(K = G \times G\) (Theorem 34.5.1) and, for a form \(\omega \), let \(A\omega = \int _Kk^*\omega \, d\mu (k)\), defined pointwise; \(A\omega \) is smooth and bi-invariant, \(A\) commutes with \(d\) (differentiation under the integral sign), and \(A\omega = \omega \) for bi-invariant \(\omega \).

Averaging does not change classes. Let \(\omega \) be closed. Each \(k \in K\) is connected to the identity by a smooth path, so \(k^*\omega \) is cohomologous to \(\omega \) (Theorem 37.2.4). For a closed form \(\eta \) of complementary degree, Fubini’s theorem gives \(\int _GA\omega \wedge \eta = \int _K(\int _Gk^*\omega \wedge \eta )\, d\mu (k) = \int _G\omega \wedge \eta \), since the inner integral depends only on the class of \(k^*\omega \) (Stokes). By Poincaré duality (Theorem 37.3.13), \([A\omega ] = [\omega ]\).

Bi-invariant forms are closed. The inversion \(\iota (x) = x^{-1}\) satisfies \(\iota \circ L_a = R_{a^{-1}} \circ \iota \), so it maps bi-invariant forms to bi-invariant forms, and \(d_e\iota = -\mathrm{id}\) (differentiate \(\exp (tX)^{-1} = \exp (-tX)\)). A bi-invariant form is determined by its value at \(e\), so \(\iota ^*\omega = (-1)^k\omega \) for a bi-invariant \(k\)-form. As \(d\omega \) is bi-invariant of degree \(k + 1\), \((-1)^{k+1}d\omega = \iota ^*d\omega = d\iota ^*\omega = (-1)^kd\omega \), so \(d\omega = 0\).

Uniqueness. If a bi-invariant \(\omega \) is exact, \(\omega = d\eta \), then \(\omega = A\omega = d(A\eta ) = 0\), because \(A\eta \) is bi-invariant, hence closed. So bi-invariant forms map isomorphically onto \(H^\bullet _{\mathrm{dR}}(G)\). A left-invariant form is determined by its value \(\omega _e \in \Lambda ^\bullet \mathfrak {g}^*\), and it is also right-invariant if and only if \(\omega _e\) is invariant under \(\operatorname {Ad}(g) = d_e(L_g \circ R_{g^{-1}})\).

02A3 Example 37.3.15

For \(G = \mathrm{SU}(2)\), \(\mathfrak {g} = \mathfrak {su}(2) \cong \mathbb {R}^3\) and \(\operatorname {Ad}\) maps \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\) onto \(\mathrm{SO}(3)\) (Exercise 34.7.2). The \(\mathrm{SO}(3)\)-invariants in \(\Lambda ^\bullet \mathbb {R}^3\) are the constants and the multiples of the determinant \(e_1^* \wedge e_2^* \wedge e_3^*\), since \(\Lambda ^1\) and \(\Lambda ^2 \cong \mathbb {R}^3\) are irreducible nontrivial representations. So \(H^\bullet (\mathrm{SU}(2))\) is \(\mathbb {R}\) in degrees \(0\) and \(3\), in accordance with \(\mathrm{SU}(2) \cong S^3\).
