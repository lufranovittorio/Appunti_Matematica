40.6 The exponential map and minimising geodesics027P

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

Geodesics are the projections to \(M\) of the integral curves of a vector field on \(TM\), the geodesic spray, which in the coordinates \((x, v)\) of \(TM\) is \(v^k\partial _{x^k} - \Gamma ^k_{ij}(x)v^iv^j\partial _{v^k}\). By Theorem 33.9.2, the set \(\mathcal{E} \subseteq TM\) of vectors \(v\) for which \(\gamma _v\) is defined on \([0, 1]\) is open, and \(\exp \colon \mathcal{E} \to M\) is smooth. Since \(\gamma _{cv}(t) = \gamma _v(ct)\), each \(\mathcal{E}_p = \mathcal{E} \cap T_pM\) is star-shaped about \(0\), and \(\exp _p(tv) = \gamma _v(t)\).

02VY Proposition 40.6.1 The geodesic spray and the Sasaki metric

Let \((M, g)\) be a Riemannian manifold, \(\pi \colon TM \to M\), and \(T(TM) = H \oplus V\) the splitting into the horizontal distribution of the Levi-Civita connection and the vertical subbundle (Proposition 38.7.3), with connection map \(K\).

  1. The vector field \(G\) on \(TM\) with \(G(v) = v^h_v\), the horizontal lift of \(v \in T_pM\) to the point \(v\) itself, is smooth, and its integral curves are the velocity curves \(t \mapsto \dot\gamma (t)\) of the geodesics. So the flow of \(G\), the geodesic flow, is \(\Phi _t(v) = \dot\gamma _v(t)\).

  2. \(g_S(A, B) = g(d\pi \, A, d\pi \, B) + g(KA, KB)\) is a Riemannian metric on \(TM\), the Sasaki metric, for which \(H \perp V\), \(d\pi \colon H_v \to T_pM\) and \(K \colon V_v \to T_pM\) are isometries; in particular \(\pi \) is a Riemannian submersion.

Proof â–¼

(1) In coordinates \((x, v)\) on \(TM\), by Proposition 38.7.3(1), \(G(x, v) = (v, -\theta _x(v)v) = \sum _kv^k\partial _{x^k} - \sum _{i,j,k}\Gamma ^k_{ij}v^iv^j\partial _{v^k}\), which is smooth. A curve \(u(t) = (x(t), v(t))\) is an integral curve if and only if \(\dot x = v\) and \(\dot v^k = -\sum \Gamma ^k_{ij}v^iv^j\), that is, \(\ddot x^k + \sum \Gamma ^k_{ij}\dot x^i\dot x^j = 0\) with \(v = \dot x\): the geodesic equation of Definition 40.2.9. Equivalently, by Proposition 38.7.3(3), the curve \(\dot\gamma \) over \(\gamma \) is horizontal if and only if it is parallel, \(D_t\dot\gamma = 0\), and its projection has velocity \(\dot\gamma \) exactly when it is an integral curve of \(G\).

(2) \(g_S\) is symmetric and smooth. If \(g_S(A, A) = 0\) then \(d\pi \, A = 0\), so \(A\) is vertical, and \(KA = 0\), so \(A = 0\) (\(K\) is injective on \(V\), being the inverse of the vertical lift); hence \(g_S\) is positive definite. For \(A \in H\) and \(B \in V\), \(KA = 0\) and \(d\pi \, B = 0\), so \(g_S(A, B) = 0\). The other statements follow from the definition.

So geodesics are the projections of the integral curves of a single vector field on \(TM\), so their properties (existence, uniqueness, smooth dependence) are those of flows (Theorem 33.9.2); on \(T^*M\) the same flow is Hamiltonian (Chapter 41).

01CE Proposition 40.6.2

\(d_0\exp _p = \mathrm{id}\), so \(\exp _p\) is a diffeomorphism from a neighbourhood of \(0\) onto a neighbourhood of \(p\), giving normal coordinates in which \(g_{ij}(p) = \delta _{ij}\) and \(\Gamma ^k_{ij}(p) = 0\). Geodesics through \(p\) are the images of lines through \(0\).

Proof â–¼

Since \(\exp _p(tv) = \gamma _v(t)\), the differential of \(\exp _p\) at \(0\) sends \(v\) to \(\dot\gamma _v(0) = v\), and the inverse function theorem applies. In normal coordinates for an orthonormal basis of \(T_pM\), \(g_{ij}(p) = \delta _{ij}\), and the lines \(t \mapsto tv\) are geodesics, so the geodesic equation at \(t = 0\) gives \(\Gamma ^k_{ij}(p)v^iv^j = 0\) for all \(v\); as \(\Gamma ^k_{ij}\) is symmetric in \(i\), \(j\), it vanishes at \(p\).

027Q Theorem 40.6.3 Gauss lemma

Let \(v \in \mathcal{E}_p\) and \(w \in T_pM\), and identify \(T_v(T_pM)\) with \(T_pM\). Then \(\langle d_v\exp _p(v), d_v\exp _p(w)\rangle = \langle v, w\rangle \).

Proof â–¼

Since \(\mathcal{E}_p\) is open and star-shaped, \(f(s, t) = \exp _p(t(v + sw))\) is defined and smooth for \(|s|\) small and \(t \in [0, 1]\). Let \(T = \partial _tf\) and \(S = \partial _sf\). For fixed \(s\), \(t \mapsto f(s, t)\) is the geodesic with initial velocity \(v + sw\), so \(D_tT = 0\) and \(|T|^2 = |v + sw|^2\) for all \(t\). Using Proposition 40.4.1 and Lemma 40.4.3,

\[ \partial _t\langle S, T\rangle = \langle D_tS, T\rangle + \langle S, D_tT\rangle = \langle D_sT, T\rangle = \tfrac 12\partial _s|T|^2 = \tfrac 12\partial _s|v + sw|^2 = \langle v + sw, w\rangle . \]

At \(s = 0\) this is \(\langle v, w\rangle \). Also \(f(s, 0) = p\), so \(S(s, 0) = 0\). Integrating, \(\langle S, T\rangle (0, 1) = \langle v, w\rangle \). Finally \(T(0, 1) = \frac{d}{dt}\exp _p(tv)|_{t=1} = d_v\exp _p(v)\) and \(S(0, 1) = \frac{d}{ds}\exp _p(v + sw)|_{s=0} = d_v\exp _p(w)\).

027R Theorem 40.6.4 Geodesics are locally minimising

Let \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\) be such that \(\exp _p\) is a diffeomorphism from \(B(0, \varepsilon ) \subseteq T_pM\) onto an open set \(U\) (a normal ball).

  1. For \(|v| {\lt} \varepsilon \), every curve from \(p\) to \(\exp _p(v)\) has length \(\geq |v|\), with equality if and only if it is a monotone reparametrisation of the radial geodesic \(t \mapsto \exp _p(tv)\), \(t \in [0, 1]\).

  2. \(d(p, \exp _p(v)) = |v|\) for \(|v| {\lt} \varepsilon \), and \(\exp _p(B(0, r))\) is the metric ball \(\{ x : d(p, x) {\lt} r\} \) for \(0 {\lt} r \leq \varepsilon \).

Proof â–¼

For \(x \in U\) let \(\rho (x) = |\exp _p^{-1}(x)|\), a continuous function, smooth on \(U \setminus \{ p\} \). Let \(c \colon [a, b] \to U \setminus \{ p\} \) be a piecewise smooth curve and write \(c(t) = \exp _p(\rho (t)u(t))\) with \(\rho (t) = \rho (c(t)) {\gt} 0\) and \(|u(t)| = 1\). Then \(\dot c = d\exp _p(\dot\rho u + \rho \dot u)\) at the point \(\rho u\), with \(\langle u, \dot u\rangle = 0\). By the Gauss lemma, \(d\exp _p(u)\) has length \(1\) and is orthogonal to \(d\exp _p(\rho \dot u)\) (both at \(\rho u\)), so

\begin{equation} |\dot c|^2 = \dot\rho ^2 + |d\exp _p(\rho \dot u)|^2 \geq \dot\rho ^2, \qquad \text{hence}\qquad L(c) \geq \int _a^b|\dot\rho |\, dt \geq \rho (b) - \rho (a). \end{equation}
40.2

Now let \(c \colon [a, b] \to M\) go from \(p\) to \(q = \exp _p(v)\), \(0 {\lt} |v| = r {\lt} \varepsilon \). Suppose first that \(c\) stays in \(U\). Let \(a'\) be the last time with \(c(a') = p\); applying 40.2 on \([a'', b]\) for \(a'' \downarrow a'\) gives \(L(c) \geq r\). If \(L(c) = r\), then \(c\) is constant on \([a, a']\), and on \((a', b]\) equality holds in 40.2: \(\dot\rho \geq 0\) and \(d\exp _p(\rho \dot u) = 0\), so \(\dot u = 0\) (\(d\exp _p\) being injective on \(B(0, \varepsilon )\)), \(u\) is constant and \(c\) is a monotone reparametrisation of the radial geodesic. Conversely such reparametrisations have length \(r\). If \(c\) leaves \(U\), choose \(r {\lt} r' {\lt} \varepsilon \). The set \(K = \exp _p(\bar B(0, r'))\) is compact, hence closed, and contains \(p\); so \(c\) leaves \(K\), and by continuity \(\rho (c(t))\) takes the value \(r'\) at some first time \(t_2\) with \(c([a, t_2]) \subseteq K\). The first case applied to \(c|_{[a, t_2]}\) gives \(L(c) \geq r' {\gt} r\). This proves (1).

For (2), the radial geodesic has length \(|v|\), so \(d(p, \exp _p(v)) = |v|\) by (1). The ball \(\exp _p(B(0, r))\) lies in the metric ball of radius \(r\). Conversely, if \(d(p, x) {\lt} r\), choose a curve \(c\) from \(p\) to \(x\) with \(L(c) {\lt} r' {\lt} r\); if \(x \notin \exp _p(B(0, r'))\), the argument above shows \(L(c) \geq r'\), a contradiction.

027S Remark 40.6.5

Every point also has a geodesically convex neighbourhood, in which any two points are joined by a unique minimising geodesic, contained in the neighbourhood [dC92, Chapter 3].

027T Lemma 40.6.6 Uniformly normal neighbourhoods

Every \(q \in M\) has a neighbourhood \(W\) and a \(\delta {\gt} 0\) such that for every \(x \in W\), \(\exp _x\) is defined on \(B(0, \delta ) \subseteq T_xM\), is a diffeomorphism from it onto an open set, and \(\exp _x(B(0, \delta )) \supseteq W\).

Proof â–¼

Consider \(E \colon \mathcal{E} \to M \times M\), \(E(v) = (\pi (v), \exp (v))\). In coordinates \((x, v)\) near \(0_q \in TM\), its differential at \(0_q\) is \(\begin{pmatrix} I & 0 \\ I & I \end{pmatrix}\), since \(\exp (0_x) = x\) and \(d_0\exp _x = \mathrm{id}\); so by the inverse function theorem \(E\) is a diffeomorphism from a neighbourhood \(O\) of \(0_q\) onto a neighbourhood of \((q, q)\). Shrinking, we may take \(O = \{ v \in T_xM : x \in W', |v| {\lt} \delta \} \) for a neighbourhood \(W'\) of \(q\) and some \(\delta {\gt} 0\) (the norm is continuous on \(TM\)), and choose a neighbourhood \(W \subseteq W'\) of \(q\) with \(W \times W \subseteq E(O)\). For \(x \in W\), \(\exp _x\) is injective on \(B(0, \delta )\) with invertible differential, hence a diffeomorphism onto an open set; and for \(y \in W\), \((x, y) = E(v)\) with \(v \in O\), that is \(y = \exp _x(v)\) with \(|v| {\lt} \delta \).

027U Lemma 40.6.7

Let \(c \colon [a, b] \to M\) be a piecewise smooth curve of constant speed with \(L(c) = d(c(a), c(b))\). Then \(c\) is a geodesic; in particular it is smooth.

Proof â–¼

Every subarc of \(c\) is also minimising, since otherwise replacing it by a shorter curve would shorten \(c\). Let \(t_0 \in [a, b]\), and take \(W\) and \(\delta \) for \(q = c(t_0)\) as in Lemma 40.6.6. For \(t_1 {\lt} t_0 {\lt} t_2\) close to \(t_0\) (or \(t_1 = t_0\) at the endpoints), \(c([t_1, t_2]) \subseteq W\) and \(L(c|_{[t_1, t_2]}) {\lt} \delta \). Then \(c|_{[t_1, t_2]}\) is a minimising curve from \(x = c(t_1)\) to a point of the normal ball \(\exp _x(B(0, \delta ))\), so by Theorem 40.6.4(1) it is a monotone reparametrisation of a radial geodesic from \(x\), and having constant speed, it is that geodesic, affinely reparametrised. So \(c\) is a geodesic near every \(t_0\).

01CF Theorem 40.6.8 Hopf–Rinow

For a connected Riemannian manifold \((M, g)\) the following are equivalent: \((M, d)\) is a complete metric space; the geodesics are defined for all time; closed bounded subsets are compact. In that case any two points are joined by a minimising geodesic. In particular compact Riemannian manifolds are geodesically complete.

Proof â–¼

We show more: the three conditions are also equivalent to (d) \(\exp _p\) is defined on all of \(T_pM\) for some \(p\); and (d) implies that every \(q\) is joined to \(p\) by a minimising geodesic.

Step 1: (d) gives minimising geodesics from \(p\). Let \(q \neq p\), \(r = d(p, q)\), and choose \(0 {\lt} \delta {\lt} r\) with \(\exp _p\) a diffeomorphism on \(B(0, \delta ')\) for some \(\delta ' {\gt} \delta \). The geodesic sphere \(S = \exp _p(\{ |w| = \delta \} )\) is compact; let \(x_0 = \exp _p(\delta u) \in S\), \(|u| = 1\), minimise \(d(\cdot , q)\) on \(S\). Every curve from \(p\) to \(q\) meets \(S\) (by the argument in the proof of Theorem 40.6.4), and points of \(S\) have distance \(\delta \) from \(p\), so \(r = \delta + d(x_0, q)\). Let \(\gamma (t) = \exp _p(tu)\), defined for all \(t\) by (d), and let \(T = \{ t \in [\delta , r] : d(\gamma (t), q) = r - t\} \), a closed set containing \(\delta \). Let \(t_0 = \max T\) and suppose \(t_0 {\lt} r\). Apply the same construction at \(\gamma (t_0)\): for small \(\eta {\gt} 0\) there is \(x_1\) on the geodesic sphere of radius \(\eta \) about \(\gamma (t_0)\) with \(d(\gamma (t_0), q) = \eta + d(x_1, q)\), so \(d(x_1, q) = r - t_0 - \eta \) and \(d(p, x_1) \geq d(p, q) - d(x_1, q) = t_0 + \eta \). The curve going along \(\gamma \) from \(p\) to \(\gamma (t_0)\) and then along the radial geodesic to \(x_1\) has length \(t_0 + \eta \), so it is minimising, and by Lemma 40.6.7 (after parametrising by arc length) it is a geodesic, hence equal to \(\gamma \) on \([0, t_0 + \eta ]\). So \(x_1 = \gamma (t_0 + \eta )\) and \(t_0 + \eta \in T\), a contradiction. Hence \(t_0 = r\), \(\gamma (r) = q\) and \(L(\gamma |_{[0, r]}) = r\).

Step 2: the equivalences. If closed bounded sets are compact, a Cauchy sequence, being bounded, has a convergent subsequence, hence converges; so \(M\) is complete. If \(M\) is complete, let \(\gamma \colon [0, b) \to M\) be a maximal unit-speed geodesic with \(b {\lt} \infty \). For \(t_k \uparrow b\), \(d(\gamma (t_k), \gamma (t_l)) \leq |t_k - t_l|\), so \((\gamma (t_k))\) is Cauchy and converges to some \(q\). Take \(W\), \(\delta \) for \(q\) as in Lemma 40.6.6; then geodesics with initial point in \(W\) and speed \(1\) are defined on \((-\delta , \delta )\). Choose \(t_k {\gt} b - \delta \) with \(\gamma (t_k) \in W\); the geodesic with initial velocity \(\dot\gamma (t_k)\) is defined up to time \(t_k + \delta {\gt} b\) and extends \(\gamma \) by uniqueness, a contradiction. So geodesics are defined for all time, which implies (d). Finally, assume (d) and let \(K\) be closed and bounded, say \(K \subseteq \{ x : d(p, x) \leq R\} \). By Step 1, this ball is contained in \(\exp _p(\bar B(0, R))\), which is compact; so \(K\) is compact. The last statements follow from Step 1 applied at every point, and from the compactness of closed bounded subsets of a compact space.

02T1 Remark 40.6.9

The proof of Step 2 shows that in any metric space the Heine–Borel property (closed bounded sets are compact) implies completeness. The converse is false for general metric spaces: \(\mathbb {R}\) with the complete metric \(\min (|x - y|, 1)\) is bounded and closed in itself but not compact, and the closed unit ball of an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space is closed, bounded and complete but not compact (Chapter 30). What the Hopf–Rinow theorem adds is that for the distance of a Riemannian metric, which is defined by lengths of curves on a locally compact space, completeness does imply the Heine–Borel property; and geodesic completeness, the second condition, has no meaning in a general metric space.

027V Counterexample 40.6.10

Completeness is needed. In \(M = \mathbb {R}^2 \setminus \{ 0\} \) with the Euclidean metric, the geodesic \(t \mapsto (1 - t, 0)\) is not defined at \(t = 1\), the Cauchy sequence \((1/k, 0)\) has no limit, and the set \(\{ x \in M : |x| \leq 1\} \) is closed in \(M\) and bounded but not compact. The points \((1, 0)\) and \((-1, 0)\) have distance \(2\), the infimum of the lengths of curves avoiding the origin, but no curve of length \(2\) joins them in \(M\), since in \(\mathbb {R}^2\) the only such curve is the segment through the origin.

02UB Example 40.6.11

The exponential of a Lie group and the Riemannian exponential. Let \(G\) be a Lie group with a bi-invariant metric, for instance a compact group (Example 40.2.5). Then the geodesics through \(e\) are the one-parameter subgroups, and the Riemannian exponential \(\exp _e \colon T_eG \to G\) is the exponential map of the Lie group (Theorem 34.3.3). Indeed, for left-invariant fields \(X\), \(Y\), \(Z\) the functions \(g(Y, Z)\) etc. are constant, and the metric on \(\mathfrak {g}\) is \(\operatorname {Ad}\)-invariant, since \(\operatorname {Ad}(h) = d(L_h \circ R_{h^{-1}})\) is the differential of an isometry; differentiating, as in the proof of Proposition 34.5.4, gives \(g([A, B], C) = -g(B, [A, C])\); the Koszul formula (Theorem 40.2.8) then reduces to \(2g(\nabla _XY, Z) = g([X, Y], Z) - g([X, Z], Y) - g([Y, Z], X) = g([X, Y], Z)\), that is,

\[ \nabla _XY = \tfrac 12[X, Y]. \]

In particular \(\nabla _XX = 0\), so the integral curves of \(X\), which are \(t \mapsto g\exp (tX)\), are geodesics. For a left-invariant metric that is not bi-invariant this fails. On the group of affine maps \(x \mapsto ax + b\) (\(a {\gt} 0\)), identified with the half plane \(\{ (a, b) : a {\gt} 0\} \) with the product \((a, b)(a^{\prime }, b^{\prime }) = (aa^{\prime }, ab^{\prime } + b)\), left translations are the maps \((a^{\prime }, b^{\prime }) \mapsto (aa^{\prime }, ab^{\prime } + b)\), which preserve the hyperbolic metric \((da^2 + db^2)/a^2\); so this metric is left-invariant. The one-parameter subgroup \(c(t) = (e^t, e^t - 1)\) is not a geodesic: the Christoffel symbols of this metric are \(\Gamma ^a_{aa} = -\frac1a\), \(\Gamma ^a_{bb} = \frac1a\), \(\Gamma ^b_{ab} = \Gamma ^b_{ba} = -\frac1a\) and \(0\) otherwise, and the first geodesic equation \(\ddot a - \dot a^2/a + \dot b^2/a = 0\) gives \(e^t \neq 0\) along \(c\).

02VU Example 40.6.12

Geodesics and parallel transport in the model spaces.

  1. The sphere. Along a great circle \(\gamma (t) = \cos t\, e_1 + \sin t\, e_2\) in \(S^2\), with the projection connection, \(D_t\dot\gamma = P(\ddot\gamma ) = P(-\gamma ) = 0\), so great circles are geodesics; and the constant field \(e_3\), orthogonal to the plane of the circle, is tangent to \(S^2\) along \(\gamma \) with \(D_te_3 = P(0) = 0\). So parallel transport along the great circle by an angle \(\theta \) is the rotation of \(\mathbb {R}^3\) by \(\theta \) about \(e_3\), restricted to the tangent planes: it keeps the angle with the circle constant. Since through every point and in every direction there is a great circle, these are all the geodesics (by uniqueness, Definition 40.2.9).

  2. The hyperbolic plane. For \((dx^2 + dy^2)/y^2\) on \(\{ y {\gt} 0\} \), the formula of Theorem 40.2.8 gives \(\Gamma ^x_{xy} = \Gamma ^x_{yx} = \Gamma ^y_{yy} = -\frac1y\), \(\Gamma ^y_{xx} = \frac1y\) and the others \(0\), so the geodesic equations are

    \[ \ddot x - \tfrac 2y\dot x\dot y = 0, \qquad \ddot y + \tfrac 1y(\dot x^2 - \dot y^2) = 0 . \]

    The vertical lines \(t \mapsto (c, e^t)\) and the semicircles \(t \mapsto (c + r\tanh t, r\operatorname {sech}t)\) centred on the real axis solve them, with unit speed: for the semicircle, \(\dot x = r\operatorname {sech}^2t\), \(\dot y = -r\operatorname {sech}t\tanh t\), \(\dot x^2 + \dot y^2 = r^2\operatorname {sech}^2t = y^2\), and substituting, both equations hold (for instance \(\ddot x = -2r\operatorname {sech}^2t\tanh t = \frac2y\dot x\dot y\)). Through every point and in every direction passes exactly one vertical line or semicircle centred on the axis (the centre is where the normal to the direction meets the axis), so by uniqueness these are all the geodesics. In particular \(d(i, iy) = |\log y|\), the length of the vertical segment, which is minimising since the projection \((x, y) \mapsto (0, y)\) does not increase lengths.

027W Remark 40.6.13

Minimising geodesics need not be unique: on the round sphere every half great circle joins two antipodal points, and has length \(\pi \). And geodesics stop minimising: a great circle arc longer than \(\pi \) is a geodesic but not the shortest curve between its endpoints. Both phenomena are explained by conjugate points (Section 40.8).
