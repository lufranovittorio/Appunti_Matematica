38.6 Stiefel–Whitney classes and Wu’s formula02GY

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

With coefficients in \(\mathbb {F}_2\) no orientations are needed, and the Steenrod squares (Theorem 23.4.1) produce the Stiefel–Whitney classes directly from the Thom class. For the tangent bundle of a closed manifold this has a striking consequence, Wu’s formula: the Stiefel–Whitney classes of \(TM\) are determined by the cohomology ring \(H^*(M; \mathbb {F}_2)\) with its Steenrod squares, so they are homotopy invariants, although \(TM\) is not. In this section \(H^*\) means \(H^*(-; \mathbb {F}_2)\), and \(\mathrm{Sq} = \sum _i\mathrm{Sq}^i\).

02GZ Theorem 38.6.1 Thom

Let \(\pi \colon E \to M\) be a real vector bundle of rank \(n\) over a manifold, and \(E_0\) the complement of the zero section.

  1. (Thom isomorphism mod \(2\)) There is a unique class \(u \in H^n(E, E_0)\) restricting to the nonzero class on every fibre, and \(\Phi (x) = \pi ^*(x) \smile u\) is an isomorphism \(H^k(M) \to H^{k+n}(E, E_0)\) for all \(k\).

  2. The classes \(w_i(E) = \Phi ^{-1}(\mathrm{Sq}^i u) \in H^i(M)\) are the Stiefel–Whitney classes of Definition 38.5.2: \(w_0 = 1\), \(w_i = 0\) for \(i {\gt} n\), they are natural, the total class \(w = \sum _iw_i\) satisfies \(w(E \oplus E') = w(E)w(E')\), and \(w_1\) of the tautological line bundle on \(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^1\) is nonzero. Equivalently, \(\mathrm{Sq}(u) = \pi ^*w(E) \smile u\).

This is [MS74, Chapters 8 and 10]; it is Thom’s definition of the Stiefel–Whitney classes. The properties in (2) follow from the axioms of Theorem 23.4.1: \(w_0 = 1\) from \(\mathrm{Sq}^0 = \mathrm{id}\), \(w_i = 0\) for \(i {\gt} n\) from \(\mathrm{Sq}^iu = 0\) for \(i {\gt} \deg u\), and the Whitney formula from the Cartan formula, as the Thom class of \(E \oplus E'\) is the cross product of the Thom classes.

02H0 Definition 38.6.2

Let \(M\) be a closed \(n\)-manifold and \([M] \in H_n(M)\) its fundamental class (every manifold is \(\mathbb {F}_2\)-oriented, Proposition 22.3.2(3)). For each \(i\), the map \(H^{n-i}(M) \to \mathbb {F}_2\), \(x \mapsto \langle \mathrm{Sq}^i(x), [M]\rangle \), is linear, so by Poincaré duality (Corollary 22.6.3) there is a unique class \(v_i \in H^i(M)\) with

\[ \langle v_i \smile x, [M]\rangle = \langle \mathrm{Sq}^i(x), [M]\rangle \quad \text{for all } x \in H^{n-i}(M). \]

The \(v_i\) are the Wu classes of \(M\), and \(v = 1 + v_1 + v_2 + \cdots \) is the total Wu class.

Since \(\mathrm{Sq}^0 = \mathrm{id}\), \(v_0 = 1\). If \(2i {\gt} n\), then \(\mathrm{Sq}^i(x) = 0\) for \(x\) of degree \(n - i {\lt} i\), so \(v_i = 0\): only \(v_0, \ldots , v_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor }\) can be nonzero.

02H1 Theorem 38.6.3 Wu’s formula

Let \(M\) be a closed smooth \(n\)-manifold. Then \(w(TM) = \mathrm{Sq}(v)\), that is, \(w_k(TM) = \sum _i\mathrm{Sq}^{k-i}(v_i)\) for every \(k\).

Sketch of proof â–¼

Choose a basis \((b_j)\) of \(H^*(M)\) and the dual basis \((b_j^\# )\) for the cup pairing, \(\langle b_j \smile b_l^\# , [M]\rangle = \delta _{jl}\) (Corollary 22.6.3); then every \(y \in H^*(M)\) is \(y = \sum _j\langle y \smile b_j, [M]\rangle \, b_j^\# \). We quote two geometric facts [MS74, Chapter 11], which are the omitted verifications. First, the class \(U = \sum _jb_j \times b_j^\# \in H^n(M \times M)\) (Künneth, Theorem 21.4.2) is the Poincaré dual of the diagonal. Second, a tubular neighbourhood of the diagonal is isomorphic to \(TM\), and under this isomorphism and excision \(U\) corresponds to the Thom class of \(TM\); with Theorem 38.6.1(2) this gives \(\mathrm{Sq}(U) = (1 \times w(TM)) \smile U\).

The rest is algebra. Let \(\Theta \colon H^*(M \times M) \to H^*(M)\) be the linear map with \(\Theta (a \times b) = \langle a, [M]\rangle \, b\) (the slant product with \([M]\) in the first factor), so that \(\Theta ((1 \times w) \smile Z) = w \smile \Theta (Z)\). Since \(\langle b_j, [M]\rangle = \langle 1 \smile b_j, [M]\rangle \), \(\Theta (U) = \sum _j\langle 1 \smile b_j, [M]\rangle b_j^\# = 1\), so \(\Theta (\mathrm{Sq}(U)) = w(TM)\). On the other hand, by the Cartan formula \(\mathrm{Sq}(U) = \sum _j\mathrm{Sq}(b_j) \times \mathrm{Sq}(b_j^\# )\), and \(\langle \mathrm{Sq}(b_j), [M]\rangle = \langle \mathrm{Sq}^{n - \deg b_j}(b_j), [M]\rangle = \langle v \smile b_j, [M]\rangle \) by the definition of the Wu classes; so \(\Theta (\mathrm{Sq}(U)) = \sum _j\langle v \smile b_j, [M]\rangle \, \mathrm{Sq}(b_j^\# ) = \mathrm{Sq}\bigl(\sum _j\langle v \smile b_j, [M]\rangle b_j^\# \bigr) = \mathrm{Sq}(v)\).

02H2 Corollary 38.6.4

Let \(M\) and \(N\) be closed connected smooth \(n\)-manifolds and \(h \colon M \to N\) a homotopy equivalence. Then \(h^*w(TN) = w(TM)\), and \(M\) and \(N\) have the same Stiefel–Whitney numbers \(\langle w_{i_1}(T-) \cdots w_{i_r}(T-), [-]\rangle \). Moreover \(w_1(TM) = v_1\), so \(M\) is orientable if and only if \(\mathrm{Sq}^1 \colon H^{n-1}(M) \to H^n(M)\) is zero.

Proof â–¼

The map \(h^*\) is an isomorphism of rings commuting with the Steenrod squares (naturality), and \(h_*[M] = [N]\), because \(h_*\) is an isomorphism \(H_n(M) \cong \mathbb {F}_2 \to H_n(N) \cong \mathbb {F}_2\) (Theorem 22.4.3). For \(x \in H^{n-i}(N)\), \(\langle h^*(v_i(N)) \smile h^*x, [M]\rangle = \langle v_i(N) \smile x, [N]\rangle = \langle \mathrm{Sq}^i(x), [N]\rangle = \langle \mathrm{Sq}^i(h^*x), [M]\rangle \), and \(h^*\) is surjective; so \(h^*v(N) = v(M)\), and \(h^*w(TN) = h^*\mathrm{Sq}(v(N)) = \mathrm{Sq}(v(M)) = w(TM)\) by Theorem 38.6.3. The Stiefel–Whitney numbers agree because \(\langle h^*y, [M]\rangle = \langle y, h_*[M]\rangle \). Next, \(w_1 = \mathrm{Sq}^1(v_0) + \mathrm{Sq}^0(v_1) = v_1\), as \(\mathrm{Sq}^1(1) = 0\). By definition \(v_1 = 0\) if and only if \(\langle \mathrm{Sq}^1(x), [M]\rangle = 0\) for all \(x \in H^{n-1}(M)\), that is, since \(\langle -, [M]\rangle \) is an isomorphism \(H^n(M) \to \mathbb {F}_2\), if and only if \(\mathrm{Sq}^1 = 0\) on \(H^{n-1}(M)\); and \(w_1(TM) = 0\) if and only if \(TM\), equivalently \(M\), is orientable (Definition 38.5.2).

02H3 Lemma 38.6.5

Let \(V\) be a finite-dimensional vector space over \(\mathbb {F}_2\) with a nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form \(B\). There is a unique \(c \in V\) with \(B(x, x) = B(c, x)\) for all \(x\), and \(B(c, c) \equiv \dim V \pmod2\).

Proof â–¼

The map \(x \mapsto B(x, x)\) is linear, because \(B(x + y, x + y) = B(x, x) + B(y, y) + 2B(x, y)\), so \(c\) exists and is unique by nondegeneracy. We argue by induction on \(\dim V\), the case \(V = 0\) being trivial. If \(B(x, x) = 0\) for all \(x\), then \(c = 0\) and \(B\) is alternating. Choose \(x \neq 0\) and \(y\) with \(B(x, y) = 1\); the plane \(P\) spanned by \(x\) and \(y\) has Gram matrix \(\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\), so \(B\) is nondegenerate on \(P\), \(V = P \oplus P^{\perp _B}\) (Lemma 10.8.2), \(B\) is nondegenerate and alternating on \(P^{\perp _B}\), and by induction \(\dim V\) is even, as required. Otherwise choose \(x\) with \(B(x, x) = 1\); then \(V = \mathbb {F}_2x \oplus W\) with \(W = x^{\perp _B}\), \(B\) is nondegenerate on \(W\), and if \(c'\) is the element of \(W\) given by induction, \(c = x + c'\) works: for \(y = \lambda x + w\), \(B(y, y) = \lambda + B(w, w) = \lambda + B(c', w) = B(x + c', y)\). Then \(B(c, c) = 1 + B(c', c') \equiv 1 + \dim W = \dim V\).

02H4 Corollary 38.6.6

For a closed smooth \(n\)-manifold \(M\), \(\langle w_n(TM), [M]\rangle \equiv \chi (M) \pmod2\).

Proof â–¼

We may assume \(M\) connected. By Theorem 38.6.3, \(w_n = \sum _i\mathrm{Sq}^{n-i}(v_i)\). For \(i {\lt} n/2\) the term vanishes, because \(n - i {\gt} i = \deg v_i\), and for \(i {\gt} n/2\) we have \(v_i = 0\). If \(n\) is odd nothing remains, so \(w_n = 0\), and \(\chi (M) = 0\) by Corollary 22.6.4 (closed manifolds have finitely generated homology [Hat02, Appendix]). If \(n = 2k\), then \(w_n = \mathrm{Sq}^k(v_k) = v_k^2\). The cup pairing \(B(x, y) = \langle x \smile y, [M]\rangle \) on \(V = H^k(M)\) is symmetric (mod \(2\) there are no signs) and nondegenerate, and \(B(x, x) = \langle \mathrm{Sq}^k(x), [M]\rangle = B(v_k, x)\); so \(v_k\) is the element \(c\) of Lemma 38.6.5, and \(\langle w_n, [M]\rangle = B(v_k, v_k) \equiv \dim H^k(M)\). Finally \(\chi (M) = \sum _i(-1)^i\dim H^i(M)\) (Corollary 22.6.4 and universal coefficients over a field), and \(\dim H^i(M) = \dim H^{n-i}(M)\) by Poincaré duality; so the terms with \(i \neq k\) cancel in pairs modulo \(2\), and \(\chi (M) \equiv \dim H^k(M)\).

02H5 Example 38.6.7

Let \(M = \mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^n\), with \(H^*(M) = \mathbb {F}_2[\alpha ]/(\alpha ^{n+1})\), \([M]\) pairing to \(1\) with \(\alpha ^n\), and \(\mathrm{Sq}^i(\alpha ^m) = \binom {m}{i}\alpha ^{m+i}\) (Proposition 23.4.2, restricted from \(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^\infty \)). Then \(v_i = \binom {n-i}{i}\alpha ^i\), since \(\mathrm{Sq}^i(\alpha ^{n-i}) = \binom {n-i}{i}\alpha ^n\), and \(\mathrm{Sq}(\alpha ^i) = \alpha ^i(1 + \alpha )^i\).

  1. \(n = 2\): \(v = 1 + \alpha \) and \(w = 1 + \alpha (1 + \alpha ) = 1 + \alpha + \alpha ^2\). So \(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^2\) is not orientable (\(w_1 \neq 0\)), and \(w_2 \neq 0\) matches \(\chi (\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^2) = 1\).

  2. \(n = 3\): \(v_1 = \binom {2}{1}\alpha = 0\), so \(v = 1\) and \(w = 1\): all Stiefel–Whitney classes of \(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^3\) vanish. This is consistent with \(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^3 \cong \mathrm{SO}(3)\) being a Lie group, hence parallelisable.

  3. \(n = 4\): \(v = 1 + \alpha + \alpha ^2\) and \(w = 1 + \alpha (1 + \alpha ) + \alpha ^2(1 + \alpha )^2 = 1 + \alpha + \alpha ^4\). So \(w_4[\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^4] = 1\), and \(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^4\) has a nonzero Stiefel–Whitney number; by a theorem of Pontryagin, the Stiefel–Whitney numbers of a boundary vanish, so \(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^4\) is not the boundary of any compact \(5\)-manifold [MS74, Chapter 4]. (Thom proved the converse: a closed manifold whose Stiefel–Whitney numbers all vanish is a boundary [Tho54].)

In all three cases \(w = (1 + \alpha )^{n+1}\), which is the general formula for \(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^n\) [MS74, Chapter 4].
