27.11 Curves, arc length and line integrals03D5

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

A curve in \(\mathbb {R}^n\) can be studied through a parametrisation, a map \(t \mapsto \gamma (t)\), but many of the quantities we care about belong to the path traced out and not to the particular way of running along it: its length, the mass of a wire bent along it, the work done by a force field along it. This section defines these quantities, computes them by integrals, and checks that they do not depend on the parametrisation. Two kinds of integrals appear. The integral of a function with respect to arc length, \(\int _\gamma f\, ds\), does not see the direction of travel; the integral of a vector field, \(\int _\gamma F \cdot d\gamma \), does, and changes sign when the curve is run backwards. Traditionally they are called line integrals of the first and of the second kind. The first is the special case \(f = 1\) of a mass, the second the special case of a \(1\)-form; both reappear on manifolds (Chapters 36 and 40). Throughout, \(|x|\) is the Euclidean norm on \(\mathbb {R}^n\) and \(\langle x, y\rangle = x \cdot y\) the standard inner product.

27.11.1 Curves and their length03D6

03D7 Definition 27.11.1

A curve in \(\mathbb {R}^n\) is a continuous map \(\gamma \colon [a, b] \to \mathbb {R}^n\), with \(a {\lt} b\); \(\gamma (a)\) and \(\gamma (b)\) are its initial and final points, and it is closed if they agree. It is of class \(C^1\) if it is differentiable with continuous derivative \(\gamma ^{\prime } = (\gamma _1^{\prime }, \ldots , \gamma _n^{\prime })\), one-sided at the ends, and piecewise \(C^1\) if there is a subdivision \(a = c_0 {\lt} c_1 {\lt} \cdots {\lt} c_m = b\) such that each restriction \(\gamma |_{[c_{k-1}, c_k]}\) is \(C^1\). A \(C^1\) curve is regular if \(\gamma ^{\prime }(t) \neq 0\) for all \(t\); then \(\gamma ^{\prime }(t)\) is its velocity or tangent vector at \(t\) and \(|\gamma ^{\prime }(t)|\) its speed.

A reparametrisation of \(\gamma \) is \(\gamma \circ \varphi \), where \(\varphi \colon [c, d] \to [a, b]\) is a continuous bijection. Such a \(\varphi \) is strictly monotone; it preserves the orientation if it is increasing, so that \(\gamma \circ \varphi \) runs from \(\gamma (a)\) to \(\gamma (b)\), and reverses it if it is decreasing. The reverse of \(\gamma \) is \(\gamma ^-(t) = \gamma (a + b - t)\). For \(C^1\) curves we use \(C^1\) reparametrisations with \(\varphi ^{\prime }\) nowhere zero; by the chain rule \((\gamma \circ \varphi )^{\prime }(s) = \varphi ^{\prime }(s)\, \gamma ^{\prime }(\varphi (s))\), so the velocity is rescaled but keeps its direction (up to sign). For instance \(\gamma (t) = (\cos t, \sin t)\), \(t \in [0, 2\pi ]\), and \(\tilde\gamma (s) = (\cos 2\pi s^2, \sin 2\pi s^2)\), \(s \in [0, 1]\), run once around the unit circle at different speeds, and \(\gamma ^-\) runs around it clockwise.

How long is a curve? The ancient answer, used by Archimedes for the circle, is to approximate it by inscribed polygons. A polygon through points of the curve is shorter than the curve, because the segment is the shortest path between two points, and refining the polygon can only make it longer, by the triangle inequality. So the length should be the supremum of the lengths of the inscribed polygons.

03D8 Definition 27.11.2

For a curve \(\gamma \colon [a, b] \to \mathbb {R}^n\) and a partition \(P \colon a = t_0 {\lt} t_1 {\lt} \cdots {\lt} t_k = b\), let \(\ell (\gamma , P) = \sum _{i=1}^k|\gamma (t_i) - \gamma (t_{i-1})|\) be the length of the inscribed polygon. The length of \(\gamma \) is

\[ L(\gamma ) = \sup _P\ell (\gamma , P) \in [0, \infty ], \]

and \(\gamma \) is rectifiable if \(L(\gamma ) {\lt} \infty \).

03D9 Lemma 27.11.3

Let \(\gamma \colon [a, b] \to \mathbb {R}^n\) be a curve.

  1. If \(P^{\prime }\) refines \(P\), then \(\ell (\gamma , P) \leq \ell (\gamma , P^{\prime })\), and \(|\gamma (b) - \gamma (a)| \leq L(\gamma )\).

  2. (Additivity) For \(a {\lt} c {\lt} b\), \(L(\gamma ) = L(\gamma |_{[a, c]}) + L(\gamma |_{[c, b]})\).

  3. (Invariance) \(L(\gamma \circ \varphi ) = L(\gamma )\) for every reparametrisation, of either orientation; in particular \(L(\gamma ^-) = L(\gamma )\).

Proof â–¼

(1) Adding a point \(s\) between \(t_{i-1}\) and \(t_i\) replaces \(|\gamma (t_i) - \gamma (t_{i-1})|\) by \(|\gamma (s) - \gamma (t_{i-1})| + |\gamma (t_i) - \gamma (s)|\), which is at least as large by the triangle inequality; refining adds finitely many points. The trivial partition gives \(|\gamma (b) - \gamma (a)| = \ell (\gamma , \{ a, b\} ) \leq L(\gamma )\).

(2) A partition of \([a, c]\) and one of \([c, b]\) together form a partition of \([a, b]\), so \(L(\gamma |_{[a, c]}) + L(\gamma |_{[c, b]}) \leq L(\gamma )\), taking suprema over the two partitions separately. Conversely, for a partition \(P\) of \([a, b]\) let \(P^{\prime } = P \cup \{ c\} \); then by (1) \(\ell (\gamma , P) \leq \ell (\gamma , P^{\prime })\), and \(\ell (\gamma , P^{\prime })\) is the sum of the polygon lengths of the two restrictions, which is at most \(L(\gamma |_{[a, c]}) + L(\gamma |_{[c, b]})\).

(3) A strictly monotone bijection \(\varphi \colon [c, d] \to [a, b]\) maps partitions of \([c, d]\) bijectively to partitions of \([a, b]\) (reversing their order if \(\varphi \) decreases), and the inscribed polygons of \(\gamma \circ \varphi \) and of \(\gamma \) for corresponding partitions have the same vertices, hence the same length.

For a \(C^1\) curve the supremum is an integral: the length is the integral of the speed, as the distance travelled by a car is the integral of the reading of its speedometer.

03DA Theorem 27.11.4 Length of a \(C^1\) curve

Every piecewise \(C^1\) curve is rectifiable, and

\[ L(\gamma ) = \int _a^b|\gamma ^{\prime }(t)|\, dt, \]

the integral being the sum of the integrals over the pieces where \(\gamma \) is \(C^1\).

Proof â–¼

By the additivity of Lemma 27.11.3(2) and of the integral, it suffices to treat a \(C^1\) curve. The function \(|\gamma ^{\prime }|\) is continuous, hence integrable. For any partition, the fundamental theorem of calculus (Theorem 27.8.3) and the norm inequality of Proposition 27.8.2(3) give

\[ |\gamma (t_i) - \gamma (t_{i-1})| = \Bigl|\int _{t_{i-1}}^{t_i}\gamma ^{\prime }(t)\, dt\Bigr| \leq \int _{t_{i-1}}^{t_i}|\gamma ^{\prime }(t)|\, dt, \]

and summing, \(\ell (\gamma , P) \leq \int _a^b|\gamma ^{\prime }|\). So \(L(\gamma ) \leq \int _a^b|\gamma ^{\prime }|\).

For the converse inequality let \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\). Since \(\gamma ^{\prime }\) is uniformly continuous on \([a, b]\) (Corollary 18.7.5), there is \(\delta {\gt} 0\) with \(|\gamma ^{\prime }(s) - \gamma ^{\prime }(t)| {\lt} \varepsilon \) whenever \(|s - t| {\lt} \delta \). Take a partition with \(t_i - t_{i-1} {\lt} \delta \) for all \(i\), and write \(\Delta _i = t_i - t_{i-1}\). On \([t_{i-1}, t_i]\),

\[ \gamma (t_i) - \gamma (t_{i-1}) = \int _{t_{i-1}}^{t_i}\gamma ^{\prime }(t)\, dt = \Delta _i\, \gamma ^{\prime }(t_i) + \int _{t_{i-1}}^{t_i}\bigl(\gamma ^{\prime }(t) - \gamma ^{\prime }(t_i)\bigr)\, dt, \]

and the last integral has norm at most \(\varepsilon \Delta _i\). Hence \(|\gamma (t_i) - \gamma (t_{i-1})| \geq \Delta _i|\gamma ^{\prime }(t_i)| - \varepsilon \Delta _i\). On the other hand \(|\gamma ^{\prime }(t)| \leq |\gamma ^{\prime }(t_i)| + \varepsilon \) on the interval, so \(\int _{t_{i-1}}^{t_i}|\gamma ^{\prime }| \leq \Delta _i|\gamma ^{\prime }(t_i)| + \varepsilon \Delta _i\). Combining and summing over \(i\),

\[ L(\gamma ) \geq \ell (\gamma , P) \geq \int _a^b|\gamma ^{\prime }(t)|\, dt - 2\varepsilon (b - a). \]

As \(\varepsilon \) is arbitrary, \(L(\gamma ) \geq \int _a^b|\gamma ^{\prime }|\).

In coordinates, \(L(\gamma ) = \int _a^b\sqrt{\gamma _1^{\prime }(t)^2 + \cdots + \gamma _n^{\prime }(t)^2}\, dt\). That this integral does not depend on the parametrisation, which is clear for the definition by polygons, can also be seen by the substitution rule: for \(\varphi \) increasing, \(\int _c^d|\gamma ^{\prime }(\varphi (s))|\, \varphi ^{\prime }(s)\, ds = \int _a^b|\gamma ^{\prime }(t)|\, dt\) (Corollary 27.8.4), and for \(\varphi \) decreasing the absolute value \(|\varphi ^{\prime }| = -\varphi ^{\prime }\) compensates the reversal of the limits.

03DB Example 27.11.5

  1. Circles. For \(\gamma (t) = (r\cos t, r\sin t)\), \(t \in [0, 2\pi ]\), \(\gamma ^{\prime }(t) = (-r\sin t, r\cos t)\) has norm \(r\), so \(L(\gamma ) = 2\pi r\). With the definition of \(\pi \) in Theorem 27.7.3, this is the theorem that the circumference of a circle is \(2\pi \) times its radius.

  2. Graphs. The graph of a \(C^1\) function \(f \colon [a, b] \to \mathbb {R}\), parametrised by \(\gamma (x) = (x, f(x))\), has length \(\int _a^b\sqrt{1 + f^{\prime }(x)^2}\, dx\). For a line \(f(x) = mx\) this is \((b - a)\sqrt{1 + m^2}\), the length of the segment, by Pythagoras.

  3. The helix. \(\gamma (t) = (r\cos t, r\sin t, ct)\) has constant speed \(\sqrt{r^2 + c^2}\), so one turn has length \(2\pi \sqrt{r^2 + c^2}\): unrolling the cylinder of radius \(r\) into a plane turns the helix into the diagonal of a rectangle with sides \(2\pi r\) and \(2\pi c\).

  4. The cycloid. A point on a rolling wheel of radius \(1\) traces \(\gamma (t) = (t - \sin t, 1 - \cos t)\). Here \(|\gamma ^{\prime }(t)|^2 = (1 - \cos t)^2 + \sin ^2t = 2 - 2\cos t = 4\sin ^2(t/2)\), so one arch has length \(\int _0^{2\pi }2\sin (t/2)\, dt = 8\), four times the diameter of the wheel (Wren, 1658). The curve is not regular: the speed vanishes at \(t = 0\) and \(t = 2\pi \), where the cycloid has cusps.

03DC Remark 27.11.6

The length of an ellipse \((a\cos t, b\sin t)\) with \(0 {\lt} b {\lt} a\) is \(\int _0^{2\pi }\sqrt{a^2\sin ^2t + b^2\cos ^2t}\, dt = 4a\int _0^{\pi /2}\sqrt{1 - e^2\cos ^2t}\, dt\), with eccentricity \(e = \sqrt{1 - b^2/a^2}\). Unlike the circle, this integral cannot be expressed by elementary functions. It is an elliptic integral, and the attempt to understand such integrals, by Euler, Legendre, Abel and Jacobi, led to elliptic functions and to elliptic curves: inverting the integral gives doubly periodic functions, and the natural domain of the integrand is a curve of genus one (Chapter 59).

Continuity alone does not give a finite length.

03DD Counterexample 27.11.7

Let \(f(x) = x\cos (\pi /x)\) for \(0 {\lt} x \leq 1\) and \(f(0) = 0\); it is continuous, since \(|f(x)| \leq x\), and \(C^1\) on \((0, 1]\). At \(x_m = 1/m\), \(f(x_m) = (-1)^m/m\). Take the partition of \([0, 1]\) by \(0\) and the points \(x_m\), \(m \leq N\); consecutive values have opposite signs, so the inscribed polygon of the graph \(\gamma (x) = (x, f(x))\) has length at least

\[ \sum _{m=1}^{N-1}|f(x_m) - f(x_{m+1})| = \sum _{m=1}^{N-1}\Bigl(\frac1m + \frac{1}{m+1}\Bigr) \geq \sum _{m=1}^{N-1}\frac1m, \]

which tends to \(\infty \) with \(N\) (Proposition 27.5.5(1)). So \(\gamma \) is a continuous curve of infinite length: it oscillates too much near \(0\). Worse examples exist: the Koch snowflake curve has infinite length between any two of its points, and the space-filling curves of Chapter 18 fill a square.

For a regular curve the length can be used as a parameter. This removes the arbitrariness of the parametrisation and is the starting point of the differential geometry of curves.

03DE Proposition 27.11.8 Arc length parametrisation

Let \(\gamma \colon [a, b] \to \mathbb {R}^n\) be a regular \(C^1\) curve of length \(L\), and \(\sigma (t) = \int _a^t|\gamma ^{\prime }(u)|\, du\) the length of \(\gamma |_{[a, t]}\). Then \(\sigma \colon [a, b] \to [0, L]\) is a \(C^1\) bijection with \(\sigma ^{\prime } = |\gamma ^{\prime }| {\gt} 0\), its inverse is \(C^1\), and \(\tilde\gamma = \gamma \circ \sigma ^{-1} \colon [0, L] \to \mathbb {R}^n\) has \(|\tilde\gamma ^{\prime }(s)| = 1\) for all \(s\). It is the unique orientation-preserving reparametrisation of \(\gamma \) with unit speed.

Proof â–¼

By the fundamental theorem of calculus \(\sigma \) is \(C^1\) with \(\sigma ^{\prime }(t) = |\gamma ^{\prime }(t)| {\gt} 0\), so it is strictly increasing, with \(\sigma (a) = 0\) and \(\sigma (b) = L\) by Theorem 27.11.4. By Proposition 27.6.8 its inverse is differentiable with \((\sigma ^{-1})^{\prime }(s) = 1/|\gamma ^{\prime }(\sigma ^{-1}(s))|\), which is continuous. By the chain rule \(|\tilde\gamma ^{\prime }(s)| = |\gamma ^{\prime }(\sigma ^{-1}(s))|\, (\sigma ^{-1})^{\prime }(s) = 1\). If \(\gamma \circ \varphi \) is another orientation-preserving \(C^1\) reparametrisation with unit speed, defined on \([c, d]\), then \(1 = |\gamma ^{\prime }(\varphi (u))|\varphi ^{\prime }(u) = (\sigma \circ \varphi )^{\prime }(u)\), so \(\sigma (\varphi (u)) = u - c\), and \(\gamma \circ \varphi \) is \(\tilde\gamma \) up to the translation \(u \mapsto u - c\) of the parameter.

The parameter \(s\) of \(\tilde\gamma \) is the arc length, and one writes \(ds = |\gamma ^{\prime }(t)|\, dt\) for the element of arc length: it is the length of the piece of curve swept out between \(t\) and \(t + dt\), to first order.

27.11.2 Line integrals of the first kind03DF

Imagine a wire bent along a curve, with a linear density of mass \(f\), in grams per centimetre. Its mass is obtained by cutting it into short pieces, multiplying the density at a point of each piece by the length of the piece, and adding up; in the limit this is an integral with respect to arc length.

03DG Definition 27.11.9

Let \(\gamma \colon [a, b] \to \mathbb {R}^n\) be a piecewise \(C^1\) curve and \(f\) a continuous real function on its image \(\gamma ([a, b])\). The line integral of \(f\) with respect to arc length, or of the first kind, is

\[ \int _\gamma f\, ds = \int _a^bf(\gamma (t))\, |\gamma ^{\prime }(t)|\, dt, \]

the sum of the integrals over the pieces where \(\gamma \) is \(C^1\).

03DH Proposition 27.11.10

Let \(\gamma \) and \(f\) be as in the definition.

  1. \(\int _\gamma 1\, ds = L(\gamma )\).

  2. The integral is linear in \(f\), monotone (\(f \leq g\) implies \(\int _\gamma f\, ds \leq \int _\gamma g\, ds\)), and \(\bigl|\int _\gamma f\, ds\bigr| \leq \sup |f|\cdot L(\gamma )\).

  3. It is unchanged under every \(C^1\) reparametrisation \(\gamma \circ \varphi \) with \(\varphi ^{\prime }\) nowhere zero, of either orientation; in particular \(\int _{\gamma ^-}f\, ds = \int _\gamma f\, ds\).

  4. It is additive: if \(\gamma \) is the concatenation of \(\gamma |_{[a, c]}\) and \(\gamma |_{[c, b]}\), the integral is the sum of the two integrals.

  5. (Riemann sums) \(\sum _if(\gamma (t_i))\, |\gamma (t_i) - \gamma (t_{i-1})| \to \int _\gamma f\, ds\) as the mesh \(\max _i(t_i - t_{i-1})\) of the partition tends to \(0\).

Proof â–¼

(1) is Theorem 27.11.4, and (2) and (4) follow from the corresponding properties of the integral (Proposition 27.8.2). (3) For each \(C^1\) piece and \(\varphi \) increasing, the substitution rule (Corollary 27.8.4) gives \(\int _c^df(\gamma (\varphi (u)))|\gamma ^{\prime }(\varphi (u))|\varphi ^{\prime }(u)\, du = \int _a^bf(\gamma (t))|\gamma ^{\prime }(t)|\, dt\), and \(|(\gamma \circ \varphi )^{\prime }| = |\gamma ^{\prime } \circ \varphi |\, \varphi ^{\prime }\). For \(\varphi \) decreasing, \(|(\gamma \circ \varphi )^{\prime }| = -|\gamma ^{\prime } \circ \varphi |\, \varphi ^{\prime }\), and the substitution reverses the limits, so the two signs cancel.

(5) It suffices to treat a \(C^1\) curve. The proof of Theorem 27.11.4 shows that for partitions of mesh \({\lt} \delta \), \(\bigl||\gamma (t_i) - \gamma (t_{i-1})| - \int _{t_{i-1}}^{t_i}|\gamma ^{\prime }|\bigr| \leq 2\varepsilon \Delta _i\). Also \(f \circ \gamma \) is uniformly continuous, so for small mesh it varies by less than \(\varepsilon \) on each interval. Hence the Riemann sum differs from \(\sum _i\int _{t_{i-1}}^{t_i}f(\gamma (t))|\gamma ^{\prime }(t)|\, dt = \int _\gamma f\, ds\) by at most \(\sup |f| \cdot 2\varepsilon (b - a) + \varepsilon L(\gamma )\).

By (3), the integral of the first kind depends only on the curve traced out, not on the direction or the speed of travel, as the mass of a wire should. The centre of mass of a wire of density \(f {\gt} 0\) is the point with coordinates \(\bar x_j = \int _\gamma x_jf\, ds / \int _\gamma f\, ds\).

03DI Example 27.11.11

  1. The upper half of the circle of radius \(r\), \(\gamma (t) = (r\cos t, r\sin t)\), \(t \in [0, \pi ]\), with constant density \(1\), has mass \(\pi r\) and \(\int _\gamma y\, ds = \int _0^\pi r\sin t \cdot r\, dt = 2r^2\). So its centre of mass is \((0, 2r/\pi )\), inside the half disc and not on the wire.

  2. For the helix of Example 27.11.5(3) over one turn, \(\int _\gamma z\, ds = \int _0^{2\pi }ct\sqrt{r^2 + c^2}\, dt = 2\pi ^2c\sqrt{r^2 + c^2}\), and the centre of mass is at height \(\pi c\), half way up, as symmetry suggests.

27.11.3 Line integrals of the second kind03DJ

Now let a force field \(F\) act on a particle moving along a curve. The work done by the force on a short displacement \(\Delta x\) is \(F \cdot \Delta x\): only the component of the force along the displacement counts. Adding up along the curve gives an integral that depends on the direction of travel: going backwards, every displacement changes sign.

03DK Definition 27.11.12

Let \(\gamma \colon [a, b] \to \mathbb {R}^n\) be a piecewise \(C^1\) curve and \(F = (F_1, \ldots , F_n)\) a continuous vector field on its image. The line integral of \(F\) along \(\gamma \), or of the second kind, is

\[ \int _\gamma F \cdot d\gamma = \int _a^b\bigl\langle F(\gamma (t)), \gamma ^{\prime }(t)\bigr\rangle \, dt = \int _a^b\sum _{j=1}^nF_j(\gamma (t))\, \gamma _j^{\prime }(t)\, dt, \]

also written \(\int _\gamma F_1\, dx_1 + \cdots + F_n\, dx_n\); in the plane, \(\int _\gamma P\, dx + Q\, dy\) for \(F = (P, Q)\).

The notation \(F_1\, dx_1 + \cdots + F_n\, dx_n\) is a differential form (Chapter 36): the rule is to substitute \(x_j = \gamma _j(t)\) and \(dx_j = \gamma _j^{\prime }(t)\, dt\).

03DL Proposition 27.11.13

Let \(\gamma \) and \(F\) be as in the definition.

  1. The integral is linear in \(F\) and additive under concatenation.

  2. It is unchanged under orientation-preserving \(C^1\) reparametrisations and changes sign under orientation-reversing ones: \(\int _{\gamma ^-}F \cdot d\gamma ^- = -\int _\gamma F \cdot d\gamma \).

  3. If \(\gamma \) is regular, with unit tangent \(T = \gamma ^{\prime }/|\gamma ^{\prime }|\), then \(\int _\gamma F \cdot d\gamma = \int _\gamma \langle F, T\rangle \, ds\): the integral of the second kind is the integral of the first kind of the tangential component of \(F\).

  4. \(\bigl|\int _\gamma F \cdot d\gamma \bigr| \leq \sup |F| \cdot L(\gamma )\).

  5. (Fundamental theorem for line integrals) If \(F = \nabla g\) is the gradient of a \(C^1\) function \(g\) on an open set containing the image of \(\gamma \), then \(\int _\gamma \nabla g \cdot d\gamma = g(\gamma (b)) - g(\gamma (a))\). In particular the integral over a closed curve is \(0\).

Proof â–¼

(1) follows from the properties of the integral. (2) On a \(C^1\) piece, with \((\gamma \circ \varphi )^{\prime }(u) = \varphi ^{\prime }(u)\gamma ^{\prime }(\varphi (u))\), the substitution rule gives \(\int _c^d\langle F(\gamma (\varphi (u))), \gamma ^{\prime }(\varphi (u))\rangle \varphi ^{\prime }(u)\, du = \int _{\varphi (c)}^{\varphi (d)}\langle F(\gamma (t)), \gamma ^{\prime }(t)\rangle \, dt\), which is the original integral if \(\varphi \) increases and its negative if \(\varphi \) decreases. (3) \(\langle F(\gamma (t)), \gamma ^{\prime }(t)\rangle = \langle F(\gamma (t)), T(t)\rangle |\gamma ^{\prime }(t)|\). (4) By Cauchy–Schwarz, \(|\langle F(\gamma (t)), \gamma ^{\prime }(t)\rangle | \leq \sup |F|\, |\gamma ^{\prime }(t)|\); integrate and use Theorem 27.11.4. (5) On a \(C^1\) piece, by the chain rule (Proposition 27.10.3), \((g \circ \gamma )^{\prime }(t) = \langle \nabla g(\gamma (t)), \gamma ^{\prime }(t)\rangle \), so the integral is \(\int (g \circ \gamma )^{\prime } = g(\gamma (c_k)) - g(\gamma (c_{k-1}))\) by Theorem 27.8.3; the intermediate values cancel in the sum.

A vector field that is a gradient, \(F = \nabla g\), is called conservative, and \(-g\) (in physics) its potential: the work done along a path depends only on its endpoints. Not every field is of this kind.

03DM Example 27.11.14

  1. Rotation. For \(F(x, y) = (-y, x)\) and the circle \(\gamma (t) = (r\cos t, r\sin t)\), \(t \in [0, 2\pi ]\), \(\langle F(\gamma (t)), \gamma ^{\prime }(t)\rangle = r^2\sin ^2t + r^2\cos ^2t = r^2\), so \(\int _\gamma F \cdot d\gamma = 2\pi r^2\), twice the area enclosed; this is no accident (Green’s theorem, Chapter 40). The integral over a closed curve is not \(0\), so \(F\) is not a gradient.

  2. Dependence on the path. For \(F(x, y) = (y, 0)\), that is \(\int y\, dx\), from \((0, 0)\) to \((1, 1)\): along the segment \(\gamma _1(t) = (t, t)\) the integral is \(\int _0^1t\, dt = \frac12\); along the parabola \(\gamma _2(t) = (t, t^2)\) it is \(\int _0^1t^2\, dt = \frac13\); along the path that goes first to \((1, 0)\) and then up to \((1, 1)\) it is \(0 + 0 = 0\), since \(y = 0\) on the first piece and \(dx = 0\) on the second.

  3. A gradient. For \(F = \nabla g\) with \(g(x, y) = x^2y\), \(F = (2xy, x^2)\), and every path from \((0, 0)\) to \((1, 1)\) gives \(g(1, 1) - g(0, 0) = 1\). Along \(\gamma _2\), for instance, \(\int _0^1(2t \cdot t^2 + t^2 \cdot 2t)\, dt = \int _0^14t^3\, dt = 1\).

  4. The angle field. \(F(x, y) = (-y, x)/(x^2 + y^2)\) on \(\mathbb {R}^2 \setminus \{ 0\} \) has integral \(2\pi \) around the unit circle, so it is not a gradient on \(\mathbb {R}^2 \setminus \{ 0\} \), although it is the gradient of the polar angle on every half-plane through the origin. This field, the \(1\)-form \((x\, dy - y\, dx)/(x^2 + y^2)\), is the simplest example of a closed form that is not exact, and the beginning of de Rham cohomology (Chapter 37).

03DN Remark 27.11.15

The two kinds of line integrals answer different questions and behave differently. The first kind integrates a density, needs the Euclidean length to define \(ds\), and ignores orientation; it generalises to integrals over curves in a Riemannian manifold (Chapter 40) and, in higher dimensions, to surface integrals \(\int _\Sigma f\, dA\). The second kind integrates a \(1\)-form, needs no metric at all, since \(F_1\, dx_1 + \cdots + F_n\, dx_n\) is evaluated on velocity vectors, and depends on the orientation; it generalises to the integrals of \(k\)-forms over oriented \(k\)-dimensional submanifolds, and Stokes’ theorem lives there (Chapter 36). Complex line integrals \(\int _\gamma f(z)\, dz = \int _a^bf(\gamma (t))\gamma ^{\prime }(t)\, dt\) (Chapter 29) are of the second kind, with complex coefficients: \(f\, dz = (u + iv)(dx + i\, dy)\). The relation \(\int _\gamma F \cdot d\gamma = \int _\gamma \langle F, T\rangle \, ds\) shows that the second kind is a special case of the first, once a direction has been chosen; conversely \(\int _\gamma f\, ds = \int _\gamma fT \cdot d\gamma \) for a regular curve. In Italian the two are the integrali curvilinei di prima e di seconda specie.
