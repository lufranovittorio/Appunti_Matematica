57.6 Reductive groups01QZ

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

01R0 Definition 57.6.1

A linear algebraic group \(G\) over \(k\) is unipotent if it is isomorphic to a closed subgroup of the upper triangular unipotent matrices. The unipotent radical \(R_u(G)\) is the largest connected normal unipotent subgroup of \(G_{\bar k}\). A connected \(G\) is reductive if \(R_u(G) = 1\), and semisimple if moreover its centre is finite.

01R1 Theorem 57.6.2

Let \(k\) have characteristic \(0\) and \(G\) be connected.

  1. \(G\) is reductive if and only if every finite-dimensional representation of \(G\) is semisimple (a direct sum of irreducible representations).

  2. If \(G\) is reductive, then \(G = Z(G)^\circ \cdot G^{\mathrm{der}}\), where the connected centre \(Z(G)^\circ \) is a torus and the derived group \(G^{\mathrm{der}}\) is semisimple, and \(Z(G)^\circ \cap G^{\mathrm{der}}\) is finite.

  3. Tori, \(\mathrm{GL}_n\), \(\mathrm{SL}_n\), \(\mathrm{Sp}_{2g}\) and \(\mathrm{SO}(q)\) are reductive; \(\mathbb {G}_a\) and the group of invertible upper triangular matrices are not.

See [Mil17] and [Bor91]. For example, the representation of \(\mathbb {G}_a\) on \(k^2\) by \(t \mapsto \left(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & t \\ 0 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right)\) has the invariant line \(k e_1\) without an invariant complement, so \(\mathbb {G}_a\) is not reductive.

01R2 Proposition 57.6.3 Compact real forms

Let \(G\) be a connected linear algebraic group over \(\mathbb {R}\) and \(\theta \) an involution of \(G_\mathbb {C}\) such that \(G^{(\theta )} = \{ g \in G(\mathbb {C}) : \theta (\bar g) = g\} \) is compact. Then \(G\) is reductive. In particular, if \(G \subseteq \mathrm{GL}(V)\) and there is a positive definite inner product on \(V\) such that \(G\) is stable under \(g \mapsto g^T\) (the adjoint), then \(G\) is reductive.

Proof â–¼

The group \(K = G^{(\theta )}\) is a compact real form of \(G(\mathbb {C})\): it is the fixed group of the antiholomorphic involution \(g \mapsto \theta (\bar g)\), so its Lie algebra \(\mathfrak {k}\) is a real form of \(\mathrm{Lie}(G_\mathbb {C})\), that is, \(\mathrm{Lie}(G_\mathbb {C}) = \mathfrak {k} \oplus i\mathfrak {k}\). Let \(W\) be a complex representation of \(G_\mathbb {C}\) and \(W' \subseteq W\) a subrepresentation. By Corollary 33.5.3, \(W\) has a \(K\)-invariant Hermitian inner product, and the orthogonal complement \(W''\) of \(W'\) is \(K\)-stable, hence \(\mathfrak {k}\)-stable. As \(\mathfrak {k}\) spans \(\mathrm{Lie}(G_\mathbb {C})\) over \(\mathbb {C}\), \(W''\) is \(\mathrm{Lie}(G_\mathbb {C})\)-stable, hence \(G_\mathbb {C}\)-stable by Proposition 57.5.3. So every representation of \(G_\mathbb {C}\), and therefore of \(G\), is semisimple, and \(G\) is reductive by Theorem 57.6.2(1). For the second statement, extend the inner product to a Hermitian one on \(V_\mathbb {C}\) and take \(\theta (g) = (g^*)^{-1}\), where \(g^*\) is the Hermitian adjoint of \(g \in G(\mathbb {C})\). Then \(\theta (\bar g) = (g^T)^{-1}\) in a real orthonormal basis, so \(G^{(\theta )} = G(\mathbb {C}) \cap U(V_\mathbb {C})\), which is compact.

01R3 Remark 57.6.4

A variant of this argument, with the Weil operator of a polarisation in place of the transpose, shows that Mumford–Tate groups of polarisable Hodge structures are reductive (Chapter 64).
