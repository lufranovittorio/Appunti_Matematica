17.2 Topological spaces and continuous maps00QV

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

Why open sets. In analysis, “\(x_n\) tends to \(x\)” and “\(f\) is continuous at \(x\)” are expressed with distances: \(|x_n - x| {\lt} \varepsilon \) eventually, \(|f(y) - f(x)| {\lt} \varepsilon \) when \(|y - x| {\lt} \delta \). Looking closely, the actual numbers \(\varepsilon \) and \(\delta \) do not matter, only the sets \(\{ y : |y - x| {\lt} \delta \} \) and, more precisely, the sets that contain such a ball around each of their points, the open sets. For instance \(f \colon \mathbb {R}\to \mathbb {R}\) is continuous if and only if the preimage of every open set is open (Proposition 17.3.5 below). Open sets have three basic properties, all following from the definition: \(\emptyset \) and the whole space are open; a union of open sets is open (a ball around a point of the union lies in one of the sets); and the intersection of two open sets is open (around a point of the intersection take the smaller of two balls). An infinite intersection need not be open: the intervals \((-\frac1n, \frac1n)\) are open and their intersection is \(\{ 0\} \), which contains no ball. A topology is a choice of “open sets” with exactly these three properties, and nothing else; this is enough to speak of limits and continuity, and it applies to spaces where no natural distance exists, such as quotient spaces, spaces of functions with pointwise convergence, or the Zariski topology of algebraic geometry.

00QW Definition 17.2.1

A topology on a set \(X\) is a set \(\mathcal{T}\) of subsets of \(X\), called open sets, such that \(\emptyset \) and \(X\) are open, every union of open sets is open, and every finite intersection of open sets is open. A topological space is a set with a topology. A subset \(C \subseteq X\) is closed if \(X \setminus C\) is open. A neighbourhood of a point \(x\) (or of a subset \(A\)) is a set containing an open set containing \(x\) (or \(A\)). If \(\mathcal{T} \subseteq \mathcal{T}'\) are two topologies on \(X\), then \(\mathcal{T}'\) is finer and \(\mathcal{T}\) is coarser.

00QX Example 17.2.2

  1. The discrete topology \(\mathcal{P}(X)\) and the indiscrete topology \(\{ \emptyset , X\} \) are the finest and the coarsest topologies on \(X\).

  2. The cofinite topology on \(X\) consists of \(\emptyset \) and the complements of finite sets. The cocountable topology consists of \(\emptyset \) and the complements of countable sets.

  3. The Sierpiński space is \(\{ 0, 1\} \) with open sets \(\emptyset \), \(\{ 1\} \) and \(\{ 0, 1\} \).

  4. On a two-point set \(\{ a, b\} \) there are exactly four topologies: the indiscrete one, the discrete one, and the two Sierpiński topologies \(\{ \emptyset , \{ a\} , \{ a, b\} \} \) and \(\{ \emptyset , \{ b\} , \{ a, b\} \} \). Indeed a topology must contain \(\emptyset \) and \(\{ a, b\} \), and any choice of which singletons to add satisfies the axioms. The family \(\{ \emptyset , \{ a\} , \{ b\} \} \) is not a topology, since the union \(\{ a\} \cup \{ b\} \) and the whole space are missing.

  5. The metric topology of \(\mathbb {R}\) (Section 17.3): \(U\) is open if every \(x \in U\) has an \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\) with \((x - \varepsilon , x + \varepsilon ) \subseteq U\). Open intervals and their unions are open; \([0, 1)\) is not open (no interval around \(0\) fits inside) and not closed (its complement \((-\infty , 0) \cup [1, \infty )\) is not open at \(1\)). So “not open” does not mean “closed”: a set can be both (\(\emptyset \), \(\mathbb {R}\)), one of the two, or neither.

A finer topology has more open sets. The finer the topology on \(X\), the easier it is for maps from \(X\) to be continuous and the harder for maps to \(X\); every map from a discrete space and every map to an indiscrete space is continuous (Definition 17.2.12 below).

00QY Definition 17.2.3

Let \(A\) be a subset of a space \(X\). Its interior \(\operatorname {int} A\) is the union of all open sets contained in \(A\), its closure \(\overline{A}\) is the intersection of all closed sets containing \(A\), and its boundary is \(\partial A = \overline{A} \setminus \operatorname {int} A\). The set \(A\) is dense if \(\overline{A} = X\).

00QZ Lemma 17.2.4

Let \(A, B \subseteq X\). Then \(\operatorname {int} A\) is the largest open subset of \(A\) and \(\overline{A}\) is the smallest closed set containing \(A\); \(X \setminus \overline{A} = \operatorname {int}(X \setminus A)\); a point \(x\) lies in \(\overline{A}\) if and only if every open set containing \(x\) meets \(A\); and \(\overline{A \cup B} = \overline{A} \cup \overline{B}\).

Proof â–¼

The first two statements hold because unions of open sets are open and intersections of closed sets are closed. Taking complements turns closed sets containing \(A\) into open sets contained in \(X \setminus A\), which gives the third statement. So \(x \notin \overline{A}\) if and only if \(x\) lies in an open set contained in \(X \setminus A\), that is, an open set not meeting \(A\). Finally \(\overline{A} \cup \overline{B}\) is closed and contains \(A \cup B\), so it contains \(\overline{A \cup B}\); the reverse inclusion holds as \(\overline{A}, \overline{B} \subseteq \overline{A \cup B}\).

02T5 Definition 17.2.5

Let \(A \subseteq X\) and \(x \in X\). Then \(x\) is an adherent point of \(A\) if every neighbourhood of \(x\) meets \(A\) (that is, \(x \in \overline{A}\)), an accumulation point of \(A\) if every neighbourhood of \(x\) meets \(A \setminus \{ x\} \), and an isolated point of \(A\) if \(x \in A\) and some neighbourhood of \(x\) meets \(A\) only in \(x\). The set \(A^{\prime }\) of accumulation points is the derived set. A set without isolated points is dense in itself, and a closed set without isolated points is perfect.

The term limit point is used by some authors for accumulation points and by others for adherent points (limits of nets or, in metric spaces, of sequences in \(A\)); we avoid it.

02T6 Proposition 17.2.6

Let \(A \subseteq X\).

  1. \(\overline{A} = A \cup A^{\prime }\), and every point of \(\overline{A}\) is an accumulation point of \(A\) or an isolated point of \(A\), but not both.

  2. \(A\) is closed if and only if \(A^{\prime } \subseteq A\); \(A\) is open if and only if it is a neighbourhood of each of its points.

  3. \(\partial A = \overline{A} \cap \overline{X \setminus A}\) is closed; \(A\) is closed if and only if \(\partial A \subseteq A\), and open if and only if \(\partial A \cap A = \emptyset \); \(\overline{A} = A \cup \partial A\) and \(\operatorname {int}A = A \setminus \partial A\).

  4. \(A\) is dense if and only if it meets every nonempty open set, if and only if \(\operatorname {int}(X \setminus A) = \emptyset \).

  5. If \(X\) is Hausdorff, \(x \in A^{\prime }\) if and only if every neighbourhood of \(x\) contains infinitely many points of \(A\); in particular finite sets have no accumulation points.

  6. A point \(x \in X\) is isolated in \(X\) if and only if \(\{ x\} \) is open; a dense set contains all isolated points of \(X\).

Proof â–¼

(1) A point of \(\overline{A}\) not in \(A\) has neighbourhoods meeting \(A = A \setminus \{ x\} \); a point of \(A\) either has a neighbourhood meeting \(A\) only in \(x\) (isolated) or not (accumulation point), and the two cases exclude each other. Conversely \(A^{\prime } \subseteq \overline{A}\) by Lemma 17.2.4. (2) The first statement follows from (1). If \(A\) is open it is a neighbourhood of its points; if \(A\) contains an open \(U_x \ni x\) for every \(x \in A\), then \(A = \bigcup _xU_x\) is open. (3) \(\overline{X \setminus A} = X \setminus \operatorname {int}A\) by Lemma 17.2.4, so \(\overline{A} \cap \overline{X \setminus A} = \overline{A} \setminus \operatorname {int}A\); it is an intersection of closed sets. The other statements follow from \(\operatorname {int}A \subseteq A \subseteq \overline{A}\). (4) By Lemma 17.2.4, \(\overline{A} = X\) if and only if every point lies in no open set missing \(A\), if and only if no nonempty open set misses \(A\); and \(X \setminus \overline{A} = \operatorname {int}(X \setminus A)\). (5) If a neighbourhood \(U\) of \(x\) meets \(A \setminus \{ x\} \) only in finitely many points \(a_1, \ldots , a_k\), choose by the Hausdorff property open sets \(U_i \ni x\) not containing \(a_i\); then \(U \cap U_1 \cap \cdots \cap U_k\) is a neighbourhood of \(x\) missing \(A \setminus \{ x\} \). (6) The first statement is the definition, and an open \(\{ x\} \) must meet a dense set by (4).

02T7 Example 17.2.7

In \(\mathbb {R}\): for \(A = \{ 1/n : n \geq 1\} \), every point of \(A\) is isolated, \(A^{\prime } = \{ 0\} \), and \(\overline{A} = A \cup \{ 0\} \); so \(A\) is neither open nor closed. \(\mathbb {Z}\) has no accumulation points and is closed. \(\mathbb {Q}\) is dense, with \(\mathbb {Q}^{\prime } = \mathbb {R}\), and has empty interior, so \(\partial \mathbb {Q}= \mathbb {R}\). A closed interval \([a, b]\) (\(a {\lt} b\)) is perfect. In the indiscrete topology on a set with at least two points, every point is an accumulation point of every nonempty set other than a singleton, and (5) fails: the space is not Hausdorff.

02W3 Example 17.2.8

In \(\mathbb {R}\) with its usual topology:

  1. For \(A = [0, 1)\): \(\operatorname {int}A = (0, 1)\), \(\overline{A} = [0, 1]\) and \(\partial A = \{ 0, 1\} \). For \(A = (0, 1) \cup \{ 2\} \): \(\operatorname {int}A = (0, 1)\), \(\overline{A} = [0, 1] \cup \{ 2\} \), and \(\partial A = \{ 0, 1, 2\} \); the isolated point \(2\) belongs to the boundary although it belongs to \(A\).

  2. For \(A = \mathbb {Q}\): \(\operatorname {int}\mathbb {Q}= \emptyset \) (every interval contains irrationals), \(\overline{\mathbb {Q}} = \mathbb {R}\) (every interval contains rationals), and \(\partial \mathbb {Q}= \mathbb {R}\).

  3. The closure does not commute with intersections: for \(A = (0, 1)\) and \(B = (1, 2)\), \(\overline{A \cap B} = \emptyset \) while \(\overline{A} \cap \overline{B} = \{ 1\} \); in general only \(\overline{A \cap B} \subseteq \overline{A} \cap \overline{B}\) holds. It commutes with finite unions (Lemma 17.2.4) but not with infinite ones: the sets \(\{ \frac1n\} \) are closed, while their union has closure \(\{ \frac1n\} \cup \{ 0\} \).

In the discrete topology every set is open and closed, so \(\operatorname {int}A = A = \overline{A}\) and \(\partial A = \emptyset \). In the indiscrete topology on \(X\), a nonempty \(A \neq X\) has \(\operatorname {int}A = \emptyset \) and \(\overline{A} = X\): the topology is too coarse to distinguish the points of \(A\) from the others.

00R0 Definition 17.2.9

A basis of a topology \(\mathcal{T}\) is a subset \(\mathcal{B} \subseteq \mathcal{T}\) such that every open set is a union of members of \(\mathcal{B}\). A subbasis is a subset \(\mathcal{S} \subseteq \mathcal{T}\) such that the finite intersections of members of \(\mathcal{S}\) (the empty intersection being \(X\)) form a basis.

00R1 Lemma 17.2.10

  1. A set \(\mathcal{B}\) of subsets of \(X\) is a basis of some topology if and only if \(\mathcal{B}\) covers \(X\) and for all \(B_1, B_2 \in \mathcal{B}\) and \(x \in B_1 \cap B_2\) there is \(B_3 \in \mathcal{B}\) with \(x \in B_3 \subseteq B_1 \cap B_2\). This topology consists of the unions of members of \(\mathcal{B}\).

  2. For every set \(\mathcal{S}\) of subsets of \(X\) there is a coarsest topology containing \(\mathcal{S}\), the topology generated by \(\mathcal{S}\), and \(\mathcal{S}\) is a subbasis of it.

Proof â–¼

(1) If \(\mathcal{B}\) is a basis, then \(X\) is a union of members and \(B_1 \cap B_2\) is open, hence a union of members, which gives the two conditions. Conversely, let \(\mathcal{T}\) be the set of unions of members of \(\mathcal{B}\). It contains \(\emptyset \) (the empty union) and \(X\) (first condition), and is closed under unions. If \(U = \bigcup _i B_i\) and \(V = \bigcup _j B'_j\), then \(U \cap V = \bigcup _{i,j} B_i \cap B'_j\), and each \(B_i \cap B'_j\) is a union of members of \(\mathcal{B}\) by the second condition. By induction \(\mathcal{T}\) is closed under finite intersections.

(2) The set \(\mathcal{B}\) of finite intersections of members of \(\mathcal{S}\) contains \(X\) and is closed under finite intersections, so it satisfies the conditions of (1). The topology it generates contains \(\mathcal{S}\), and every topology containing \(\mathcal{S}\) contains \(\mathcal{B}\) and all unions of its members.

02W4 Example 17.2.11

  1. In a metric space the open balls form a basis (Section 17.3). In \(\mathbb {R}\) already the intervals \((p, q)\) with rational endpoints form a basis: if \(x \in U\) open, there is \((x - \varepsilon , x + \varepsilon ) \subseteq U\), and rationals \(p \in (x - \varepsilon , x)\), \(q \in (x, x + \varepsilon )\) give \(x \in (p, q) \subseteq U\). This basis is countable, so \(\mathbb {R}\) is second countable.

  2. The rays \((-\infty , a)\) and \((b, \infty )\) form a subbasis of the topology of \(\mathbb {R}\) which is not a basis: the interval \((0, 1) = (-\infty , 1) \cap (0, \infty )\) is open but not a union of rays.

  3. The half-open intervals \([a, b)\) satisfy the conditions of Lemma 17.2.10(1), so they are a basis of a topology, the Sorgenfrey line; it is finer than the usual topology, since \((a, b) = \bigcup _n[a + \frac1n, b)\), and strictly finer, since \([0, 1)\) is open in it.

00R2 Definition 17.2.12

A map \(f \colon X \to Y\) between spaces is continuous if \(f^{-1}(V)\) is open for every open \(V \subseteq Y\). It is a homeomorphism if it is bijective and both \(f\) and \(f^{-1}\) are continuous. It is open (closed) if it maps open (closed) sets to open (closed) sets.

00R3 Proposition 17.2.13

For a map \(f \colon X \to Y\) the following are equivalent:

  1. \(f\) is continuous;

  2. \(f^{-1}(C)\) is closed for every closed \(C \subseteq Y\);

  3. \(f(\overline{A}) \subseteq \overline{f(A)}\) for every \(A \subseteq X\);

  4. for every \(x \in X\) and every open \(V \ni f(x)\) there is an open \(U \ni x\) with \(f(U) \subseteq V\);

  5. \(f^{-1}(S)\) is open for every member \(S\) of a subbasis of \(Y\).

Compositions of continuous maps are continuous, so spaces and continuous maps form a category \(\mathsf{Top}\).

Proof â–¼

(1)\(\Leftrightarrow \)(2) since \(f^{-1}(Y \setminus C) = X \setminus f^{-1}(C)\). (2)\(\Rightarrow \)(3): \(f^{-1}(\overline{f(A)})\) is closed and contains \(A\), hence contains \(\overline{A}\). (3)\(\Rightarrow \)(2): if \(C\) is closed and \(A = f^{-1}(C)\), then \(f(\overline{A}) \subseteq \overline{f(A)} \subseteq C\), so \(\overline{A} \subseteq A\). (1)\(\Rightarrow \)(4): take \(U = f^{-1}(V)\). (4)\(\Rightarrow \)(1): \(f^{-1}(V)\) is the union of the open sets \(U\) provided by (4) for its points. (1)\(\Leftrightarrow \)(5): preimages commute with unions and intersections, and every open set is a union of finite intersections of members of the subbasis. The last statement follows from \((g \circ f)^{-1}(W) = f^{-1}(g^{-1}(W))\).

02W5 Example 17.2.14

  1. Constant maps are continuous: the preimage of any set is \(\emptyset \) or the whole space. The identity of \(X\) is continuous from a finer to a coarser topology, and only in that direction.

  2. The floor function \(\lfloor \cdot \rfloor \colon \mathbb {R}\to \mathbb {R}\) is not continuous: the preimage of the open interval \((-\frac12, \frac12)\) is \([0, 1)\), which is not open. In terms of (4), at \(x = 0\) the open set \(V = (-\frac12, \frac12) \ni 0 = \lfloor 0\rfloor \) contains \(\lfloor U\rfloor \) for no open \(U \ni 0\), since \(U\) contains negative numbers. It is continuous at every non-integer, where it is locally constant.

  3. The indicator function \(\mathbf{1}_\mathbb {Q}\) is continuous at no point, since every interval contains rational and irrational points; restricted to the subspace \(\mathbb {Q}\) it is constant, hence continuous. Continuity depends on the topologies on both sides, and restricting a map to a subspace can make it continuous.

00R4 Counterexample 17.2.15

A continuous bijection need not be a homeomorphism: the identity map from \(X\) with the discrete topology to \(X\) with the indiscrete topology is continuous, and its inverse is not if \(X\) has at least two points.

02T8 Proposition 17.2.16

For a bijection \(f \colon X \to Y\) the following are equivalent:

  1. \(f\) is a homeomorphism;

  2. \(f\) is continuous and open;

  3. \(f\) is continuous and closed;

  4. \(f(\overline{A}) = \overline{f(A)}\) for every \(A \subseteq X\);

  5. \(U \subseteq X\) is open if and only if \(f(U)\) is open.

Proof â–¼

For a bijection, \((f^{-1})^{-1}(U) = f(U)\), so \(f^{-1}\) is continuous if and only if \(f\) is open, if and only if (taking complements) \(f\) is closed; this gives (1)\(\Leftrightarrow \)(2)\(\Leftrightarrow \)(3), and (5) is (2) written out. (1)\(\Rightarrow \)(4): by Proposition 17.2.13(3) applied to \(f\) and to \(f^{-1}\), \(f(\overline{A}) \subseteq \overline{f(A)}\) and \(f^{-1}(\overline{f(A)}) \subseteq \overline{A}\). (4)\(\Rightarrow \)(3): \(f\) is continuous by Proposition 17.2.13(3), and if \(C\) is closed, \(f(C) = f(\overline{C}) = \overline{f(C)}\) is closed.

So a homeomorphism is a bijection of the points that is also a bijection of the open sets: every topological property (being open, closed, dense, compact, connected, Hausdorff, …) is preserved. Two further criteria are proved later: a continuous bijection from a compact space to a Hausdorff space is a homeomorphism (Proposition 17.6.3(4)), and by Brouwer’s invariance of domain a continuous injection from an open subset of \(\mathbb {R}^n\) to \(\mathbb {R}^n\) is open, hence a homeomorphism onto an open image [Hat02, Theorem 2B.3]. Examples: \(x \mapsto x/(1 - |x|)\) is a homeomorphism from the open ball \(B(0, 1) \subseteq \mathbb {R}^n\) onto \(\mathbb {R}^n\), with inverse \(y \mapsto y/(1 + |y|)\), so all open balls are homeomorphic to \(\mathbb {R}^n\); \(t \mapsto e^{2\pi it}\) is a continuous bijection \([0, 1) \to S^1\) that is not a homeomorphism, since \(S^1\) is compact and \([0, 1)\) is not; and \(\mathbb {R}\) is not homeomorphic to \(\mathbb {R}^2\), since removing a point disconnects \(\mathbb {R}\) but not \(\mathbb {R}^2\) (Section 17.5).

00R5 Definition 17.2.17

A space \(X\) is Hausdorff if any two distinct points have disjoint open neighbourhoods. A sequence \((x_n)_{n \in \mathbb {N}}\) in \(X\) converges to \(x\) if every open set containing \(x\) contains \(x_n\) for all sufficiently large \(n\).

00R6 Lemma 17.2.18

In a Hausdorff space every sequence has at most one limit, and every finite subset is closed.

Proof â–¼

If \(x \neq y\) are limits, take disjoint open \(U \ni x\) and \(V \ni y\); for large \(n\), \(x_n \in U \cap V\), a contradiction. For a point \(x\), every \(y \neq x\) has an open neighbourhood not containing \(x\), so \(X \setminus \{ x\} \) is open; finite unions of closed sets are closed.

00R7 Counterexample 17.2.19

The cofinite topology on an infinite set \(X\) is not Hausdorff: two nonempty open sets have finite complements, and the union of these complements is not all of \(X\), so they meet. In this space every injective sequence converges to every point, since an open set misses only finitely many of its terms.
