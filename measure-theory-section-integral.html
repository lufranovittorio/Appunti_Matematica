27.4 The integral013B

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

013C Definition 27.4.1

For a simple function \(s = \sum _ia_i\mathbf{1}_{A_i} \geq 0\) with disjoint \(A_i\), \(\int s\, d\mu = \sum _ia_i\mu (A_i)\) (independent of the representation, by passing to a common refinement). For measurable \(f \colon X \to [0, \infty ]\), \(\int f\, d\mu = \sup \{ \int s\, d\mu : 0 \leq s \leq f \text{ simple}\} \), and \(\int _Ef = \int f\mathbf{1}_E\). A measurable \(f \colon X \to \mathbb {C}\) is integrable if \(\int |f| {\lt} \infty \); then \(\int f = \int (\operatorname {Re}f)^+ - \int (\operatorname {Re}f)^- + i(\int (\operatorname {Im}f)^+ - \int (\operatorname {Im}f)^-)\), where \(g^\pm = \max (\pm g, 0)\).

013D Theorem 27.4.2 Monotone convergence

If \(0 \leq f_1 \leq f_2 \leq \cdots \) are measurable and \(f = \lim f_n\), then \(\int f = \lim \int f_n\).

Proof â–¼

The integrals increase and are at most \(\int f\). Let \(0 \leq s \leq f\) be simple and \(0 {\lt} c {\lt} 1\), and \(E_n = \{ f_n \geq cs\} \), which increase to \(X\). Then \(\int f_n \geq c\int _{E_n}s\), and \(A \mapsto \int _As\) is a measure, so \(\int _{E_n}s \to \int s\) (Proposition 27.2.2). Hence \(\lim \int f_n \geq c\int s\) for all such \(c\) and \(s\).

013E Corollary 27.4.3

  1. For measurable \(f, g \geq 0\) and \(a \geq 0\), \(\int (af + g) = a\int f + \int g\), and \(\int \sum _nf_n = \sum _n\int f_n\).

  2. (Fatou) For measurable \(f_n \geq 0\), \(\int \liminf f_n \leq \liminf \int f_n\).

  3. The integral is linear on integrable functions and \(|\int f| \leq \int |f|\).

  4. For measurable \(f \geq 0\), \(\int f = 0\) if and only if \(f = 0\) a.e.; and \(\mu (\{ f \geq t\} ) \leq \frac1t\int f\) (Chebyshev).

Proof â–¼

(1) For simple functions additivity is clear; approximate by increasing simple functions (Proposition 27.2.4(4)) and use monotone convergence. (2) Apply monotone convergence to \(g_n = \inf _{k \geq n}f_k\), which satisfy \(\int g_n \leq \inf _{k \geq n}\int f_k\). (3) Linearity follows from (1) by decomposing into positive and negative parts. For complex \(f\), write \(\int f = |\int f|e^{i\theta }\); then \(|\int f| = \int \operatorname {Re}(e^{-i\theta }f) \leq \int |f|\). (4) \(t\mathbf{1}_{\{ f \geq t\} } \leq f\); and \(\{ f {\gt} 0\} = \bigcup _n\{ f \geq 1/n\} \).

013F Theorem 27.4.4 Dominated convergence

Let \(f_n\) be measurable with \(f_n \to f\) a.e. and \(|f_n| \leq g\) a.e. for an integrable \(g\). Then \(f\) is integrable and \(\int |f_n - f| \to 0\); in particular \(\int f_n \to \int f\).

Proof â–¼

Modifying on a null set, we may assume convergence and domination everywhere. Then \(|f| \leq g\), and Fatou’s lemma applied to \(2g - |f_n - f| \geq 0\) gives \(\int 2g \leq \int 2g - \limsup \int |f_n - f|\).

013G Proposition 27.4.5

For continuous \(f \colon [a, b] \to \mathbb {C}\), the Lebesgue integral \(\int _{[a,b]}f\, d\lambda \) equals the Riemann integral.

Proof â–¼

For a partition \(P\) with mesh tending to \(0\), the step functions \(\sum _i\min _{[t_{i-1}, t_i]}\operatorname {Re}f \cdot \mathbf{1}_{[t_{i-1}, t_i)}\) converge to \(\operatorname {Re}f\) uniformly, and their Lebesgue integrals are the lower sums (Theorem 27.3.4). Apply dominated convergence, and treat the imaginary part in the same way.

02AD Proposition 27.4.6

Every bounded Riemann integrable function \(f \colon [a, b] \to \mathbb {R}\) (Definition 26.8.1) is Lebesgue measurable and Lebesgue integrable, with the same integral.

Proof â–¼

Choose partitions \(P_1, P_2, \ldots \), each refining the previous one, with \(U(f, P_k) - L(f, P_k) {\lt} 1/k\) (Proposition 26.8.2(1)). Let \(\phi _k\) and \(\psi _k\) be the step functions equal, on the interior of each interval of \(P_k\), to the infimum and supremum of \(f\) there, and to \(f\) at the finitely many partition points. Then \(\phi _k \leq f \leq \psi _k\), \(\phi _k\) increases and \(\psi _k\) decreases (refinement), and their Lebesgue integrals are \(L(f, P_k)\) and \(U(f, P_k)\), since points have measure zero. The limits \(\phi = \lim \phi _k\) and \(\psi = \lim \psi _k\) are measurable, \(\phi \leq f \leq \psi \), and by monotone convergence \(\int (\psi - \phi ) = \lim (U(f, P_k) - L(f, P_k)) = 0\). So \(\psi = \phi \) a.e. (Corollary 27.4.3(4)), hence \(f = \phi \) a.e., and \(f\) is measurable because Lebesgue measure is complete. Finally \(\int f\, d\lambda = \lim \int \phi _k\, d\lambda = \lim L(f, P_k)\), which is the Riemann integral.

02AE Counterexample 27.4.7

The converse fails. The Dirichlet function \(\mathbf{1}_\mathbb {Q}\) on \([0, 1]\) is Lebesgue integrable with integral \(0\), since \(\mathbb {Q}\) is countable and hence of measure zero. It is not Riemann integrable: every nondegenerate interval contains rational and irrational points, so \(L(\mathbf{1}_\mathbb {Q}, P) = 0\) and \(U(\mathbf{1}_\mathbb {Q}, P) = 1\) for every partition \(P\). It is the increasing pointwise limit of the Riemann integrable functions \(\mathbf{1}_{\{ q_1, \ldots , q_n\} }\), each of integral \(0\), for an enumeration of \(\mathbb {Q}\cap [0, 1]\): the Riemann integral does not behave well under monotone limits, which is what the Lebesgue integral repairs (Theorem 27.4.2).
