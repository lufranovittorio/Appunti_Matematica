28.4 The integral013B

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

013C Definition 28.4.1

For a simple function \(s = \sum _ia_i\mathbf{1}_{A_i} \geq 0\) with disjoint \(A_i\), \(\int s\, d\mu = \sum _ia_i\mu (A_i)\) (independent of the representation, by passing to a common refinement). For measurable \(f \colon X \to [0, \infty ]\), \(\int f\, d\mu = \sup \{ \int s\, d\mu : 0 \leq s \leq f \text{ simple}\} \), and \(\int _Ef = \int f\mathbf{1}_E\). A measurable \(f \colon X \to \mathbb {C}\) is integrable if \(\int |f| {\lt} \infty \); then \(\int f = \int (\operatorname {Re}f)^+ - \int (\operatorname {Re}f)^- + i(\int (\operatorname {Im}f)^+ - \int (\operatorname {Im}f)^-)\), where \(g^\pm = \max (\pm g, 0)\).

The idea of the definition. The Riemann integral cuts the domain into intervals; the Lebesgue integral cuts the range: for \(f \geq 0\) and levels \(0 = y_0 {\lt} y_1 {\lt} \cdots \), the simple function \(\sum _iy_i\mathbf{1}_{\{ y_i \leq f {\lt} y_{i+1}\} }\) lies below \(f\), its integral is \(\sum _iy_i\, \mu (\{ y_i \leq f {\lt} y_{i+1}\} )\), and refining the levels approximates \(\int f\) from below. This only needs the measures of the level sets, which is why it works on any measure space and for very irregular functions.

02WF Example 28.4.2

  1. Counting measure on \(\mathbb {N}\). For \(f \colon \mathbb {N}\to [0, \infty ]\), \(\int f\, d\mu = \sum _nf(n)\). If some \(f(n) = \infty \) both sides are \(\infty \). Otherwise the functions \(s_N = f\mathbf{1}_{\{ 0, \ldots , N\} }\) are simple and satisfy \(\int s_N = \sum _{n \leq N}f(n)\) and increase to \(f\), so the monotone convergence theorem below gives the claim; a function \(f \colon \mathbb {N}\to \mathbb {C}\) is integrable exactly when \(\sum _n|f(n)| {\lt} \infty \). So series are integrals, and theorems about integrals specialise to theorems about series.

  2. Dirac measure. \(\int f\, d\delta _x = f(x)\): a simple function \(s \leq f\) has \(\int s\, d\delta _x = s(x) \leq f(x)\), and the simple function \(f(x)\mathbf{1}_{\{ x\} }\) attains it.

  3. The indicator of the rationals. \(\int _{[0, 1]}\mathbf{1}_\mathbb {Q}\, d\lambda = \lambda (\mathbb {Q}\cap [0, 1]) = 0\), as countable sets have Lebesgue measure \(0\). The Riemann integral of \(\mathbf{1}_\mathbb {Q}\) does not exist: every upper sum is \(1\) and every lower sum is \(0\).

013DLean Theorem 28.4.3 Monotone convergence

If \(0 \leq f_1 \leq f_2 \leq \cdots \) are measurable and \(f = \lim f_n\), then \(\int f = \lim \int f_n\).

Proof â–¼

The integrals increase and are at most \(\int f\). Let \(0 \leq s \leq f\) be simple and \(0 {\lt} c {\lt} 1\), and \(E_n = \{ f_n \geq cs\} \), which increase to \(X\). Then \(\int f_n \geq c\int _{E_n}s\), and \(A \mapsto \int _As\) is a measure, so \(\int _{E_n}s \to \int s\) (Proposition 28.2.4). Hence \(\lim \int f_n \geq c\int s\) for all such \(c\) and \(s\).

013E Corollary 28.4.4

  1. For measurable \(f, g \geq 0\) and \(a \geq 0\), \(\int (af + g) = a\int f + \int g\), and \(\int \sum _nf_n = \sum _n\int f_n\).

  2. (Fatou) For measurable \(f_n \geq 0\), \(\int \liminf f_n \leq \liminf \int f_n\).

  3. The integral is linear on integrable functions and \(|\int f| \leq \int |f|\).

  4. For measurable \(f \geq 0\), \(\int f = 0\) if and only if \(f = 0\) a.e.; and \(\mu (\{ f \geq t\} ) \leq \frac1t\int f\) for \(t {\gt} 0\) (Markov’s inequality).

Proof â–¼

(1) For simple functions additivity is clear; approximate by increasing simple functions (Proposition 28.2.6(4)) and use monotone convergence. (2) Apply monotone convergence to \(g_n = \inf _{k \geq n}f_k\), which satisfy \(\int g_n \leq \inf _{k \geq n}\int f_k\). (3) Linearity follows from (1) by decomposing into positive and negative parts. For complex \(f\), write \(\int f = |\int f|e^{i\theta }\); then \(|\int f| = \int \operatorname {Re}(e^{-i\theta }f) \leq \int |f|\). (4) \(t\mathbf{1}_{\{ f \geq t\} } \leq f\); and \(\{ f {\gt} 0\} = \bigcup _n\{ f \geq 1/n\} \).

02SU Remark 28.4.5

Names. The inequality \(\mu (\{ f \geq t\} ) \leq \frac1t\int f\) is usually called Markov’s inequality, and some books call it Chebyshev’s. Chebyshev’s inequality proper is the special case applied to \((f - m)^2\): for a probability measure, \(f \in L^2\) with mean \(m = \int f\) and variance \(\sigma ^2 = \int (f - m)^2\),

\[ \mu (\{ |f - m| \geq t\} ) = \mu (\{ (f - m)^2 \geq t^2\} ) \leq \frac{\sigma ^2}{t^2}. \]

More generally \(\mu (\{ |f| \geq t\} ) \leq t^{-p}\int |f|^p\) for \(p {\gt} 0\). The proof is one line, \(t\mathbf{1}_{\{ f \geq t\} } \leq f\), and it is often all one needs: for instance it shows that convergence in \(L^p\) implies convergence in measure (Proposition 28.6.3).

02SVLean Proposition 28.4.6 Jensen’s inequality

Let \(\mu \) be a probability measure, \(I \subseteq \mathbb {R}\) an open interval, \(\varphi \colon I \to \mathbb {R}\) convex, and \(f\) an integrable function with values in \(I\). Then \(\int f \in I\), and

\[ \varphi \Bigl(\int f\, d\mu \Bigr) \leq \int \varphi \circ f\, d\mu , \]

where the right side is defined in \((-\infty , \infty ]\).

Proof â–¼

Convex functions. For \(s {\lt} t {\lt} u\) in \(I\), writing \(t = \lambda s + (1 - \lambda )u\), convexity \(\varphi (t) \leq \lambda \varphi (s) + (1 - \lambda )\varphi (u)\) is equivalent to each of the inequalities between slopes

\[ \frac{\varphi (t) - \varphi (s)}{t - s} \leq \frac{\varphi (u) - \varphi (s)}{u - s} \leq \frac{\varphi (u) - \varphi (t)}{u - t}. \]

So for \(c \in I\) the slope \((\varphi (t) - \varphi (c))/(t - c)\) is nondecreasing in \(t \neq c\); with \(m = \sup _{t {\lt} c}(\varphi (t) - \varphi (c))/(t - c) \leq \inf _{t {\gt} c}(\varphi (t) - \varphi (c))/(t - c)\), a finite number, \(\varphi (t) \geq \varphi (c) + m(t - c)\) for all \(t \in I\): the graph lies above a line through \((c, \varphi (c))\). The same monotonicity shows that \(\varphi \) has finite one-sided derivatives, so it is continuous, and \(\varphi \circ f\) is measurable.

Jensen. Let \(c = \int f\). If \(I\) is bounded above by \(b\), then \(c \leq b\), and \(c = b\) would force \(\int (b - f) = 0\) with \(b - f {\gt} 0\), which is impossible; similarly at the lower end, so \(c \in I\). With \(m\) as above, \(\varphi (f(x)) \geq \varphi (c) + m(f(x) - c)\); the right side is integrable, so \(\int \varphi \circ f\) is defined in \((-\infty , \infty ]\), and integrating, using \(\mu (X) = 1\), gives \(\int \varphi \circ f \geq \varphi (c) + m(c - c) = \varphi (c)\).

02SW Example 28.4.7

  1. With the convex function \(\varphi (t) = |t|^p\) on \(\mathbb {R}\), \(p \geq 1\), applied to \(|f|\): \((\int |f|)^p \leq \int |f|^p\) for a probability measure.

  2. With \(\varphi = \exp \): \(\exp (\int f) \leq \int e^f\).

  3. Arithmetic and geometric means. Let \(\mu \) give mass \(\frac1n\) to each of \(n\) points, where \(f\) takes the values \(a_1, \ldots , a_n {\gt} 0\), and \(\varphi = -\log \). Then \(-\log (\frac1n\sum a_i) \leq -\frac1n\sum \log a_i\), that is, \((a_1\cdots a_n)^{1/n} \leq \frac1n(a_1 + \cdots + a_n)\).

The hypothesis \(\mu (X) = 1\) matters: for Lebesgue measure on \([0, 2]\) and \(f = 1\), \(\varphi (t) = t^2\) gives \(\varphi (\int f) = 4 {\gt} 2 = \int \varphi (f)\).

013FLean Theorem 28.4.8 Dominated convergence

Let \(f_n\) be measurable with \(f_n \to f\) a.e. and \(|f_n| \leq g\) a.e. for an integrable \(g\). Then \(f\) is integrable and \(\int |f_n - f| \to 0\); in particular \(\int f_n \to \int f\).

Proof â–¼

Modifying on a null set, we may assume convergence and domination everywhere. Then \(|f| \leq g\), and Fatou’s lemma applied to \(2g - |f_n - f| \geq 0\) gives \(\int 2g \leq \int 2g - \limsup \int |f_n - f|\).

013G Proposition 28.4.9

For continuous \(f \colon [a, b] \to \mathbb {C}\), the Lebesgue integral \(\int _{[a,b]}f\, d\lambda \) equals the Riemann integral.

Proof â–¼

For a partition \(P\) with mesh tending to \(0\), the step functions \(\sum _i\min _{[t_{i-1}, t_i]}\operatorname {Re}f \cdot \mathbf{1}_{[t_{i-1}, t_i)}\) converge to \(\operatorname {Re}f\) uniformly, and their Lebesgue integrals are the lower sums (Theorem 28.3.4). Apply dominated convergence, and treat the imaginary part in the same way.

02AD Proposition 28.4.10

Every bounded Riemann integrable function \(f \colon [a, b] \to \mathbb {R}\) (Definition 27.8.1) is Lebesgue measurable and Lebesgue integrable, with the same integral.

Proof â–¼

Choose partitions \(P_1, P_2, \ldots \), each refining the previous one, with \(U(f, P_k) - L(f, P_k) {\lt} 1/k\) (Proposition 27.8.2(1)). Let \(\phi _k\) and \(\psi _k\) be the step functions equal, on the interior of each interval of \(P_k\), to the infimum and supremum of \(f\) there, and to \(f\) at the finitely many partition points. Then \(\phi _k \leq f \leq \psi _k\), \(\phi _k\) increases and \(\psi _k\) decreases (refinement), and their Lebesgue integrals are \(L(f, P_k)\) and \(U(f, P_k)\), since points have measure zero. The limits \(\phi = \lim \phi _k\) and \(\psi = \lim \psi _k\) are measurable, \(\phi \leq f \leq \psi \), and by monotone convergence \(\int (\psi - \phi ) = \lim (U(f, P_k) - L(f, P_k)) = 0\). So \(\psi = \phi \) a.e. (Corollary 28.4.4(4)), hence \(f = \phi \) a.e., and \(f\) is measurable because Lebesgue measure is complete. Finally \(\int f\, d\lambda = \lim \int \phi _k\, d\lambda = \lim L(f, P_k)\), which is the Riemann integral.

02AE Counterexample 28.4.11

The converse fails. The Dirichlet function \(\mathbf{1}_\mathbb {Q}\) on \([0, 1]\) is Lebesgue integrable with integral \(0\), since \(\mathbb {Q}\) is countable and hence of measure zero. It is not Riemann integrable: every nondegenerate interval contains rational and irrational points, so \(L(\mathbf{1}_\mathbb {Q}, P) = 0\) and \(U(\mathbf{1}_\mathbb {Q}, P) = 1\) for every partition \(P\). It is the increasing pointwise limit of the Riemann integrable functions \(\mathbf{1}_{\{ q_1, \ldots , q_n\} }\), each of integral \(0\), for an enumeration of \(\mathbb {Q}\cap [0, 1]\): the Riemann integral does not behave well under monotone limits, which is what the Lebesgue integral repairs (Theorem 28.4.3).

02TK Proposition 28.4.12 Improper integrals

Let \(f \colon [a, \infty ) \to \mathbb {R}\) be continuous, and call \(\lim _{b \to \infty }\int _a^bf\), when it exists, the improper (Riemann) integral \(\int _a^\infty f\).

  1. If \(f \geq 0\), then \(\int _{[a, \infty )}f\, d\lambda = \lim _{b \to \infty }\int _a^bf\) in \([0, \infty ]\).

  2. \(f \in L^1([a, \infty ))\) if and only if \(\int _a^\infty |f| {\lt} \infty \), and then the improper integral exists and equals the Lebesgue integral.

  3. The improper integral can exist without \(f\) being Lebesgue integrable: \(\int _\pi ^\infty \frac{\sin x}{x}\, dx\) converges, but \(\int _\pi ^\infty \frac{|\sin x|}{x}\, dx = \infty \).

The same holds for continuous functions on \((a, b]\) with a singularity at \(a\); for instance \(\int _0^1x^{-p}\, dx\) is finite if and only if \(p {\lt} 1\).

Proof â–¼

(1) \(f\mathbf{1}_{[a, b]}\) increases to \(f\) as \(b \to \infty \) (along any sequence \(b_n \uparrow \infty \)), and \(\int _{[a, b]}f\, d\lambda = \int _a^bf\) (Proposition 28.4.9); apply monotone convergence. (2) The first statement is (1) for \(|f|\). Then \(|f\mathbf{1}_{[a, b]}| \leq |f|\), and dominated convergence gives \(\int _a^bf \to \int f\, d\lambda \). (3) On \([k\pi , (k + 1)\pi ]\) the integral of \(|\sin x|/x\) is at least \(\frac{2}{(k + 1)\pi }\), and \(\sum _k\frac1{k + 1} = \infty \). The integrals \(c_k = \int _{k\pi }^{(k+1)\pi }\frac{\sin x}{x}\, dx\) alternate in sign, decrease in absolute value (substitute \(x \mapsto x + \pi \)) and tend to \(0\), so \(\sum _kc_k\) converges (Lemma 27.5.7); and for \(b\) between \(k\pi \) and \((k + 1)\pi \), \(\int _\pi ^b\) differs from \(\sum _{j {\lt} k}c_j\) by at most \(|c_k| \to 0\). For \(x^{-p}\), \(\int _\varepsilon ^1x^{-p}\, dx = \frac{1 - \varepsilon ^{1-p}}{1 - p}\) for \(p \neq 1\) and \(-\log \varepsilon \) for \(p = 1\).

So for nonnegative functions the improper integral is the Lebesgue integral, and in general Lebesgue integrability is absolute convergence of the improper integral; the convergent improper integrals of non-integrable functions are the analogue of conditionally convergent series.

02TL Proposition 28.4.13 Differentiation under the integral sign

Let \((X, \mu )\) be a measure space, \(I \subseteq \mathbb {R}\) an open interval, and \(f \colon X \times I \to \mathbb {R}\) such that \(f(\cdot , t)\) is integrable for every \(t\), \(f(x, \cdot )\) is differentiable for every \(x\), and \(|\partial _tf(x, t)| \leq g(x)\) for an integrable \(g\) independent of \(t\). Then \(F(t) = \int _Xf(x, t)\, d\mu (x)\) is differentiable, with \(F^{\prime }(t) = \int _X\partial _tf(x, t)\, d\mu (x)\).

Proof â–¼

For \(h_n \to 0\), \((f(x, t + h_n) - f(x, t))/h_n \to \partial _tf(x, t)\), and by the mean value theorem these quotients are bounded by \(g(x)\). So \(\partial _tf(\cdot , t)\) is measurable, as a limit of measurable functions, and dominated convergence gives \((F(t + h_n) - F(t))/h_n \to \int \partial _tf(x, t)\, d\mu \).

02TM Example 28.4.14

Feynman’s trick. Introducing a parameter and differentiating under the integral sign computes integrals that have no elementary antiderivative.

  1. \(F(t) = \int _0^1\frac{x^t - 1}{\log x}\, dx\) for \(t {\gt} -1\): here \(\partial _t\frac{x^t - 1}{\log x} = x^t\), dominated by \(x^{s}\) for \(t \geq s {\gt} -1\), so \(F^{\prime }(t) = \int _0^1x^t\, dx = \frac{1}{t + 1}\), and \(F(0) = 0\) gives \(F(t) = \log (1 + t)\).

  2. \(F(t) = \int _\mathbb {R}e^{-x^2}\cos (tx)\, dx\). Differentiating, with domination by \(|x|e^{-x^2}\), and integrating by parts, \(F^{\prime }(t) = -\int _\mathbb {R}xe^{-x^2}\sin (tx)\, dx = -\frac t2F(t)\); so \(F(t) = F(0)e^{-t^2/4} = \sqrt\pi \, e^{-t^2/4}\) by Example 28.11.3. This is the Fourier transform of the Gaussian.

  3. The Dirichlet integral \(\int _0^\infty \frac{\sin x}{x}\, dx = \frac\pi 2\) (an improper integral, Proposition 28.4.12(3)). For \(t {\gt} 0\) let \(F(t) = \int _0^\infty e^{-tx}\frac{\sin x}{x}\, dx\), a Lebesgue integral. On \(t \geq \delta {\gt} 0\) the derivative \(-e^{-tx}\sin x\) is dominated by \(e^{-\delta x}\), so \(F^{\prime }(t) = -\int _0^\infty e^{-tx}\sin x\, dx = -\operatorname {Im}\int _0^\infty e^{-(t - i)x}\, dx = -\operatorname {Im}\frac{1}{t - i} = -\frac{1}{1 + t^2}\). Since \(|\sin x/x| \leq 1\), \(|F(t)| \leq 1/t \to 0\) as \(t \to \infty \); hence \(F(t) = \frac\pi 2 - \arctan t\), with \(\arctan \) the inverse of \(\tan \colon (-\frac\pi 2, \frac\pi 2) \to \mathbb {R}\). Finally \(F(t) \to \int _0^\infty \frac{\sin x}x\) as \(t \to 0^+\): for a nonincreasing \(C^1\) function \(u \geq 0\), integrating by parts, \(|\int _R^Su(x)\sin x\, dx| \leq 2u(R)\); with \(u(x) = e^{-tx}/x\) this bounds the tails beyond \(R\) by \(2/R\) for all \(t \geq 0\), while \(|\int _0^R(e^{-tx} - 1)\frac{\sin x}{x}\, dx| \leq \int _0^Rtx\, dx = \frac{tR^2}2\). Choosing first \(R\) large and then \(t\) small gives the limit.
