26.4 Continuous functions of a real variable024K

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

024L Definition 26.4.1

Let \(D \subseteq \mathbb {R}\) and \(f \colon D \to \mathbb {R}\). Then \(f\) is continuous at \(a \in D\) if for every \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\) there is \(\delta {\gt} 0\) with \(|f(x) - f(a)| {\lt} \varepsilon \) for all \(x \in D\) with \(|x - a| {\lt} \delta \), and continuous if it is continuous at every point of \(D\).

This is continuity for the metric of \(\mathbb {R}\) restricted to \(D\) (Proposition 17.3.5). If \(a \in D\) is an accumulation point of \(D\), then \(f\) is continuous at \(a\) if and only if \(\lim _{x \to a}f(x) = f(a)\); if \(a\) is isolated, every \(f\) is continuous at \(a\).

024M Proposition 26.4.2

Let \(f, g \colon D \to \mathbb {R}\) and \(a \in D\).

  1. \(f\) is continuous at \(a\) if and only if \(f(x_n) \to f(a)\) for every sequence \((x_n)\) in \(D\) with \(x_n \to a\).

  2. If \(f\) and \(g\) are continuous at \(a\), so are \(f + g\), \(fg\), \(|f|\), \(\max (f, g)\), \(\min (f, g)\), and \(f/g\) if \(g(a) \neq 0\).

  3. If \(f\) is continuous at \(a\) and \(h \colon E \to \mathbb {R}\), with \(f(D) \subseteq E\), is continuous at \(f(a)\), then \(h \circ f\) is continuous at \(a\).

  4. Polynomials are continuous on \(\mathbb {R}\), and rational functions are continuous where the denominator does not vanish.

Proof â–¼

(1) The proof of Theorem 26.3.4 applies verbatim, now allowing \(x_n = a\). (2) follows from (1) and Proposition 26.2.4, since \(\max (f, g) = \frac12(f + g + |f - g|)\) and \(\min (f, g) = \frac12(f + g - |f - g|)\). (3) If \(x_n \to a\), then \(f(x_n) \to f(a)\) and so \(h(f(x_n)) \to h(f(a))\). (4) Constants and the identity are continuous; apply (2).

024N Definition 26.4.3

Let \(f\) be defined on an open interval containing \(a\), and discontinuous at \(a\). The discontinuity is removable if \(\lim _{x \to a}f(x)\) exists in \(\mathbb {R}\) (and differs from \(f(a)\)); it is a jump if both one-sided limits exist in \(\mathbb {R}\) and differ; otherwise it is essential.

024O Example 26.4.4

The function equal to \(1\) for \(x \neq 0\) and \(0\) at \(0\) has a removable discontinuity at \(0\). The function equal to \(\operatorname {sgn}(x)\) for \(x \neq 0\) and \(0\) at \(0\) has a jump. The function equal to \(1/x\) for \(x \neq 0\) and \(0\) at \(0\) has an essential discontinuity, and the Dirichlet function \(\mathbf{1}_\mathbb {Q}\) has an essential discontinuity at every point (Counterexample 26.3.10).

024P Theorem 26.4.5

Let \(f \colon I \to \mathbb {R}\) be monotone on an interval \(I\). Then every discontinuity of \(f\) at an interior point of \(I\) is a jump, and the set of points of discontinuity of \(f\) is countable.

Proof â–¼

We may assume \(f\) increasing. At an interior point \(c\) the one-sided limits \(f(c^-) \leq f(c) \leq f(c^+)\) exist by Proposition 26.3.9, and \(f\) is continuous at \(c\) if and only if \(f(c^-) = f(c^+)\). So every interior discontinuity is a jump, with nonempty open interval \(J_c = (f(c^-), f(c^+))\). If \(c {\lt} c'\) are interior discontinuity points and \(c {\lt} x {\lt} c'\), then \(f(c^+) \leq f(x) \leq f(c'^-)\), so \(J_c \cap J_{c'} = \emptyset \). The set \(\mathbb {Q}\) is countable (see the proof of Proposition 4.8.6); fix an injection \(\iota \colon \mathbb {Q}\to \mathbb {N}\) and send \(c\) to the rational number \(q \in J_c\) with the smallest \(\iota (q)\), which exists as \(J_c\) contains rational numbers. This map is injective, since the \(J_c\) are disjoint, so the interior discontinuities form a countable set. Adding the at most two endpoints of \(I\) keeps it countable.

024Q Example 26.4.6 Thomae’s function

Let \(T(x) = 1/q\) if \(x = p/q\) with \(p \in \mathbb {Z}\), \(q \geq 1\) coprime, and \(T(x) = 0\) for irrational \(x\). Then \(T\) is continuous at every irrational number and discontinuous at every rational number.

Indeed, at a rational \(a\), \(T(a) {\gt} 0\), but there are irrational \(y_n \to a\) (Counterexample 26.3.10) with \(T(y_n) = 0\). At an irrational \(a\), let \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\). The rationals \(p/q\) in \((a - 1, a + 1)\) with \(q \leq 1/\varepsilon \) are finitely many, and none equals \(a\); let \(\delta \) be smaller than \(1\) and than their distances to \(a\). For \(|x - a| {\lt} \delta \), either \(x\) is irrational and \(T(x) = 0\), or \(x = p/q\) with \(q {\gt} 1/\varepsilon \), so \(|T(x) - T(a)| = 1/q {\lt} \varepsilon \).

024R Remark 26.4.7

There is no function \(\mathbb {R}\to \mathbb {R}\) that is continuous exactly at the rational numbers: the set of points of continuity of any function is a countable intersection of open sets, and \(\mathbb {Q}\) is not, by the Baire category theorem (Theorem 17.7.7). By contrast, \(x\mathbf{1}_\mathbb {Q}(x)\) is continuous exactly at \(0\) (Exercise 26.14.9).

024S Theorem 26.4.8 Bolzano’s theorem on zeros

Let \(f \colon [a, b] \to \mathbb {R}\) be continuous with \(f(a) {\lt} 0 {\lt} f(b)\). Then \(f(c) = 0\) for some \(c \in (a, b)\).

Proof â–¼

We construct intervals by bisection. Let \(a_0 = a\), \(b_0 = b\). Given \([a_n, b_n]\) with \(f(a_n) {\lt} 0 \leq f(b_n)\), let \(m = (a_n + b_n)/2\); if \(f(m) {\lt} 0\) put \(a_{n+1} = m\), \(b_{n+1} = b_n\), and otherwise \(a_{n+1} = a_n\), \(b_{n+1} = m\). Then \(f(a_{n+1}) {\lt} 0 \leq f(b_{n+1})\), \((a_n)\) is increasing, \((b_n)\) is decreasing, and \(b_n - a_n = (b - a)/2^n \to 0\). By Proposition 26.2.10 both converge, to the same limit \(c \in [a, b]\). By continuity, \(f(c) = \lim f(a_n) \leq 0\) and \(f(c) = \lim f(b_n) \geq 0\) (Proposition 26.2.5(1)), so \(f(c) = 0\), and \(c \neq a, b\).

024T Corollary 26.4.9

Let \(I\) be an interval and \(f \colon I \to \mathbb {R}\) continuous.

  1. (Intermediate value theorem) \(f\) takes every value between any two of its values, so \(f(I)\) is an interval.

  2. If \(f\) is injective, it is strictly monotone.

  3. Every continuous map \(f \colon [a, b] \to [a, b]\) has a fixed point.

  4. Every real polynomial of odd degree has a real root.

Proof â–¼

(1) If \(f(x) {\lt} y {\lt} f(z)\), apply Theorem 26.4.8 to \(f - y\) on the interval between \(x\) and \(z\) (to \(y - f\) if \(x {\gt} z\)). This is also the case \(X = I\) of Corollary 17.5.5. (2) Let \(a {\lt} b\) in \(I\), and assume \(f(a) {\lt} f(b)\) (otherwise replace \(f\) by \(-f\)). Let \(x {\lt} y\) in \(I\) and put \(h(t) = f((1 - t)b + ty) - f((1 - t)a + tx)\) for \(t \in [0, 1]\). Since \((1 - t)a + tx {\lt} (1 - t)b + ty\) and \(f\) is injective, \(h(t) \neq 0\) for all \(t\). As \(h\) is continuous and \(h(0) {\gt} 0\), (1) gives \(h(1) = f(y) - f(x) {\gt} 0\). So \(f\) is strictly increasing. (3) \(g(x) = f(x) - x\) satisfies \(g(a) \geq 0 \geq g(b)\); if neither is \(0\), apply (1). (4) We may take \(p(x) = x^{2k+1} + c_{2k}x^{2k} + \cdots + c_0\). For \(|x| \geq 1 + \sum |c_i|\) we have \(|c_{2k}x^{2k} + \cdots + c_0| \leq \sum |c_i|\, |x|^{2k} {\lt} |x|^{2k+1}\), so \(p(x)\) has the sign of \(x\) there. Apply (1).

024U Theorem 26.4.10 Weierstrass

A continuous function \(f \colon [a, b] \to \mathbb {R}\) is bounded and attains its maximum and its minimum.

Proof â–¼

If \(f\) were unbounded, there would be \(x_n \in [a, b]\) with \(|f(x_n)| {\gt} n\). By Theorem 26.2.12 a subsequence converges to some \(c\), and \(c \in [a, b]\) by Proposition 26.2.5(1). Then \(f(x_{n_k}) \to f(c)\), so \((f(x_{n_k}))\) is bounded, a contradiction. Let \(s = \sup f\) and choose \(x_n\) with \(f(x_n) {\gt} s - 1/(n + 1)\); a subsequence converges to some \(c \in [a, b]\), and \(f(c) = \lim f(x_{n_k}) = s\) by the squeeze theorem. The minimum is the maximum of \(-f\). (This is the case \(X = [a, b]\) of Corollary 17.6.5.)

024V Counterexample 26.4.11

Each hypothesis of Theorem 26.4.10 is needed: \(x\) on \((0, 1)\) attains neither its supremum nor its infimum; \(1/x\) on \((0, 1]\) is unbounded; \(x\) on \([0, +\infty )\) is unbounded; and the function on \([0, 1]\) equal to \(x\) for \(x {\lt} 1\) and to \(0\) at \(1\) has supremum \(1\), which is not attained.

024W Definition 26.4.12

A function \(f \colon D \to \mathbb {R}\) is uniformly continuous if for every \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\) there is \(\delta {\gt} 0\) with \(|f(x) - f(y)| {\lt} \varepsilon \) for all \(x, y \in D\) with \(|x - y| {\lt} \delta \). It is Lipschitz with constant \(K \geq 0\) if \(|f(x) - f(y)| \leq K|x - y|\) for all \(x, y \in D\).

024X Proposition 26.4.13

Let \(f \colon D \to \mathbb {R}\).

  1. Lipschitz functions are uniformly continuous, and uniformly continuous functions are continuous.

  2. \(f\) is uniformly continuous if and only if \(f(x_n) - f(y_n) \to 0\) for all sequences \((x_n)\), \((y_n)\) in \(D\) with \(x_n - y_n \to 0\).

  3. (Heine–Cantor) If \(D = [a, b]\) and \(f\) is continuous, then \(f\) is uniformly continuous.

  4. A uniformly continuous function maps Cauchy sequences to Cauchy sequences. A uniformly continuous \(f \colon (a, b) \to \mathbb {R}\) on a bounded interval extends to a continuous function on \([a, b]\).

Proof â–¼

(1) Take \(\delta = \varepsilon /K\) (any \(\delta \) if \(K = 0\)); the second statement is clear. (2) If \(f\) is uniformly continuous and \(x_n - y_n \to 0\), eventually \(|x_n - y_n| {\lt} \delta \) and so \(|f(x_n) - f(y_n)| {\lt} \varepsilon \). If \(f\) is not uniformly continuous, there is \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\) and, for each \(n\), points \(x_n, y_n\) with \(|x_n - y_n| {\lt} 1/(n + 1)\) and \(|f(x_n) - f(y_n)| \geq \varepsilon \). (3) If not, take \(x_n\), \(y_n\) as in the last sentence. By Theorem 26.2.12, \(x_{n_k} \to c \in [a, b]\), and then \(y_{n_k} \to c\). By continuity \(f(x_{n_k}) - f(y_{n_k}) \to f(c) - f(c) = 0\), a contradiction. (4) If \((x_n)\) is Cauchy and \(\delta \) corresponds to \(\varepsilon \), eventually \(|x_n - x_m| {\lt} \delta \), so \(|f(x_n) - f(x_m)| {\lt} \varepsilon \). For the extension to \(a\): for any sequence \(x_n \to a\) in \((a, b)\), \((f(x_n))\) is Cauchy, hence converges; by (2), two such sequences give the same limit (as \(x_n - y_n \to 0\)), say \(L\). By Theorem 26.3.4, \(\lim _{x \to a^+}f(x) = L\), and setting \(f(a) = L\) gives a function continuous at \(a\); similarly at \(b\).

024Y Counterexample 26.4.14

  1. \(f(x) = x^2\) on \(\mathbb {R}\) is continuous but not uniformly continuous: with \(x_n = n + 1\) and \(y_n = n + 1 + 1/(n+1)\), \(x_n - y_n \to 0\) but \(f(y_n) - f(x_n) \geq 2\).

  2. \(f(x) = 1/x\) on \((0, 1)\) is continuous but not uniformly continuous: with \(x_n = 1/(n + 2)\) and \(y_n = 1/(n + 3)\), \(f(y_n) - f(x_n) = 1\); it has no continuous extension to \([0, 1)\), in accordance with Proposition 26.4.13(4).

  3. \(f(x) = \sqrt x\) on \([0, +\infty )\) is uniformly continuous, since \(|\sqrt x - \sqrt y| \leq \sqrt{|x - y|}\), but not Lipschitz, since \(\sqrt x/x \to +\infty \) as \(x \to 0^+\). So the implications in (1) are strict.
