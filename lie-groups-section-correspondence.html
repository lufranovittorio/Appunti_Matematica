34.6 The Lie correspondence: Ado’s theorem and Lie’s third theorem038C

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

Every Lie group has a Lie algebra, and a homomorphism of Lie groups has a differential which is a homomorphism of Lie algebras (Proposition 34.2.8). This section shows how much of the group can be recovered from the Lie algebra. The answer, due to Lie for the local statements and to É. Cartan for the global ones, is: everything, up to coverings.

  1. Subalgebras of \(\mathfrak {g}\) correspond to connected Lie subgroups of \(G\) (Theorem 34.6.3).

  2. Every connected Lie group has a universal covering group (Theorem 34.6.5), and homomorphisms of Lie algebras integrate to homomorphisms of groups when the source group is simply connected (Theorem 34.6.7).

  3. Every finite-dimensional real Lie algebra is the Lie algebra of a Lie group (Lie’s third theorem, Theorem 34.6.12). The proof uses Ado’s theorem (Theorem 34.6.9): every finite-dimensional Lie algebra is a Lie algebra of matrices.

So connected, simply connected Lie groups and finite-dimensional real Lie algebras are “the same thing”, and every connected Lie group is a quotient of a simply connected one by a discrete central subgroup (Corollary 34.6.13).

34.6.1 Lie subgroups038D

Cartan’s theorem (Theorem 34.4.1) produces Lie subgroups from closed subgroups. But the subgroups attached to Lie subalgebras need not be closed, and they are not always submanifolds.

038E Definition 34.6.1

A Lie subgroup of a Lie group \(G\) is a subgroup \(H \subseteq G\) together with a structure of Lie group on \(H\) for which the inclusion \(\iota \colon H \to G\) is an immersion (Definition 33.6.1). Its Lie algebra \(\mathfrak {h} = T_eH\) is identified with the subalgebra \(d_e\iota (\mathfrak {h})\) of \(\mathfrak {g}\) (Proposition 34.2.8).

038F Example 34.6.2

Let \(G = T^2 = \mathbb {R}^2/\mathbb {Z}^2\), with abelian Lie algebra \(\mathbb {R}^2\), and let \(\mathfrak {h} = \mathbb {R}(1, \alpha )\) with \(\alpha \) irrational. The subgroup \(H = \{ (t, \alpha t) \bmod \mathbb {Z}^2 : t \in \mathbb {R}\} \), with the manifold structure of \(\mathbb {R}\) transported by the injective homomorphism \(t \mapsto (t, \alpha t) \bmod \mathbb {Z}^2\), is a Lie subgroup with Lie algebra \(\mathfrak {h}\). It is dense in \(T^2\): its intersection with the circle \(\{ 0\} \times \mathbb {R}/\mathbb {Z}\) is \(\{ \alpha n \bmod 1 : n \in \mathbb {Z}\} \), which is dense in \(\mathbb {R}/\mathbb {Z}\) (Kronecker’s theorem: the subgroup \(\mathbb {Z}+ \alpha \mathbb {Z}\) of \(\mathbb {R}\) is not cyclic, hence dense, since a subgroup of \(\mathbb {R}\) with a smallest positive element is generated by it); so \(H\) meets every vertical circle \(\{ s\} \times \mathbb {R}/\mathbb {Z}\) in a dense set. Hence \(H\) is not closed, and not a submanifold, by Proposition 34.2.4: the topology of \(H\) is not the subspace topology.

038G Theorem 34.6.3

Let \(G\) be a Lie group with Lie algebra \(\mathfrak {g}\). For every Lie subalgebra \(\mathfrak {h} \subseteq \mathfrak {g}\) there is a unique connected Lie subgroup \(H \subseteq G\) with Lie algebra \(\mathfrak {h}\). As a group, \(H\) is the subgroup generated by \(\exp (\mathfrak {h})\).

Sketch of proof â–¼

Uniqueness. Let \(H\) be a connected Lie subgroup with Lie algebra \(\mathfrak {h}\). By naturality of the exponential map (Theorem 34.3.3, applied to the inclusion), \(\exp _H = \exp _G\) on \(\mathfrak {h}\), so by Proposition 34.3.9(1) the group \(H\) is generated by \(\exp (\mathfrak {h})\). If \(H_1\) and \(H_2\) are two Lie group structures on this subgroup with Lie algebra \(\mathfrak {h}\), choose a ball \(B \subseteq \mathfrak {h}\) around \(0\) on which both exponential maps are diffeomorphisms onto open neighbourhoods of \(e\); these neighbourhoods are the same set \(\exp (B)\), and in the coordinates given by \(B\) the identity map \(H_1 \to H_2\) is the identity of \(B\). So the identity is a bijective homomorphism which is a diffeomorphism near \(e\), hence (by translation) a local diffeomorphism everywhere, hence a diffeomorphism (Theorem 33.4.14).

Existence. Choose a basis \(X_1, \ldots , X_k\) of \(\mathfrak {h}\) and let \(\widetilde{X}_i\) be the left-invariant fields. They span a left-invariant distribution \(D\), \(D_g = d_e(L_g)(\mathfrak {h})\), which is involutive: for sections \(\sum _if_i\widetilde{X}_i\) and \(\sum _jg_j\widetilde{X}_j\) of \(D\),

\[ \Bigl[\sum _if_i\widetilde{X}_i, \sum _jg_j\widetilde{X}_j\Bigr] = \sum _{i,j}f_ig_j[\widetilde{X}_i, \widetilde{X}_j] + \sum _{i,j}f_i\widetilde{X}_i(g_j)\widetilde{X}_j - \sum _{i,j}g_j\widetilde{X}_j(f_i)\widetilde{X}_i, \]

and \([\widetilde{X}_i, \widetilde{X}_j]\) is the left-invariant field of \([X_i, X_j] \in \mathfrak {h}\). By the Frobenius theorem (Theorem 33.9.8) there is a chart \(\varphi \colon U \to (-\varepsilon , \varepsilon )^n\) centred at \(e\) in which \(D\) is spanned by \(\partial /\partial x^1, \ldots , \partial /\partial x^k\). A smooth curve in \(U\) tangent to \(D\) has constant last \(n - k\) coordinates, so it stays in one slice \(\{ x^{k+1}, \ldots , x^n \text{ constant}\} \); and left translates of curves tangent to \(D\) are tangent to \(D\). Let \(S\) be the slice through \(e\) and \(H\) the subgroup generated by \(S\). One declares the sets \(hW\), for \(h \in H\) and \(W\) open in \(S\), to be open in \(H\), and uses the maps \(hW \to W\), \(y \mapsto h^{-1}y\), followed by the first \(k\) coordinates of \(\varphi \), as charts. The curve argument shows that if a translate \(hW\) meets \(S\) near a point, then it is contained in \(S\) near that point (a curve in \(hW\) through the point stays in one slice), and this gives that the charts are compatible. With this structure \(H\) is a Lie group, the inclusion is an immersion with \(d\iota (T_eH) = T_eS = \mathfrak {h}\), and \(H\) is connected, being generated by the connected set \(S\). The omitted verifications, that the topology is Hausdorff and second countable and that multiplication and inversion are smooth for it, are in [War83, Chapter 3]. Finally \(H\) is generated by \(\exp (\mathfrak {h})\) by the uniqueness part.

For example, the Lie subgroup of \(\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb {R})\) with Lie algebra \(\mathfrak {sl}_n(\mathbb {R})\) is \(\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb {R})\), and the one with Lie algebra the strictly upper triangular matrices is the group of upper triangular unipotent matrices, both closed. In Example 34.6.2 it is the irrational line.

34.6.2 Covering groups and homomorphisms038H

038I Lemma 34.6.4

Let \(\varphi \colon K \to G\) be a homomorphism of connected Lie groups whose differential \(d_e\varphi \) is an isomorphism. Then \(\varphi \) is surjective, its kernel \(\Gamma \) is discrete and central, and \(\varphi \) is a covering map.

Proof â–¼

Since \(\varphi \circ L_k = L_{\varphi (k)} \circ \varphi \), \(d\varphi \) is an isomorphism everywhere, so \(\varphi \) is a local diffeomorphism (Theorem 33.4.14) and in particular open. Its image is an open subgroup of the connected group \(G\), hence all of \(G\) (Proposition 20.2.3(2)). Choose an open \(V \ni e\) in \(K\) on which \(\varphi \) is injective, and an open \(V_0 \ni e\) with \(V_0^{-1}V_0 \subseteq V\). Then \(V_0 \cap \Gamma = \{ e\} \), so \(\Gamma \) is discrete, and for \(\gamma \neq \gamma ^{\prime }\) in \(\Gamma \) the sets \(V_0\gamma \) and \(V_0\gamma ^{\prime }\) are disjoint (if \(v\gamma = v^{\prime }\gamma ^{\prime }\) then \(v^{\prime -1}v = \gamma ^{\prime }\gamma ^{-1} \in V \cap \Gamma \), and since \(\varphi (v^{\prime -1}v) = e = \varphi (e)\) and \(\varphi \) is injective on \(V\), \(v = v^{\prime }\) and \(\gamma = \gamma ^{\prime }\)). For \(k \in K\), the open set \(W = \varphi (kV_0)\) satisfies \(\varphi ^{-1}(W) = \bigsqcup _{\gamma \in \Gamma }kV_0\gamma \): if \(\varphi (x) = \varphi (kv)\) with \(v \in V_0\), then \((kv)^{-1}x \in \Gamma \), so \(x \in kV_0\gamma \) for some \(\gamma \), and the union is disjoint by the previous sentence. On \(kV_0\gamma = L_kR_\gamma (V_0)\) the map \(\varphi \) is \(kv\gamma \mapsto \varphi (k)\varphi (v)\), injective and open, so it maps \(kV_0\gamma \) homeomorphically onto \(W\). So \(W\) is evenly covered. Finally, for \(\gamma \in \Gamma \) the continuous map \(K \to \Gamma \), \(k \mapsto k\gamma k^{-1}\), has connected image in the discrete space \(\Gamma \), so it is constant, equal to \(\gamma \): \(\Gamma \) is central.

038J Theorem 34.6.5 Universal covering group

Let \(G\) be a connected Lie group and \(p \colon \widetilde{G} \to G\) a universal covering, with a point \(\widetilde{e}\) over \(e\). There is a unique structure of Lie group on \(\widetilde{G}\) with identity \(\widetilde{e}\) for which \(p\) is a homomorphism of Lie groups and a local diffeomorphism. Then \(\widetilde{G}\) is simply connected, has the same Lie algebra as \(G\), and \(\ker p\) is a discrete central subgroup with \(G \cong \widetilde{G}/\ker p\); moreover \(\ker p \cong \pi _1(G, e)\).

Proof â–¼

A universal covering exists by Theorem 19.6.5: a manifold is locally path-connected and semilocally simply connected, since every point has a neighbourhood homeomorphic to a ball. Each point of \(\widetilde{G}\) has an open neighbourhood mapped homeomorphically by \(p\) onto an open set of \(G\), and composing with charts of \(G\) gives an atlas of \(\widetilde{G}\) (the transition maps are those of \(G\)); \(\widetilde{G}\) is Hausdorff and second countable (its points are separated by sheets or by \(p\), and the fibres are countable, being in bijection with \(\pi _1(G, e)\), which is countable for a manifold [Lee13, Chapter 1]). For this structure \(p\) is a local diffeomorphism, and a continuous map into \(\widetilde{G}\) is smooth if and only if its composite with \(p\) is.

The space \(\widetilde{G} \times \widetilde{G}\) is simply connected (Proposition 19.3.5) and locally path-connected, so by the lifting criterion (Proposition 19.6.1) the map \(m \circ (p \times p) \colon \widetilde{G} \times \widetilde{G} \to G\) has a unique lift \(\widetilde{m}\) with \(\widetilde{m}(\widetilde{e}, \widetilde{e}) = \widetilde{e}\), and likewise \(i \circ p\) has a unique lift \(\widetilde{\imath }\) with \(\widetilde{\imath }(\widetilde{e}) = \widetilde{e}\). The group axioms follow from uniqueness of lifts (Lemma 19.4.2): for instance \((x, y, z) \mapsto \widetilde{m}(\widetilde{m}(x, y), z)\) and \((x, y, z) \mapsto \widetilde{m}(x, \widetilde{m}(y, z))\) are lifts of the same map \(\widetilde{G}^3 \to G\), \((x, y, z) \mapsto p(x)p(y)p(z)\), and agree at \((\widetilde{e}, \widetilde{e}, \widetilde{e})\), so they are equal; similarly \(\widetilde{m}(\widetilde{e}, x) = x\) and \(\widetilde{m}(x, \widetilde{\imath }(x)) = \widetilde{e}\). The maps \(\widetilde{m}\) and \(\widetilde{\imath }\) are smooth because their composites with \(p\) are, and \(p \circ \widetilde{m} = m \circ (p \times p)\) says that \(p\) is a homomorphism. Uniqueness of the structure is again uniqueness of lifts. Since \(p\) is a local diffeomorphism, \(d_{\widetilde{e}}p\) identifies the Lie algebras (Proposition 34.2.8). By Lemma 34.6.4, \(\Gamma = \ker p\) is discrete and central, and \(\widetilde{G}/\Gamma \to G\) is a bijective homomorphism and a local diffeomorphism, hence an isomorphism. Finally the fibre \(p^{-1}(e) = \Gamma \) is in bijection with \(\pi _1(G, e)\) by Corollary 19.4.4(3), and the bijection \([\gamma ] \mapsto \widetilde{e} \cdot [\gamma ]\) is a homomorphism: the lift of a product of loops \(\gamma * \delta \) is the lift of \(\gamma \) followed by the left translate, by the endpoint \(\widetilde{\gamma }(1)\), of the lift of \(\delta \).

038K Example 34.6.6

  1. \(\mathbb {R}\to S^1\), \(t \mapsto e^{2\pi it}\), is the universal covering group of the circle, with kernel \(\mathbb {Z}= \pi _1(S^1)\); similarly \(\mathbb {R}^n \to T^n\).

  2. \(\mathrm{SU}(2) \cong S^3\) is simply connected, and the double covering \(\mathrm{SU}(2) \to \mathrm{SO}(3)\) with kernel \(\{ \pm I\} \) of Exercise 34.7.2 is the universal covering group of \(\mathrm{SO}(3)\); so \(\pi _1(\mathrm{SO}(3)) \cong \mathbb {Z}/2\mathbb {Z}\). This is the topology behind spin in quantum mechanics: a rotation by \(2\pi \) is a loop in \(\mathrm{SO}(3)\) which is not contractible, while a rotation by \(4\pi \) is.

  3. By the polar decomposition \(A = P \cdot Q\) with \(P = (AA^T)^{1/2}\) positive definite and \(Q = P^{-1}A \in \mathrm{SO}(2)\), the group \(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb {R})\) is homeomorphic to \(\mathcal{P} \times \mathrm{SO}(2)\), where \(\mathcal{P}\) is the set of positive definite symmetric matrices of determinant \(1\); this set is contractible (it is the image of the traceless symmetric matrices, a vector space, under the matrix exponential, which is a homeomorphism onto it by the spectral theorem). So \(\pi _1(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb {R})) \cong \pi _1(S^1) \cong \mathbb {Z}\), and the universal covering group \(\widetilde{\mathrm{SL}}_2(\mathbb {R})\) is an infinite covering of \(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb {R})\).

038L Theorem 34.6.7

Let \(G\) and \(H\) be Lie groups with \(G\) connected and simply connected, and \(\psi \colon \mathfrak {g} \to \mathfrak {h}\) a homomorphism of Lie algebras. There is a unique homomorphism of Lie groups \(\varphi \colon G \to H\) with \(d_e\varphi = \psi \).

Proof â–¼

Uniqueness is Proposition 34.3.9(2). For existence, consider the graph \(\mathfrak {k} = \{ (X, \psi (X)) : X \in \mathfrak {g}\} \), a Lie subalgebra of \(\mathfrak {g} \oplus \mathfrak {h}\), the Lie algebra of \(G \times H\), because \(\psi \) preserves brackets. Let \(K \subseteq G \times H\) be the connected Lie subgroup with Lie algebra \(\mathfrak {k}\) (Theorem 34.6.3), and \(\pi _1 \colon K \to G\), \(\pi _2 \colon K \to H\) the restrictions of the projections, homomorphisms of Lie groups. The differential of \(\pi _1\) at \(e\) is \((X, \psi (X)) \mapsto X\), an isomorphism \(\mathfrak {k} \to \mathfrak {g}\), so by Lemma 34.6.4 \(\pi _1\) is a covering map. As \(G\) is simply connected and \(K\) is connected, the fibre of \(\pi _1\) over \(e\) is in bijection with \(\pi _1(G)/\pi _{1*}\pi _1(K)\), a single point (Corollary 19.4.4(3)); so \(\pi _1\) is a bijective local diffeomorphism, hence an isomorphism of Lie groups. Put \(\varphi = \pi _2 \circ \pi _1^{-1}\). Then \(d_e\varphi (X) = d\pi _2(X, \psi (X)) = \psi (X)\).

038M Counterexample 34.6.8

Simple connectivity is needed. The identity \(\mathbb {R}\to \mathbb {R}\) is an isomorphism from the Lie algebra of \(S^1\) to that of \(\mathbb {R}\), but there is no homomorphism of Lie groups \(\varphi \colon S^1 \to \mathbb {R}\) with \(d\varphi = \mathrm{id}\): its image would be a compact subgroup of \(\mathbb {R}\), hence \(\{ 0\} \), and then \(d\varphi = 0\). Likewise the isomorphism \(\mathfrak {so}(3) \cong \mathfrak {su}(2)\) does not come from a homomorphism \(\mathrm{SO}(3) \to \mathrm{SU}(2)\): by Lemma 34.6.4 it would be a covering of the simply connected \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\) by the connected \(\mathrm{SO}(3)\), hence an isomorphism, while \(\pi _1(\mathrm{SO}(3)) \neq 1\).

34.6.3 Ado’s theorem038N

Every Lie group we have met is a group of matrices, and its Lie algebra is a Lie algebra of matrices with the commutator bracket. For Lie algebras this is always the case.

038O Theorem 34.6.9 Ado

Every finite-dimensional Lie algebra \(\mathfrak {g}\) over a field \(k\) of characteristic \(0\) has a faithful finite-dimensional representation: there is an injective homomorphism of Lie algebras \(\mathfrak {g} \to \mathfrak {gl}_n(k)\) for some \(n\). Equivalently, \(\mathfrak {g}\) is isomorphic to a Lie subalgebra of \(\mathfrak {gl}_n(k)\).

The proof is long, and we do not give it; see [Kna02, Appendix B]. (Ado proved it in 1935; Iwasawa proved the analogue in characteristic \(p\), and Harish-Chandra gave a proof valid over every field of characteristic \(0\).) Its first step is easy, and shows where the difficulty lies.

038P Proposition 34.6.10

Let \(\mathfrak {g}\) be a finite-dimensional Lie algebra with centre \(\mathfrak {z} = \{ Z : [Z, X] = 0 \text{ for all } X\} \).

  1. The kernel of the adjoint representation \(\operatorname {ad}\colon \mathfrak {g} \to \mathfrak {gl}(\mathfrak {g})\) is \(\mathfrak {z}\). In particular, if \(\mathfrak {z} = 0\), for instance if \(\mathfrak {g}\) is semisimple, then \(\operatorname {ad}\) is faithful.

  2. If \(\rho \) is a finite-dimensional representation of \(\mathfrak {g}\) that is injective on \(\mathfrak {z}\), then \(\rho \oplus \operatorname {ad}\) is faithful.

Proof â–¼

(1) \(\operatorname {ad}(X) = 0\) means \([X, Y] = 0\) for all \(Y\). A semisimple Lie algebra has no nonzero abelian ideals, and \(\mathfrak {z}\) is an abelian ideal. (2) If \((\rho \oplus \operatorname {ad})(X) = 0\), then \(X \in \ker \operatorname {ad}= \mathfrak {z}\) by (1), and \(\rho (X) = 0\) gives \(X = 0\).

So the whole difficulty of Ado’s theorem is to find a representation which is faithful on the centre; the proof constructs it by induction along a chain of ideals, using the Levi decomposition and quotients of the universal enveloping algebra of the nilradical.

038Q Example 34.6.11

  1. The abelian Lie algebra \(k^m\) has \(\operatorname {ad}= 0\), but \(x \mapsto \operatorname {diag}(x_1, \ldots , x_m)\) is a faithful representation, and so is \(x \mapsto \) the block diagonal matrix with blocks \(\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & x_i \\ 0 & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\), which consists of nilpotent matrices.

  2. The Heisenberg algebra with basis \(P\), \(Q\), \(Z\) and \([P, Q] = Z\), \([P, Z] = [Q, Z] = 0\) has centre \(kZ\), so its adjoint representation is not faithful. The assignment

    \[ P \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad Q \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad Z \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \]

    is faithful, as one checks by computing the commutators. In quantum mechanics \(P\) and \(Q\) are momentum and position, with \([P, Q]\) a multiple of the identity; that relation has no solution in matrices with \(Z\) invertible (take traces), which is why the Schrödinger representation is infinite-dimensional, while the finite-dimensional faithful representation above has \(Z\) nilpotent.

  3. The two-dimensional nonabelian Lie algebra \([x, y] = y\) has trivial centre, and \(\operatorname {ad}\) realises it as \(x \mapsto \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\), \(y \mapsto \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\) in the basis \(x\), \(y\); it is the Lie algebra of the group of affine maps \(t \mapsto at + b\), \(a {\gt} 0\).

34.6.4 Lie’s third theorem038R

038S Theorem 34.6.12 Lie’s third theorem

For every finite-dimensional real Lie algebra \(\mathfrak {g}\) there is a connected and simply connected Lie group \(\widetilde{G}\) with Lie algebra isomorphic to \(\mathfrak {g}\), and it is unique up to isomorphism.

Proof â–¼

By Ado’s theorem (Theorem 34.6.9) we may regard \(\mathfrak {g}\) as a Lie subalgebra of \(\mathfrak {gl}_n(\mathbb {R})\), the Lie algebra of \(\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb {R})\) (Example 34.2.9). By Theorem 34.6.3 there is a connected Lie subgroup \(G \subseteq \mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb {R})\) with Lie algebra \(\mathfrak {g}\), and by Theorem 34.6.5 its universal covering group \(\widetilde{G}\) is a connected, simply connected Lie group with the same Lie algebra. If \(G_1\) and \(G_2\) are connected and simply connected with Lie algebras isomorphic to \(\mathfrak {g}\), Theorem 34.6.7 integrates an isomorphism \(\psi \colon \mathfrak {g}_1 \to \mathfrak {g}_2\) and its inverse to homomorphisms \(\varphi \colon G_1 \to G_2\) and \(\varphi ^{\prime } \colon G_2 \to G_1\); the composites have differential the identity, so they are the identity by Proposition 34.3.9(2), and \(\varphi \) is an isomorphism.

038T Corollary 34.6.13 The Lie correspondence

  1. \(G \mapsto \mathfrak {g}\) is a bijection between isomorphism classes of connected, simply connected Lie groups and isomorphism classes of finite-dimensional real Lie algebras, and for such groups \(\operatorname {Hom}(G, H) \to \operatorname {Hom}(\mathfrak {g}, \mathfrak {h})\), \(\varphi \mapsto d_e\varphi \), is bijective.

  2. Every connected Lie group with Lie algebra \(\mathfrak {g}\) is isomorphic to \(\widetilde{G}/\Gamma \), where \(\widetilde{G}\) is the simply connected group of Theorem 34.6.12 and \(\Gamma \) is a discrete subgroup of the centre of \(\widetilde{G}\); and every such quotient is a connected Lie group with Lie algebra \(\mathfrak {g}\).

Proof â–¼

(1) is Theorems 34.6.12 and 34.6.7. (2) The universal covering group of a connected \(G\) with Lie algebra \(\mathfrak {g}\) is simply connected with Lie algebra \(\mathfrak {g}\), hence isomorphic to \(\widetilde{G}\), and \(G \cong \widetilde{G}/\ker p\) with \(\ker p\) discrete and central (Theorem 34.6.5). Conversely, a discrete subgroup \(\Gamma \) acts freely and properly on \(\widetilde{G}\) by translations, so \(\widetilde{G}/\Gamma \) is a manifold (Theorem 33.7.1), a group because \(\Gamma \) is normal, and a Lie group with the same Lie algebra because the projection is a local diffeomorphism and a homomorphism.

038U Example 34.6.14

  1. \(\mathfrak {g} = \mathbb {R}^n\) abelian: \(\widetilde{G} = \mathbb {R}^n\), whose discrete subgroups are lattices \(\mathbb {Z}v_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus \mathbb {Z}v_b\) (Lemma 34.3.10); the connected groups are \(\mathbb {R}^{n-b} \times T^b\), which is Theorem 34.3.11 again.

  2. \(\mathfrak {g} = \mathfrak {su}(2) \cong \mathfrak {so}(3)\): \(\widetilde{G} = \mathrm{SU}(2)\) with centre \(\{ \pm I\} \), so the connected groups with this Lie algebra are exactly \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\) and \(\mathrm{SO}(3) = \mathrm{SU}(2)/\{ \pm I\} \).

  3. \(\mathfrak {g} = \mathfrak {sl}_2(\mathbb {R})\): \(\widetilde{G} = \widetilde{\mathrm{SL}}_2(\mathbb {R})\), whose centre contains the kernel \(\pi _1(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb {R})) \cong \mathbb {Z}\) of the covering; its quotients include \(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb {R})\), \(\mathrm{PSL}_2(\mathbb {R}) = \mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb {R})/\{ \pm I\} \) and the \(m\)-fold coverings of \(\mathrm{PSL}_2(\mathbb {R})\).

038V Remark 34.6.15

Ado’s theorem makes every Lie algebra linear, but not every Lie group: the universal covering group \(\widetilde{\mathrm{SL}}_2(\mathbb {R})\) has no faithful finite-dimensional representation, so it is not isomorphic to a group of matrices [Kna02]. This is why the proof of Lie’s third theorem passes through a matrix group \(G\) and then to its universal covering, which may leave the world of matrices. For compact groups the situation is better: by the Peter–Weyl theorem every compact Lie group is isomorphic to a closed subgroup of some \(\mathrm{U}(n)\) [BtD85]. Lie’s third theorem can also be proved without Ado’s theorem, by É. Cartan’s method: by the Levi decomposition \(\mathfrak {g} = \mathfrak {r} \rtimes \mathfrak {s}\) with \(\mathfrak {r}\) solvable and \(\mathfrak {s}\) semisimple, a simply connected group for \(\mathfrak {s}\) comes from its adjoint representation (Proposition 34.6.10(1)) and the covering construction, one for \(\mathfrak {r}\) is built by successive extensions by \(\mathbb {R}\), and the two are combined in a semidirect product.
