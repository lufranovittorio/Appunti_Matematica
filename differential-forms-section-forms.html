36.2 Differential forms019Q

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

019R Definition 36.2.1

A differential \(k\)-form on a smooth manifold \(M\) is a smooth section of \(\Lambda ^kT^*M\); the space of \(k\)-forms is \(\Omega ^k(M)\), and \(\Omega ^\bullet (M) = \bigoplus _k\Omega ^k(M)\) with the wedge product, defined pointwise by \((\alpha \wedge \beta )_p = \alpha _p \wedge \beta _p\) in the exterior algebra of the vector space \(T_p^*M\) (Definition 36.2.7 below), is a graded-commutative algebra over \(C^\infty (M)\) (Proposition 36.2.8). By Proposition 36.2.6 below, a \(k\)-form is the same as a smoothly varying family of alternating \(k\)-linear maps \((T_pM)^k \to \mathbb {R}\), or as a family of coefficient functions in charts transforming in a prescribed way. For \(f \colon N \to M\) smooth, the pullback \(f^*\omega \) is defined by \((f^*\omega )_p(v_1, \ldots , v_k) = \omega _{f(p)}(d_pf\, v_1, \ldots , d_pf\, v_k)\); it is an algebra homomorphism (Proposition 36.2.8(4)) and \((g \circ f)^* = f^*g^*\).

In a chart \((U, (x^1, \ldots , x^n))\), \(dx^i\) is the differential of the coordinate function \(x^i\), so \(dx^i(\partial /\partial x^j) = \partial x^i/\partial x^j = \delta _{ij}\): the \(dx^i|_p\) form the basis of \(T_p^*M\) dual to the basis \(\partial /\partial x^j|_p\) of \(T_pM\). For a multi-index \(I = (i_1 {\lt} \cdots {\lt} i_k)\) we write \(dx^I = dx^{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge dx^{i_k}\); for a sequence \((i_1, \ldots , i_k)\) that is not increasing, \(dx^{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge dx^{i_k}\) is \(0\) if two indices coincide and \(\pm dx^I\) otherwise, with the sign of the permutation that sorts the indices.

02P1 Remark 36.2.2

The most important \(1\)-forms are the differentials of functions. For \(f \in C^\infty (M)\), the differential \(d_pf \colon T_pM \to T_{f(p)}\mathbb {R}\) (Definition 33.4.1) takes values in the tangent space of \(\mathbb {R}\), which is identified with \(\mathbb {R}\) by \(w \mapsto w(t)\), the value of the derivation \(w\) on the coordinate function \(t\) of \(\mathbb {R}\) (that is, \(w = w(t)\, d/dt\)). With this identification

\[ (df)_p(v) = d_pf(v)(t) = v(t \circ f) = v(f), \qquad \text{so}\qquad df(X) = X(f) = Xf \]

for a vector field \(X\): the differential of \(f\) evaluated on \(X\) is the derivative of \(f\) in the direction \(X\). In a chart, \(df(\partial /\partial x^j) = \partial f/\partial x^j\), so \(df = \sum _j\frac{\partial f}{\partial x^j}dx^j\); for \(f = x^i\) this gives the statement \(dx^i(\partial /\partial x^j) = \delta _{ij}\) above. The \(1\)-form \(df\) is smooth, since its coefficients are, and \(d(fg) = f\, dg + g\, df\) by the Leibniz rule for tangent vectors.

02VV Remark 36.2.3

Pullback, and what a smooth map induces. The definition of \(f^*\omega \) says: to evaluate \(f^*\omega \) on tangent vectors at \(p \in N\), push the vectors forward to \(f(p)\) with the differential \(d_pf\) and evaluate \(\omega \) there. Since \(d_pf\) is linear, \((f^*\omega )_p\) is again alternating and multilinear, and \(f^*\) is linear, compatible with wedge products (\(f^*(\omega \wedge \eta ) = f^*\omega \wedge f^*\eta \), as both sides are computed pointwise from the same multilinear algebra) and functorial: \((g \circ f)^* = f^*g^*\) by the chain rule \(d(g \circ f) = dg \circ df\), and \(\mathrm{id}^* = \mathrm{id}\). A smooth map \(f \colon N \to M\) induces maps in two directions:

  1. forward (covariantly): tangent vectors, \(d_pf \colon T_pN \to T_{f(p)}M\), and with them \(k\)-vectors (Proposition 36.2.5); and curves, \(\gamma \mapsto f \circ \gamma \);

  2. backward (contravariantly): functions, \(f^*g = g \circ f\); covectors and forms, \(f^*\omega \); covariant tensors such as metrics, \((f^*g)(v, w) = g(df\, v, df\, w)\), which is again a metric if and only if \(f\) is an immersion (Remark 36.2.4); vector bundles and their sections, \(f^*E\) (Chapter 35); and de Rham cohomology (Chapter 37).

Vector fields are neither: a vector field \(X\) on \(N\) cannot be pushed forward in general (if \(f\) is not injective, two points with the same image may give different vectors, and if \(f\) is not surjective there is nothing to define off the image), nor pulled back. A diffeomorphism transports everything in both directions, \(f_*X = df \circ X \circ f^{-1}\); for a general \(f\) one only has the notion of \(f\)-related fields, \(df(X_p) = Y_{f(p)}\), and then \(f^*(\omega (Y_1, \ldots , Y_k)) = (f^*\omega )(X_1, \ldots , X_k)\). This asymmetry is why differential forms, not vector fields, are the objects that can be integrated over images of maps and that form a contravariant functor, cohomology.

02VW Remark 36.2.4

For \(f \colon N \to (M, g)\) with \(g\) a Riemannian metric, \((f^*g)_p(v, v) = |d_pf\, v|^2\) vanishes for \(v \neq 0\) exactly when \(v \in \ker d_pf\); so \(f^*g\) is positive definite, a metric, if and only if every \(d_pf\) is injective. For the inclusion of a submanifold this is the induced metric (Chapter 40).

The maps of Remark 36.2.3 are all obtained from the single linear map \(d_pf \colon T_pN \to T_{f(p)}M\) by the functorial constructions of multilinear algebra (Chapter 12): constructions built from the tangent space go forward, those built from its dual go backward. For the exterior algebra this gives two families of maps, one in each direction, and the pullback of forms is one of them.

03BO Proposition 36.2.5

Let \(f \colon N \to M\) be smooth, \(p \in N\), and \(k \geq 0\).

  1. The differential induces linear maps \(\Lambda ^kd_pf \colon \Lambda ^kT_pN \to \Lambda ^kT_{f(p)}M\), \(v_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge v_k \mapsto d_pf\, v_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge d_pf\, v_k\), on \(k\)-vectors, and its transpose \((d_pf)^* \colon T^*_{f(p)}M \to T^*_pN\), \(\varphi \mapsto \varphi \circ d_pf\), induces \(\Lambda ^k(d_pf)^* \colon \Lambda ^kT^*_{f(p)}M \to \Lambda ^kT^*_pN\).

  2. \(\Lambda ^k(d_pf)^*\) is the pullback, \((f^*\omega )_p = \Lambda ^k(d_pf)^*(\omega _{f(p)})\), and under the pairings of Proposition 12.5.1 it is the transpose of \(\Lambda ^kd_pf\):

    \[ \langle (f^*\omega )_p, v_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge v_k\rangle = \langle \omega _{f(p)}, \Lambda ^kd_pf(v_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge v_k)\rangle . \]

  3. Let \((x^1, \ldots , x^n)\) be a chart near \(p\) and \((y^1, \ldots , y^m)\) a chart near \(f(p)\), and write \(y^i\) also for the components of \(f\) in these charts. In the bases \(\partial _{x^J} = \partial /\partial x^{j_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge \partial /\partial x^{j_k}\) and \(\partial _{y^I}\), for increasing multi-indices, the matrix of \(\Lambda ^kd_pf\) consists of the \(k \times k\) minors \(\partial (y^{i_1}, \ldots , y^{i_k})/\partial (x^{j_1}, \ldots , x^{j_k})\) of the Jacobian matrix at \(p\), and

    \[ f^*(dy^{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge dy^{i_k}) = \sum _{j_1 {\lt} \cdots {\lt} j_k}\frac{\partial (y^{i_1}, \ldots , y^{i_k})}{\partial (x^{j_1}, \ldots , x^{j_k})}\, dx^{j_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge dx^{j_k} . \]

    In particular, if \(n = m\), then \(f^*(dy^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge dy^n) = \det \bigl(\partial y^i/\partial x^j\bigr)\, dx^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge dx^n\).

  4. The rank of \(d_pf\) is the largest \(r\) with \(\Lambda ^rd_pf \neq 0\), equivalently with \(\Lambda ^r(d_pf)^* \neq 0\).

Proof â–¼

(1) \(\Lambda ^k\) is a functor on linear maps (Proposition 12.3.3(5)), and the transpose of a linear map is linear (Definition 11.4.1).

(2) Identify \(\Lambda ^kT^*_qM\) with the alternating \(k\)-linear forms on \(T_qM\) (Notation 12.5.2); then a decomposable element \(\varphi _1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \varphi _k\) is the form \((w_1, \ldots , w_k) \mapsto \det (\varphi _a(w_b))\), by Proposition 12.5.1. Its image under \(\Lambda ^k(d_pf)^*\) is \((\varphi _1 \circ d_pf) \wedge \cdots \wedge (\varphi _k \circ d_pf)\), the form \((v_1, \ldots , v_k) \mapsto \det (\varphi _a(d_pf\, v_b))\), that is, \((v_1, \ldots , v_k) \mapsto (\varphi _1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \varphi _k)(d_pf\, v_1, \ldots , d_pf\, v_k)\). By linearity, \(\Lambda ^k(d_pf)^*\) sends every \(\omega _{f(p)}\) to \((v_1, \ldots , v_k) \mapsto \omega _{f(p)}(d_pf\, v_1, \ldots , d_pf\, v_k)\), which is \((f^*\omega )_p\) by Definition 36.2.1. The displayed formula is the same statement written with the pairing, since \(\langle \alpha , v_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge v_k\rangle = \alpha (v_1, \ldots , v_k)\).

(3) The matrix of \(d_pf\) in the bases \((\partial /\partial x^j)\) and \((\partial /\partial y^i)\) is the Jacobian matrix \((\partial y^i/\partial x^j)\), because \(d_pf(\partial /\partial x^j)(y^i) = \partial (y^i \circ f)/\partial x^j\) (Definition 33.4.1). By Proposition 12.3.4(1) the matrix of \(\Lambda ^kd_pf\) consists of its \(k \times k\) minors. By (2), the matrix of the pullback in the dual bases \((dy^I)\) and \((dx^J)\) is the transpose of this matrix, which is the formula. For \(k = n = m\) there is a single minor, the determinant.

(4) The first statement is Proposition 12.3.4(3). A linear map and its transpose have the same rank (Proposition 11.4.2(5)), and by (2) \(\Lambda ^r(d_pf)^*\) is the transpose of \(\Lambda ^rd_pf\).

So the pullback of a form is “\(\Lambda ^k\) of the transpose of the Jacobian matrix”, and the familiar factor \(\det (\partial y^i/\partial x^j)\) in the pullback of a top degree form is the case \(k = n\); up to its absolute value it is the factor of the change of variables formula (Theorem 28.11.2), which is why the integral of a top degree form does not depend on the chart, up to sign (Section 36.4). The \(k\)-vectors \(v_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge v_k\) have a geometric meaning: up to scalars, a nonzero decomposable \(k\)-vector is the same as a \(k\)-dimensional subspace of the tangent space with a chosen orientation and volume, and \(\Lambda ^kd_pf\) says how \(f\) transforms such infinitesimal \(k\)-dimensional parallelepipeds. A \(k\)-form is what measures them, and \(f^*\omega \) measures a parallelepiped by measuring its image.

02I6 Proposition 36.2.6

Let \(M\) be a smooth \(n\)-manifold. For each \(p\), the fibre \(\Lambda ^kT_p^*M\) is the space of alternating \(k\)-linear maps \((T_pM)^k \to \mathbb {R}\) (Proposition 12.5.1 and Notation 12.5.2). For a family \(\omega = (\omega _p)_{p \in M}\) of such maps the following are equivalent:

  1. \(\omega \) is a smooth section of \(\Lambda ^kT^*M\), that is, a \(k\)-form;

  2. for all smooth vector fields \(X_1, \ldots , X_k\) on an open set \(U\), the function \(p \mapsto \omega _p(X_1(p), \ldots , X_k(p))\) is smooth on \(U\);

  3. in every chart of some atlas, the coefficients \(\omega _I(p) = \omega _p(\partial /\partial x^{i_1}|_p, \ldots , \partial /\partial x^{i_k}|_p)\) are smooth.

In a chart \(\omega = \sum _I\omega _I\, dx^I\), uniquely. If \((y^1, \ldots , y^n)\) is a second chart, then on the overlap

\[ dx^I = \sum _J\det \Bigl(\frac{\partial x^I}{\partial y^J}\Bigr)dy^J, \qquad \omega ^{(y)}_J = \sum _I\omega ^{(x)}_I\det \Bigl(\frac{\partial x^I}{\partial y^J}\Bigr), \]

where \(\partial x^I/\partial y^J\) is the \(k \times k\) matrix \((\partial x^{i_a}/\partial y^{j_b})_{a,b}\) and the superscripts indicate the chart. For \(k = n\) this reads \(\omega ^{(y)} = \omega ^{(x)}\det (\partial x/\partial y)\), with the Jacobian determinant of the transition map. Conversely, functions \(\omega ^{(x)}_I\) given on the charts of an atlas and satisfying this rule on all overlaps come from a unique \(k\)-form.

Proof â–¼

The expression in a chart. Since the \(dx^i|_p\) are the basis dual to the \(\partial /\partial x^i|_p\), the \(dx^I|_p\) form a basis of \(\Lambda ^kT_p^*M\) (Proposition 12.3.3(3)), and for increasing \(I\) and \(J\), \(dx^I(\partial /\partial x^{j_1}, \ldots , \partial /\partial x^{j_k}) = \det (dx^{i_a}(\partial /\partial x^{j_b}))_{a,b}\), which is \(1\) if \(I = J\) and \(0\) otherwise (Notation 12.5.2). Evaluating \(\omega _p = \sum _Ic_I\, dx^I|_p\) on \((\partial /\partial x^{j_1}, \ldots , \partial /\partial x^{j_k})\) therefore gives \(c_J = \omega _J(p)\): the coefficients are the \(\omega _J\), and they are unique.

(1) \(\Leftrightarrow \) (3). The smooth structure of \(\Lambda ^kT^*M\) (Proposition 35.3.1) is the one for which the frames \((dx^I)\) of charts are local trivialisations; a section is smooth if and only if its coefficients in these frames are smooth. (3) \(\Rightarrow \) (2). On a chart domain write \(X_b = \sum _jX_b^j\, \partial /\partial x^j\). By multilinearity \(\omega (X_1, \ldots , X_k) = \sum _I\omega _I\, dx^I(X_1, \ldots , X_k)\), and \(dx^I(X_1, \ldots , X_k) = \det (X_b^{i_a})_{a,b}\) is a polynomial in the smooth functions \(X_b^j\). (2) \(\Rightarrow \) (3). Take the \(X_b\) to be coordinate vector fields.

Change of chart. By the first part applied to the chart \(y\), the coefficient of \(dy^J\) in \(dx^I\) is \(dx^I(\partial /\partial y^{j_1}, \ldots , \partial /\partial y^{j_k}) = \det (dx^{i_a}(\partial /\partial y^{j_b})) = \det (\partial x^{i_a}/\partial y^{j_b})\), since \(dx^i(\partial /\partial y^j) = \partial x^i/\partial y^j\) is the derivative of the function \(x^i\) in the direction \(\partial /\partial y^j\). Substituting into \(\omega = \sum _I\omega ^{(x)}_I\, dx^I\) and comparing coefficients gives the rule for \(\omega ^{(y)}_J\). For \(k = n\) there is one multi-index, \((1, \ldots , n)\). Conversely, if coefficients satisfying the rule are given on the charts of an atlas, define \(\omega _p = \sum _I\omega ^{(x)}_I(p)\, dx^I|_p\) using any chart at \(p\); the rule says exactly that this does not depend on the chart, and \(\omega \) is smooth by (3).

The exterior algebra of Chapter 12 is an algebra built from one vector space \(V\). A differential form is a family of elements of the exterior algebras \(\Lambda (T_p^*M)\), one for each point \(p\), so the wedge product of forms is defined one point at a time; the work is to check that the result is again smooth and that the algebraic rules survive.

03FM Definition 36.2.7

Let \(\alpha \in \Omega ^k(M)\) and \(\beta \in \Omega ^l(M)\). Their wedge product \(\alpha \wedge \beta \) is the family

\[ (\alpha \wedge \beta )_p = \alpha _p \wedge \beta _p \in \Lambda ^{k+l}T_p^*M, \qquad p \in M, \]

where on the right \(\wedge \) is the product of the exterior algebra \(\Lambda (T_p^*M)\) of the single vector space \(T_p^*M\) (Chapter 12). For \(k = 0\), a \(0\)-form is a function \(f\), and \(f \wedge \beta = f\beta \) is the pointwise product \((f\beta )_p = f(p)\beta _p\). The wedge product is extended to \(\Omega ^\bullet (M) = \bigoplus _k\Omega ^k(M)\) by bilinearity.

03FN Proposition 36.2.8

Let \(\alpha \in \Omega ^k(M)\), \(\beta \in \Omega ^l(M)\) and \(\gamma \in \Omega ^m(M)\).

  1. \(\alpha \wedge \beta \) is smooth, so it is a \((k + l)\)-form. In a chart, if \(\alpha = \sum _I\alpha _I\, dx^I\) and \(\beta = \sum _J\beta _J\, dx^J\), then

    \[ \alpha \wedge \beta = \sum _{I, J}\alpha _I\beta _J\, dx^I \wedge dx^J, \]

    where \(dx^I \wedge dx^J\) is \(0\) if \(I\) and \(J\) have a common index, and otherwise \(\pm dx^K\) with \(K\) the increasing rearrangement of \(I \cup J\) and the sign of the permutation that sorts \((I, J)\).

  2. On tangent vectors, or on vector fields,

    \[ (\alpha \wedge \beta )(X_1, \ldots , X_{k+l}) = \sum _\sigma \operatorname {sgn}(\sigma )\, \alpha (X_{\sigma (1)}, \ldots , X_{\sigma (k)})\, \beta (X_{\sigma (k+1)}, \ldots , X_{\sigma (k+l)}), \]

    the sum over the shuffles \(\sigma \) of Proposition 12.5.4. For \(1\)-forms, \((\alpha \wedge \beta )(X, Y) = \alpha (X)\beta (Y) - \alpha (Y)\beta (X)\).

  3. The product is \(C^\infty (M)\)-bilinear, \((f\alpha ) \wedge \beta = f(\alpha \wedge \beta ) = \alpha \wedge (f\beta )\), associative, \((\alpha \wedge \beta ) \wedge \gamma = \alpha \wedge (\beta \wedge \gamma )\), and graded commutative, \(\beta \wedge \alpha = (-1)^{kl}\alpha \wedge \beta \); in particular \(\alpha \wedge \alpha = 0\) if \(k\) is odd. So \(\Omega ^\bullet (M)\) is a graded-commutative algebra over \(C^\infty (M)\), with unit the constant function \(1\).

  4. For \(f \colon N \to M\) smooth, \(f^*(\alpha \wedge \beta ) = f^*\alpha \wedge f^*\beta \).

Proof â–¼

(1) At a point \(p\) of the chart, \(\alpha _p = \sum _I\alpha _I(p)\, dx^I|_p\) and \(\beta _p = \sum _J\beta _J(p)\, dx^J|_p\), and the product of \(\Lambda (T_p^*M)\) is bilinear, so \((\alpha \wedge \beta )_p = \sum _{I,J}\alpha _I(p)\beta _J(p)\, dx^I|_p \wedge dx^J|_p\). The rule for \(dx^I \wedge dx^J\) is that for wedge products of the basis covectors \(dx^i|_p\) (the paragraph after Definition 36.2.1). So the coefficient of \(dx^K\) in \(\alpha \wedge \beta \) is a sum of terms \(\pm \alpha _I\beta _J\), a smooth function, and \(\alpha \wedge \beta \) is smooth by Proposition 36.2.6(3).

(2) Apply Proposition 12.5.4 with \(V = T_pM\) at each point \(p\), using the identification of \(\Lambda ^kT_p^*M\) with alternating maps (Notation 12.5.2). For \(k = l = 1\) the shuffles are the identity and the transposition of \(1\) and \(2\).

(3) Each identity holds at every point, because it holds in the exterior algebra \(\Lambda (T_p^*M)\): this algebra is associative, as a quotient of the tensor algebra, it is graded commutative by Proposition 12.3.3(1), and \((f\alpha )_p = f(p)\alpha _p\) with \(f(p)\) a scalar, which can be moved out of a bilinear product. If \(k\) is odd, graded commutativity gives \(\alpha \wedge \alpha = -\alpha \wedge \alpha \), so \(2\alpha \wedge \alpha = 0\) and \(\alpha \wedge \alpha = 0\).

(4) By Proposition 36.2.5(2), \((f^*\omega )_p = \Lambda (d_pf)^*(\omega _{f(p)})\) in every degree, where \(\Lambda (d_pf)^* \colon \Lambda (T^*_{f(p)}M) \to \Lambda (T^*_pN)\) is the map induced by the linear map \((d_pf)^*\). It is an algebra homomorphism: it is the extension of \(T^*_{f(p)}M \to T^*_pN \subseteq \Lambda (T^*_pN)\) given by Proposition 12.3.2(3), since it sends a product \(\varphi _1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \varphi _k\) to \((d_pf)^*\varphi _1 \wedge \cdots \wedge (d_pf)^*\varphi _k\). So \((f^*(\alpha \wedge \beta ))_p = \Lambda (d_pf)^*(\alpha _{f(p)} \wedge \beta _{f(p)}) = (f^*\alpha )_p \wedge (f^*\beta )_p\).

03FO Example 36.2.9

  1. On \(\mathbb {R}^3\), \((dx + dy) \wedge (dx - dy) = dx \wedge dx - dx \wedge dy + dy \wedge dx - dy \wedge dy = -2\, dx \wedge dy\). On the vectors \(e_1, e_2\) the formula of (2) gives \((dx + dy)(e_1)(dx - dy)(e_2) - (dx + dy)(e_2)(dx - dy)(e_1) = (1)(-1) - (1)(1) = -2\), in accordance.

  2. The functions are part of the algebra: \((x^2\, dy) \wedge (e^x\, dz) = x^2e^x\, dy \wedge dz\), by \(C^\infty \)-bilinearity.

  3. For even degree, \(\alpha \wedge \alpha \) need not vanish. On \(\mathbb {R}^4\) with \(\omega = dx^1 \wedge dx^2 + dx^3 \wedge dx^4\),

    \[ \omega \wedge \omega = dx^1 \wedge dx^2 \wedge dx^3 \wedge dx^4 + dx^3 \wedge dx^4 \wedge dx^1 \wedge dx^2 = 2\, dx^1 \wedge dx^2 \wedge dx^3 \wedge dx^4, \]

    since moving the \(2\)-form \(dx^3 \wedge dx^4\) past \(dx^1 \wedge dx^2\) costs the sign \((-1)^{2 \cdot 2} = 1\), and the squares \((dx^1 \wedge dx^2)^2\) and \((dx^3 \wedge dx^4)^2\) vanish. This is the standard symplectic form of \(\mathbb {R}^4\), and \(\omega \wedge \omega \neq 0\) is its nondegeneracy (Chapter 41).

Two operations are used constantly: the wedge product of forms, computed pointwise with the rules of the exterior algebra (\(dx^i \wedge dx^j = -dx^j \wedge dx^i\), so \(dx^i \wedge dx^i = 0\)), and the pullback, computed by substituting: for \(f \colon N \to M\), \(f^*(\sum _I\omega _I\, dx^{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge dx^{i_k}) = \sum _I(\omega _I \circ f)\, d(x^{i_1} \circ f) \wedge \cdots \wedge d(x^{i_k} \circ f)\), since \(f^*\) is an algebra homomorphism with \(f^*(dg) = d(g \circ f)\) for functions (the chain rule).

02MA Example 36.2.10

  1. A wedge product. On \(\mathbb {R}^3\), for \(\alpha = x\, dx + y\, dy\) and \(\beta = dx + z\, dz\),

    \[ \alpha \wedge \beta = x\, dx \wedge dx + xz\, dx \wedge dz + y\, dy \wedge dx + yz\, dy \wedge dz = -y\, dx \wedge dy + xz\, dx \wedge dz + yz\, dy \wedge dz . \]

    On the vectors \(e_1, e_2\) it takes the value \(\alpha (e_1)\beta (e_2) - \alpha (e_2)\beta (e_1) = x \cdot 0 - y \cdot 1 = -y\), in accordance with the coefficient of \(dx \wedge dy\).

  2. Pullback to a curve. For the helix \(\gamma (t) = (\cos t, \sin t, t)\) and \(\omega = z\, dx + x\, dy\), \(\gamma ^*\omega = t\, d(\cos t) + \cos t\, d(\sin t) = (\cos ^2t - t\sin t)\, dt\).

  3. Pullback to the sphere. Let \(x(\vartheta , \varphi ) = (\sin \vartheta \cos \varphi , \sin \vartheta \sin \varphi , \cos \vartheta )\) and \(\omega = x\, dy \wedge dz + y\, dz \wedge dx + z\, dx \wedge dy\). By Proposition 36.2.6, the pullback of \(dy \wedge dz\) is the Jacobian minor \(\partial (y, z)/\partial (\vartheta , \varphi )\) times \(d\vartheta \wedge d\varphi \), and similarly for the others:

    \[ x^*(dy \wedge dz) = \sin ^2\vartheta \cos \varphi \, d\vartheta \wedge d\varphi , \quad x^*(dz \wedge dx) = \sin ^2\vartheta \sin \varphi \, d\vartheta \wedge d\varphi , \quad x^*(dx \wedge dy) = \sin \vartheta \cos \vartheta \, d\vartheta \wedge d\varphi . \]

    So \(x^*\omega = (\sin ^3\vartheta \cos ^2\varphi + \sin ^3\vartheta \sin ^2\varphi + \sin \vartheta \cos ^2\vartheta )\, d\vartheta \wedge d\varphi = \sin \vartheta \, d\vartheta \wedge d\varphi \), the area form of the unit sphere in spherical coordinates.

  4. Spherical coordinates in space. For \((r, \vartheta , \varphi ) \mapsto r\, x(\vartheta , \varphi )\), the pullback of \(dx \wedge dy \wedge dz\) is the Jacobian determinant times \(dr \wedge d\vartheta \wedge d\varphi \), namely \(r^2\sin \vartheta \, dr \wedge d\vartheta \wedge d\varphi \) (Exercise 36.5.6).

02I7 Example 36.2.11

In the polar coordinates \((r, \theta )\) of Example 33.4.8, with \(x = r\cos \theta \) and \(y = r\sin \theta \), the rule of Proposition 36.2.6 for \(k = 1\) gives \(dx = \cos \theta \, dr - r\sin \theta \, d\theta \) and \(dy = \sin \theta \, dr + r\cos \theta \, d\theta \). Solving,

\[ dr = \cos \theta \, dx + \sin \theta \, dy = \frac{x\, dx + y\, dy}{r}, \qquad d\theta = \frac{-\sin \theta \, dx + \cos \theta \, dy}{r} = \frac{x\, dy - y\, dx}{x^2 + y^2}, \]

the second being the angle form of Counterexample 36.2.22. Compare with the tangent vectors \(\partial /\partial r = \cos \theta \, \partial /\partial x + \sin \theta \, \partial /\partial y\) and \(\partial /\partial \theta = -r\sin \theta \, \partial /\partial x + r\cos \theta \, \partial /\partial y\). The formula \(dr = \cos \theta \, dx + \sin \theta \, dy\) found in many texts is about the covector \(dr\), not the vector \(\partial /\partial r\): vectors change by the Jacobian matrix \(P = \partial (x, y)/\partial (r, \theta )\) (its columns give \(\partial /\partial r\) and \(\partial /\partial \theta \)), and covectors by its inverse (the rows of \(P^{-1}\) give \(dr\) and \(d\theta \)). The coefficients agree for \(r\) and not for \(\theta \) because \(P\) is a rotation followed by \(\operatorname {diag}(1, r)\): \(\partial /\partial r\) is a unit vector orthogonal to the circles \(r = \text{const}\), so the Euclidean metric identifies it with \(dr\), while \(\partial /\partial \theta \) has length \(r\) and corresponds to \(r^2\, d\theta \). For \(2\)-forms the rule gives

\[ dx \wedge dy = \det \frac{\partial (x, y)}{\partial (r, \theta )}\, dr \wedge d\theta = r\, dr \wedge d\theta , \]

which is the change of variables \(\int f\, dx\, dy = \int f\, r\, dr\, d\theta \) of Example 28.11.3, in the language of forms (Proposition 36.4.2).

019S Theorem 36.2.12

There is a unique family of linear maps \(d \colon \Omega ^k(M) \to \Omega ^{k+1}(M)\), for all \(M\) and \(k\), such that: \(df\) is the differential of \(f\) for \(f \in \Omega ^0(M) = C^\infty (M)\); \(d(\alpha \wedge \beta ) = d\alpha \wedge \beta + (-1)^{|\alpha |}\alpha \wedge d\beta \); and \(d \circ d = 0\). It is local, given in a chart by \(d(\sum _I\omega _I\, dx^I) = \sum _I d\omega _I \wedge dx^I\), and natural: \(f^*(d\omega ) = d(f^*\omega )\). Moreover, for vector fields,

\[ d\omega (X_0, \ldots , X_k) = \sum _i(-1)^i X_i\left(\omega (X_0, \ldots , \widehat{X_i}, \ldots , X_k)\right) + \sum _{i{\lt}j}(-1)^{i+j}\omega ([X_i, X_j], X_0, \ldots , \widehat{X_i}, \ldots , \widehat{X_j}, \ldots , X_k). \]
Proof â–¼

We write \(\partial _l = \partial /\partial x^l\) in a chart, and \(J \setminus j_m\) for the multi-index \(J = (j_0 {\lt} \cdots {\lt} j_k)\) with \(j_m\) removed.

Step 1: the formula in a chart and its coefficients. On a chart domain \(U\) define

\[ d_U\Bigl(\sum _I\omega _I\, dx^I\Bigr) = \sum _Id\omega _I \wedge dx^I = \sum _I\sum _{l=1}^n\partial _l\omega _I\, dx^l \wedge dx^I. \]

Then \(d_U(f\, dx^{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge dx^{i_k}) = df \wedge dx^{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge dx^{i_k}\) also for sequences that are not increasing, since both sides vanish for repeated indices and change by the same sign when the indices are sorted. We compute the coefficients of \(d_U\omega \): for \(J = (j_0 {\lt} \cdots {\lt} j_k)\),

\[ (d_U\omega )(\partial _{j_0}, \ldots , \partial _{j_k}) = \sum _{m=0}^k(-1)^m\, \partial _{j_m}\omega _{J \setminus j_m}. \]

Indeed the form \(dx^l \wedge dx^I\) vanishes on \((\partial _{j_0}, \ldots , \partial _{j_k})\) unless \(l \notin I\) and \(\{ l\} \cup I = J\) as sets, that is, unless \(l = j_m\) and \(I = J \setminus j_m\) for some \(m\); and then \(dx^{j_m} \wedge dx^{J \setminus j_m} = (-1)^m\, dx^J\), moving \(dx^{j_m}\) past the \(m\) forms \(dx^{j_0}, \ldots , dx^{j_{m-1}}\), which takes the value \((-1)^m\). Conversely, collecting the terms, \(\sum _J\bigl(\sum _m(-1)^m\partial _{j_m}\omega _{J \setminus j_m}\bigr)dx^J = \sum _I\sum _l\partial _l\omega _I\, dx^l \wedge dx^I\).

Step 2: the three rules hold in a chart. For a function, \(d_Uf = \sum _l\partial _lf\, dx^l\) is the differential \(df\), by the dual basis property. For the Leibniz rule it suffices by linearity to take \(\alpha = f\, dx^I\) with \(|I| = k\) and \(\beta = g\, dx^J\); then \(\alpha \wedge \beta = fg\, dx^I \wedge dx^J\) and

\[ d_U(\alpha \wedge \beta ) = (g\, df + f\, dg) \wedge dx^I \wedge dx^J = (df \wedge dx^I) \wedge (g\, dx^J) + (-1)^k(f\, dx^I) \wedge (dg \wedge dx^J), \]

moving the \(1\)-form \(dg\) past the \(k\)-form \(dx^I\). Finally \(d_Ud_U(f\, dx^I) = \sum _{m,l}\partial _m\partial _lf\, dx^m \wedge dx^l \wedge dx^I = 0\): the terms \((m, l)\) and \((l, m)\) cancel because \(\partial _m\partial _lf = \partial _l\partial _mf\) (Theorem 27.10.5) while \(dx^m \wedge dx^l = -dx^l \wedge dx^m\), and the terms with \(m = l\) vanish.

Step 3: uniqueness and locality. Let \(D\) be any family of linear maps with the three properties. If \(\omega \) vanishes on an open set \(U \ni p\), choose a bump function \(\chi \) with support in \(U\) and \(\chi (p) = 1\) (Lemma 33.3.11); then \(\chi \omega = 0\), so \(0 = D(\chi \omega ) = d\chi \wedge \omega + \chi \, D\omega \), and at \(p\) this gives \((D\omega )_p = 0\). So \(D\omega \) near \(p\) depends only on \(\omega \) near \(p\). Now let \(p\) lie in a chart domain \(U\), choose bump functions \(\chi \) and \(\tilde\chi \) with support in \(U\), with \(\chi = 1\) near \(p\) and \(\tilde\chi = 1\) near the support of \(\chi \) (Corollary 33.8.3(1), applied to a compact neighbourhood of \(\operatorname {supp}\chi \) in \(U\)). Then \(\chi \omega = \sum _I(\chi \omega _I)\, d(\tilde\chi x^{i_1}) \wedge \cdots \wedge d(\tilde\chi x^{i_k})\) is a form on \(M\) built from global functions, and the Leibniz rule and \(D \circ D = 0\) give \(D(\chi \omega ) = \sum _Id(\chi \omega _I) \wedge d(\tilde\chi x^{i_1}) \wedge \cdots \wedge d(\tilde\chi x^{i_k})\), since \(D(d(\tilde\chi x^i)) = DD(\tilde\chi x^i) = 0\). Near \(p\) this is \(\sum _Id\omega _I \wedge dx^I\), and \(D\omega = D(\chi \omega )\) near \(p\) by locality. So \(D\) is given by the coordinate formula in every chart, and is unique.

Step 4: existence, and what happens on the intersection of two charts. Define \(d\omega \) on each chart domain \(U\) by \(d_U\). On the intersection of two chart domains \(U\) and \(V\), the operator \(d_V\) satisfies the three rules (Step 2, on the manifold \(U \cap V\)), so, writing \(\omega = \sum _I\omega _I\, dx^I\) in the coordinates of \(U\) and using \(d_V(dx^i) = d_V(d_Vx^i) = 0\) and the Leibniz rule,

\[ d_V\Bigl(\sum _I\omega _I\, dx^I\Bigr) = \sum _Id_V\omega _I \wedge dx^I = \sum _Id\omega _I \wedge dx^I = d_U\Bigl(\sum _I\omega _I\, dx^I\Bigr). \]

So the local definitions agree on overlaps and glue to an operator \(d\) on \(M\), which satisfies the three rules because they are local.

Step 5: naturality. For a function \(g\) on \(M\), \(f^*(dg) = d(g \circ f)\) by the chain rule. Locally \(\omega = \sum _I\omega _I\, dx^{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge dx^{i_k}\), so \(f^*\omega = \sum _I(\omega _I \circ f)\, d(x^{i_1} \circ f) \wedge \cdots \wedge d(x^{i_k} \circ f)\), and by the Leibniz rule and \(d \circ d = 0\) on \(N\), \(d(f^*\omega ) = \sum _Id(\omega _I \circ f) \wedge d(x^{i_1} \circ f) \wedge \cdots \wedge d(x^{i_k} \circ f) = f^*\bigl(\sum _Id\omega _I \wedge dx^I\bigr) = f^*(d\omega )\).

Step 6: the invariant formula. Call the right-hand side \(T(X_0, \ldots , X_k)\). It is local in the \(X_i\). It is \(C^\infty (M)\)-linear in \(X_0\): replacing \(X_0\) by \(fX_0\), the term \(i = 0\) becomes \(f\) times itself; the terms \(i \geq 1\) acquire the extra summands \((-1)^i(X_if)\, \omega (X_0, \ldots , \widehat{X_i}, \ldots , X_k)\); and the bracket terms with \(i = 0\) acquire, since \([fX_0, X_j] = f[X_0, X_j] - (X_jf)X_0\), the extra summands \(-(-1)^j(X_jf)\, \omega (X_0, X_1, \ldots , \widehat{X_j}, \ldots , X_k)\), which cancel the previous ones. A similar check shows that \(T\) changes sign when two arguments are exchanged, so it is \(C^\infty (M)\)-linear in every argument, like \(d\omega \). Two such expressions agree if they agree on coordinate fields, and by alternation it suffices to take \(\partial _{j_0}, \ldots , \partial _{j_k}\) with \(j_0 {\lt} \cdots {\lt} j_k\). For coordinate fields all brackets vanish, and \(T(\partial _{j_0}, \ldots , \partial _{j_k}) = \sum _m(-1)^m\partial _{j_m}\omega _{J \setminus j_m}\), which is \(d\omega (\partial _{j_0}, \ldots , \partial _{j_k})\) by Step 1.

02T0 Remark 36.2.13

For a function \(f\), \(df = \sum _j(\partial f/\partial x^j)\, dx^j\) (Remark 36.2.2) is a \(1\)-form, a section of the cotangent bundle \(T^*M\); it should not be confused with the tangent map \(Tf \colon TM \to T\mathbb {R}\), although at each point both are the linear map \(v \mapsto v(f)\). The identity \(d(df) = 0\) is, in coordinates, the symmetry of second derivatives:

\[ d(df) = \sum _j d\Bigl(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x^j}\Bigr) \wedge dx^j = \sum _{i,j}\frac{\partial ^2f}{\partial x^i\partial x^j}\, dx^i \wedge dx^j = \sum _{i{\lt}j}\Bigl(\frac{\partial ^2f}{\partial x^i\partial x^j} - \frac{\partial ^2f}{\partial x^j\partial x^i}\Bigr)dx^i \wedge dx^j = 0 \]

(Theorem 27.10.5). So a \(1\)-form \(\omega = \sum _j\omega _j\, dx^j\) can be exact only if \(\partial _i\omega _j = \partial _j\omega _i\); this necessary condition is \(d\omega = 0\), and whether it is sufficient is the question answered by de Rham cohomology (Chapter 37).

02I8 Example 36.2.14

For \(k = 1\) the invariant formula reads \(d\omega (X, Y) = X\omega (Y) - Y\omega (X) - \omega ([X, Y])\), and in coordinates on \(\mathbb {R}^2\), for \(\omega = P\, dx + Q\, dy\), \(d\omega = (\partial _xQ - \partial _yP)\, dx \wedge dy\): Step 1 with \(J = (1, 2)\) gives the coefficient \(\partial _1\omega _2 - \partial _2\omega _1\). This is the integrand of Green’s theorem, which is Stokes’ theorem (Theorem 36.4.8) for a domain in the plane.

019T Definition 36.2.15

For \(X \in \mathfrak {X}(M)\) the interior product \(\iota _X \colon \Omega ^k \to \Omega ^{k-1}\) is \((\iota _X\omega )(X_1, \ldots , X_{k-1}) = \omega (X, X_1, \ldots , X_{k-1})\) (Definition 12.5.5), and the Lie derivative is \(L_X\omega = \frac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0}\Phi _t^*\omega \), where \(\Phi \) is the flow of \(X\).

The interior product inserts \(X\) as the first argument; on a function it is \(0\), and on a \(1\)-form \(\iota _X\alpha = \alpha (X)\). By Proposition 12.5.6 it is an antiderivation: \(\iota _X(\alpha \wedge \beta ) = \iota _X\alpha \wedge \beta + (-1)^k\alpha \wedge \iota _X\beta \) for a \(k\)-form \(\alpha \). For instance \(\iota _{\partial _x}(dx \wedge dy) = dy\) and \(\iota _{\partial _y}(dx \wedge dy) = -dx\).

The Lie derivative measures how \(\omega \) changes along the flow of \(X\). The flow is in general only defined for small \(|t|\) near each point (Theorem 33.9.2), but this suffices: \((\Phi _t^*\omega )_p\) involves only the values of \(\omega \) near \(\Phi _t(p)\), it is defined for \(|t|\) small, and it depends smoothly on \((t, p)\), as its coefficients in a chart are built from the coefficients of \(\omega \) and the derivatives of the flow. So \(L_X\omega \) is again a \(k\)-form. On a function, \(L_Xf = \frac{d}{dt}f(\Phi _t(p))|_{t=0} = Xf\).

02MB Example 36.2.16

On \(\mathbb {R}^2\) let \(X = x\, \partial _x + y\, \partial _y\), whose flow is \(\Phi _t(p) = e^tp\). Then \(\Phi _t^*(dx \wedge dy) = d(e^tx) \wedge d(e^ty) = e^{2t}dx \wedge dy\), so \(L_X(dx \wedge dy) = 2\, dx \wedge dy\): the flow expands areas at the rate \(2\). For the rotation field \(Y = -y\, \partial _x + x\, \partial _y\), the flow consists of rotations, which preserve \(dx \wedge dy\), so \(L_Y(dx \wedge dy) = 0\). Cartan’s formula below gives the same results by pure algebra: \(\iota _X(dx \wedge dy) = x\, dy - y\, dx\), whose differential is \(2\, dx \wedge dy\), and \(\iota _Y(dx \wedge dy) = -y\, dy - x\, dx = -d(\frac12(x^2 + y^2))\), whose differential is \(0\).

019U Proposition 36.2.17 Cartan’s formulas

\(L_X = d\iota _X + \iota _Xd\) on forms; \(L_X\) commutes with \(d\); \(\frac{d}{dt}\Phi _t^*\omega = \Phi _t^*L_X\omega \); and \(L_{[X,Y]} = L_XL_Y - L_YL_X\).

Proof â–¼

Step 1: \(L_X\) is a local derivation of degree \(0\) commuting with \(d\). Since \(\Phi _t^*(\alpha \wedge \beta ) = \Phi _t^*\alpha \wedge \Phi _t^*\beta \), differentiating at \(t = 0\) (the product rule, applied pointwise to the coefficients) gives \(L_X(\alpha \wedge \beta ) = L_X\alpha \wedge \beta + \alpha \wedge L_X\beta \). Since \(\Phi _t^*d = d\Phi _t^*\) (naturality of \(d\), Theorem 36.2.12) and derivatives in \(t\) and in the coordinates commute (the coefficients are smooth in \((t, p)\)), \(L_Xd = dL_X\). It is local, as noted above.

Step 2: \(D = d\iota _X + \iota _Xd\) is a local derivation of degree \(0\) commuting with \(d\). For a \(k\)-form \(\alpha \), using the antiderivation rules for \(d\) and for \(\iota _X\),

\[ \begin{aligned} d\iota _X(\alpha \wedge \beta ) & = d\iota _X\alpha \wedge \beta + (-1)^{k-1}\iota _X\alpha \wedge d\beta + (-1)^kd\alpha \wedge \iota _X\beta + \alpha \wedge d\iota _X\beta ,\\ \iota _Xd(\alpha \wedge \beta ) & = \iota _Xd\alpha \wedge \beta + (-1)^{k+1}d\alpha \wedge \iota _X\beta + (-1)^k\iota _X\alpha \wedge d\beta + \alpha \wedge \iota _Xd\beta , \end{aligned} \]

and adding, the middle terms cancel: \(D(\alpha \wedge \beta ) = D\alpha \wedge \beta + \alpha \wedge D\beta \). Moreover \(dD = d\iota _Xd = Dd\), since \(d \circ d = 0\).

Step 3: \(L_X = D\). On a function, \(L_Xf = Xf = df(X) = \iota _Xdf = Df\), as \(\iota _Xf = 0\). On an exact \(1\)-form \(df\), both give \(d(Xf)\), since both commute with \(d\). Locally every form is a sum of forms \(f\, dg_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge dg_k\) (in a chart, \(\omega _I\, dx^{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge dx^{i_k}\)), and two local derivations of degree \(0\) that agree on functions and on exact \(1\)-forms agree on such products, hence everywhere.

Step 4: the derivative at time \(t\). By the group law, near a point and for small \(s\), \(\Phi _{t+s} = \Phi _s \circ \Phi _t\), so \(\Phi _{t+s}^*\omega = \Phi _t^*(\Phi _s^*\omega )\), and differentiating at \(s = 0\) gives \(\frac{d}{dt}\Phi _t^*\omega = \Phi _t^*L_X\omega \) (\(\Phi _t^*\) is linear and acts pointwise, so it commutes with \(\frac{d}{ds}\)).

Step 5: brackets. \(L_XL_Y - L_YL_X\) is again a local derivation of degree \(0\) commuting with \(d\), and on functions it is \(XY f - YXf = [X, Y]f = L_{[X,Y]}f\). As in Step 3 the two derivations agree everywhere.

By Step 4, the flow of \(X\) preserves a form \(\omega \), that is \(\Phi _t^*\omega = \omega \) for all \(t\), if and only if \(L_X\omega = 0\). For example, the flow of a vector field on \(\mathbb {R}^n\) preserves the volume form \(dx^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge dx^n\) exactly when \(\operatorname {div}X = \sum _i\partial _iX^i = 0\) (Exercise 36.5.3); in dimension \(2\) the Hamiltonian vector fields are such (Exercise 36.5.9).

029P Example 36.2.18

On \(\mathbb {R}^3\) the Euclidean metric and orientation identify \(1\)-forms and \(2\)-forms with vector fields, by \(F = (F_1, F_2, F_3) \mapsto F_1\, dx + F_2\, dy + F_3\, dz\) and \(F \mapsto F_1\, dy \wedge dz + F_2\, dz \wedge dx + F_3\, dx \wedge dy\), and \(3\)-forms with functions. Under these identifications \(d\) on \(0\)-, \(1\)- and \(2\)-forms is the gradient, the curl and the divergence, and \(d \circ d = 0\) becomes \(\operatorname {curl}\operatorname {grad} = 0\) and \(\operatorname {div}\operatorname {curl} = 0\). Indeed \(df = f_x\, dx + f_y\, dy + f_z\, dz\); for a \(1\)-form,

\[ d(F_1\, dx + F_2\, dy + F_3\, dz) = (\partial _yF_3 - \partial _zF_2)\, dy \wedge dz + (\partial _zF_1 - \partial _xF_3)\, dz \wedge dx + (\partial _xF_2 - \partial _yF_1)\, dx \wedge dy, \]

since, for instance, \(dF_1 \wedge dx = \partial _yF_1\, dy \wedge dx + \partial _zF_1\, dz \wedge dx\); and for a \(2\)-form, \(d(F_1\, dy \wedge dz + F_2\, dz \wedge dx + F_3\, dx \wedge dy) = (\partial _xF_1 + \partial _yF_2 + \partial _zF_3)\, dx \wedge dy \wedge dz\), because only the derivative in the missing variable survives in each term.

029Q Definition 36.2.19

A form \(\omega \) is closed if \(d\omega = 0\) and exact if \(\omega = d\eta \) for some form \(\eta \). Exact forms are closed, since \(d \circ d = 0\).

029R Definition 36.2.20

For a \(1\)-form \(\alpha \) on \(M\) and a piecewise smooth curve \(\gamma \colon [a, b] \to M\), the line integral is \(\int _\gamma \alpha = \int _a^b\alpha _{\gamma (t)}(\dot\gamma (t))\, dt\), the sum of the integrals of \(\gamma ^*\alpha \) over the smooth pieces. For \(M\) open in \(\mathbb {R}^n\) and \(\alpha = \sum _jF_j\, dx_j\) this is the line integral of the second kind of the vector field \(F = (F_1, \ldots , F_n)\) (Definition 27.11.12).

029S Proposition 36.2.21

  1. The line integral is unchanged under orientation-preserving reparametrisation \(\gamma \circ \phi \), \(\phi \colon [c, d] \to [a, b]\) increasing and piecewise smooth with \(\phi (c) = a\), \(\phi (d) = b\), and changes sign under reversal.

  2. (Fundamental theorem for line integrals) \(\int _\gamma df = f(\gamma (b)) - f(\gamma (a))\).

  3. On a connected manifold, a \(1\)-form \(\alpha \) is exact if and only if \(\int _\gamma \alpha = 0\) for every closed piecewise smooth curve \(\gamma \).

Proof â–¼

(1) is the substitution rule (Corollary 27.8.4) on each piece, as \((\gamma \circ \phi )^*\alpha = \phi ^*\gamma ^*\alpha \). (2) \(\gamma ^*df = d(f \circ \gamma ) = (f \circ \gamma )'\, dt\), and Theorem 27.8.3 applies on each piece; the intermediate values cancel. (3) If \(\alpha = df\), closed curves give \(0\) by (2). Conversely, fix \(p_0\) and define \(f(p) = \int _\gamma \alpha \) for any piecewise smooth curve \(\gamma \) from \(p_0\) to \(p\); such curves exist since \(M\) is connected and locally path-connected by piecewise smooth curves (use straight segments in charts), and the value does not depend on \(\gamma \), because for two curves \(\gamma \), \(\gamma '\) the concatenation of \(\gamma \) with the reverse of \(\gamma '\) is closed. In a chart around \(p\), with \(p\) at the origin, computing \(f\) along \(\gamma \) followed by the segment from \(p\) to \(p + se_i\) gives \(f(p + se_i) - f(p) = \int _0^s\alpha _i(p + ue_i)\, du\), so \(\partial _if = \alpha _i\) by Theorem 27.8.3; hence \(f\) is smooth and \(df = \alpha \).

029T Counterexample 36.2.22

Closed forms need not be exact. On \(M = \mathbb {R}^2 \setminus \{ 0\} \), the angle form \(\alpha = (x\, dy - y\, dx)/(x^2 + y^2)\) is closed, as a direct computation shows, but along the unit circle \(\gamma (t) = (\cos t, \sin t)\), \(\gamma ^*\alpha = (\cos ^2t + \sin ^2t)\, dt = dt\), so \(\int _\gamma \alpha = 2\pi \neq 0\), and \(\alpha \) is not exact by Proposition 36.2.21(3). On the half-plane \(\{ x {\gt} 0\} \) it is exact, equal to \(d\arctan (y/x)\): exactness is a global question, measured by de Rham cohomology (Chapter 37).
