33.3 The exponential map018N

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

A one-parameter subgroup of \(G\) is a homomorphism of Lie groups \(\mathbb {R}\to G\). For matrix groups these are given by the exponential series, which we treat first.

02F3 Lemma 33.3.1

For \(X \in M_n(\mathbb {K})\) the series \(e^X = \sum _{k \geq 0}X^k/k!\) converges absolutely, and:

  1. if \(XY = YX\), then \(e^{X+Y} = e^Xe^Y\); in particular \(e^X\) is invertible with inverse \(e^{-X}\);

  2. \(t \mapsto e^{tX}\) is a one-parameter subgroup \(\mathbb {R}\to \mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb {K})\), with derivative \(\frac{d}{dt}e^{tX} = Xe^{tX} = e^{tX}X\);

  3. \(e^{AXA^{-1}} = Ae^XA^{-1}\) for \(A\) invertible, \((e^X)^T = e^{X^T}\) and \((e^X)^* = e^{X^*}\);

  4. \(\det e^X = e^{\operatorname {tr}X}\).

Proof â–¼

Since \(\| X^k/k!\| \leq \| X\| ^k/k!\) and \(\sum _k\| X\| ^k/k! = e^{\| X\| }\), the series converges absolutely in the complete space \(M_n(\mathbb {K})\) (Proposition 26.5.4).

(1) As \(X\) and \(Y\) commute, \((X + Y)^k = \sum _{i+j=k}\frac{k!}{i!j!}X^iY^j\). So, with \(a = \| X\| \) and \(b = \| Y\| \),

\[ \Bigl\| \sum _{k \leq N}\frac{(X + Y)^k}{k!} - \sum _{i \leq N}\frac{X^i}{i!}\sum _{j \leq N}\frac{Y^j}{j!}\Bigr\| = \Bigl\| \sum _{\substack {i, j \leq N\\ i + j {\gt} N}}\frac{X^iY^j}{i!j!}\Bigr\| \leq \sum _{i + j {\gt} N}\frac{a^ib^j}{i!j!} = e^{a+b} - \sum _{k \leq N}\frac{(a + b)^k}{k!}, \]

which tends to \(0\) as \(N \to \infty \). Hence \(e^{X+Y} = e^Xe^Y\). Taking \(Y = -X\) gives \(e^Xe^{-X} = e^0 = I\).

(2) By (1), \(e^{sX}e^{tX} = e^{(s+t)X}\). Each entry of \(e^{tX}\) is a power series \(\sum _k(X^k)_{ij}t^k/k!\) in \(t\) with infinite radius of convergence, so it is smooth, and differentiating term by term (Theorem 26.6.19) gives \(\sum _{k \geq 1}t^{k-1}X^k/(k - 1)! = Xe^{tX}\), which also equals \(e^{tX}X\).

(3) \((AXA^{-1})^k = AX^kA^{-1}\), and transposition and conjugate transposition are continuous and linear, with \((X^k)^T = (X^T)^k\); sum the series.

(4) Let \(f(t) = \det e^{tX}\). By (2), \(f(t + h) = f(t)\det (e^{hX})\), and \(e^{hX} = I + hX + O(h^2)\), so expanding the determinant as a sum of products of entries, \(\det e^{hX} = 1 + h\operatorname {tr}X + O(h^2)\). Hence \(f'(t) = f(t)\operatorname {tr}X\) and \(f(0) = 1\), so \((f(t)e^{-t\operatorname {tr}X})' = 0\) and \(f(t) = e^{t\operatorname {tr}X}\). Take \(t = 1\).

02F4 Example 33.3.2

  1. For a diagonal matrix, \(e^{\operatorname {diag}(\lambda _1, \ldots , \lambda _n)} = \operatorname {diag}(e^{\lambda _1}, \ldots , e^{\lambda _n})\).

  2. For \(N = \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\), \(N^2 = 0\), so \(e^{tN} = I + tN = \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & t \\ 0 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\).

  3. For \(J = \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\), \(J^2 = -I\), so separating even and odd powers, \(e^{tJ} = \cos t\, I + \sin t\, J = \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} \cos t & -\sin t \\ \sin t & \cos t \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\), the rotation by \(t\). Similarly \(e^{tL_3}\), with \(L_3\) as in Example 33.2.10, is the rotation by the angle \(t\) about the \(e_3\)-axis.

  4. The exponential maps each Lie algebra of Example 33.2.9 into the corresponding group. If \(X^T = -X\), then \(X\) and \(X^T\) commute, so \((e^X)^Te^X = e^{X^T + X} = I\), and \(\det e^X = e^{\operatorname {tr}X} = e^0 = 1\): so \(e^X \in \mathrm{SO}(n)\). In the same way \(e^X \in \mathrm{U}(n)\) for skew-Hermitian \(X\), \(e^X \in \mathrm{SL}_n\) for \(\operatorname {tr}X = 0\), and \(e^X \in \mathrm{SU}(n)\) for \(X \in \mathfrak {su}(n)\).

For a general Lie group we use the flows of left-invariant vector fields (Theorem 32.9.2) in place of the series.

018O Theorem 33.3.3

For \(X \in \mathfrak {g}\) the left-invariant field \(X\) is complete, and its flow through \(e\) is a homomorphism \(\gamma _X \colon \mathbb {R}\to G\); conversely every homomorphism \(\mathbb {R}\to G\) arises this way. Setting \(\exp (X) = \gamma _X(1)\) defines a smooth map \(\exp \colon \mathfrak {g} \to G\) with \(d_0\exp = \mathrm{id}\), so \(\exp \) is a diffeomorphism from a neighbourhood of \(0\) onto a neighbourhood of \(e\). It satisfies \(\exp ((s + t)X) = \exp (sX)\exp (tX)\), \(\exp (-X) = \exp (X)^{-1}\), and \(\varphi (\exp X) = \exp (d_e\varphi (X))\) for every homomorphism \(\varphi \). For matrix groups, \(\exp (X) = \sum _kX^k/k!\).

Proof â–¼

Write \(\widetilde{X}\) for the left-invariant field with value \(X\) at \(e\).

Step 1: left translates of integral curves are integral curves. If \(\gamma \) is an integral curve of \(\widetilde{X}\), so is \(L_g \circ \gamma \), since \((L_g \circ \gamma )'(t) = d(L_g)(\widetilde{X}_{\gamma (t)}) = \widetilde{X}_{g\gamma (t)}\) by left-invariance.

Step 2: completeness. By Theorem 32.9.2, the integral curve \(\gamma \) with \(\gamma (0) = e\) is defined on some \((-\varepsilon , \varepsilon )\). By Step 1, \(t \mapsto g\gamma (t)\) is the integral curve through \(g\), so every maximal integral curve is defined at least on \((-\varepsilon , \varepsilon )\). If the maximal integral curve \(\gamma _g\) through \(g\) were defined only up to \(\beta {\lt} \infty \), then for \(s \in (\beta - \varepsilon , \beta )\) the integral curve through \(\gamma _g(s)\), defined on \((-\varepsilon , \varepsilon )\), would extend \(\gamma _g\) beyond \(\beta \) (by the group law of the flow), a contradiction. So all integral curves are defined on \(\mathbb {R}\); let \(\gamma _X\) be the one with \(\gamma _X(0) = e\).

Step 3: \(\gamma _X\) is a homomorphism. For fixed \(s\), the curves \(t \mapsto \gamma _X(s)\gamma _X(t)\) (Step 1) and \(t \mapsto \gamma _X(s + t)\) are integral curves with the same value \(\gamma _X(s)\) at \(t = 0\), so they coincide by uniqueness of integral curves.

Step 4: every one-parameter subgroup is some \(\gamma _X\). Let \(\gamma \colon \mathbb {R}\to G\) be a homomorphism of Lie groups and \(X = \gamma '(0)\). Since \(\gamma (t + h) = L_{\gamma (t)}(\gamma (h))\), differentiating in \(h\) at \(h = 0\) gives \(\gamma '(t) = d(L_{\gamma (t)})(X) = \widetilde{X}_{\gamma (t)}\). So \(\gamma \) is the integral curve of \(\widetilde{X}\) through \(e\), that is, \(\gamma = \gamma _X\).

Step 5: smoothness of \(\exp \). Choose a basis \(X_1, \ldots , X_n\) of \(\mathfrak {g}\), and consider on \(G \times \mathfrak {g}\) the vector field \(Z_{(g, \sum _ia^iX_i)} = (\sum _ia^i(\widetilde{X_i})_g, 0)\). In product charts its components are the \(a^i\) times the components of the smooth fields \(\widetilde{X_i}\), so \(Z\) is smooth. Its integral curve through \((e, X)\) is \(t \mapsto (\gamma _X(t), X)\), and more generally that through \((g, X)\) is \(t \mapsto (g\gamma _X(t), X)\), defined for all \(t\). By Theorem 32.9.2 its flow \(\Phi \) is smooth on \(\mathbb {R}\times G \times \mathfrak {g}\), so \((t, X) \mapsto \gamma _X(t)\), the first component of \(\Phi (t, (e, X))\), is smooth, and in particular \(\exp (X) = \gamma _X(1)\) is smooth.

Step 6: the group laws. For fixed \(s\), \(t \mapsto \gamma _X(st)\) is a one-parameter subgroup with velocity \(sX\) at \(0\), so it equals \(\gamma _{sX}\) by Step 4. Hence \(\exp (sX) = \gamma _X(s)\), and Step 3 gives \(\exp ((s + t)X) = \exp (sX)\exp (tX)\) and, with \(t = -s = -1\), \(\exp (-X) = \exp (X)^{-1}\).

Step 7: the differential at \(0\). Identifying \(T_0\mathfrak {g}\) with \(\mathfrak {g}\), the vector \(X\) is the velocity of \(t \mapsto tX\), so \(d_0\exp (X) = \frac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0}\exp (tX) = \gamma _X'(0) = X\). As \(d_0\exp = \mathrm{id}\) is an isomorphism, \(\exp \) is a diffeomorphism near \(0\) (Theorem 32.4.14).

Step 8: naturality. For a homomorphism \(\varphi \colon G \to H\), \(t \mapsto \varphi (\exp tX)\) is a one-parameter subgroup of \(H\) with velocity \(d_e\varphi (X)\) at \(0\), so it equals \(t \mapsto \exp (t\, d_e\varphi (X))\) by Step 4; take \(t = 1\).

Step 9: matrix groups. In \(\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb {K})\), \(t \mapsto e^{tX}\) is a one-parameter subgroup with velocity \(X\) at \(0\) (Lemma 33.3.1(2)), so \(\exp (X) = e^X\) by Step 4. For a subgroup \(G \subseteq \mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb {K})\) that is a submanifold, Step 8 applied to the inclusion shows that the exponential map of \(G\) is the restriction of that of \(\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb {K})\).

02F5 Example 33.3.4

  1. For \(G = (\mathbb {R}^n, +)\), the one-parameter subgroups are \(t \mapsto tX\), and \(\exp = \mathrm{id}\). For the torus \(\mathbb {R}^n/\mathbb {Z}^n\), \(\exp \) is the projection \(\mathbb {R}^n \to \mathbb {R}^n/\mathbb {Z}^n\): it is surjective, a homomorphism, and not injective.

  2. For \(S^1 = \mathrm{U}(1)\), \(\mathfrak {u}(1) = i\mathbb {R}\) and \(\exp (it) = e^{it}\). For \(\mathrm{SO}(2)\), \(\exp (tJ)\) is the rotation by \(t\) (Example 33.3.2(3)); in both cases \(\exp \) is surjective with kernel \(2\pi \mathbb {Z}\) (times \(i\), respectively \(J\)).

  3. For \(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb {R})\), \(\exp \) is not surjective (Exercise 33.6.1), although it is a diffeomorphism near \(0\).

018P Definition 33.3.5

The adjoint representation is \(\operatorname {Ad}\colon G \to \mathrm{GL}(\mathfrak {g})\), \(\operatorname {Ad}(g) = d_e(c_g)\) where \(c_g(x) = gxg^{-1}\); its differential is \(\operatorname {ad}= d_e\operatorname {Ad}\colon \mathfrak {g} \to \mathfrak {gl}(\mathfrak {g})\).

For \(G \subseteq \mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb {K})\), \(c_A\) is the restriction of the linear map \(X \mapsto AXA^{-1}\), so \(\operatorname {Ad}(A)X = AXA^{-1}\); and differentiating \(\operatorname {Ad}(e^{tX})Y = e^{tX}Ye^{-tX}\) at \(t = 0\) gives \(\operatorname {ad}(X)Y = XY - YX\).

018Q Proposition 33.3.6

\(\operatorname {Ad}\) is a homomorphism of Lie groups, each \(\operatorname {Ad}(g)\) is an automorphism of the Lie algebra \(\mathfrak {g}\), \(\operatorname {ad}(X)(Y) = [X, Y]\), and \(\operatorname {Ad}(\exp X) = e^{\operatorname {ad}X}\).

Proof â–¼

Since \(c_{gh} = c_g \circ c_h\) and \(c_e = \mathrm{id}\), the chain rule shows that \(\operatorname {Ad}\) is a homomorphism into \(\mathrm{GL}(\mathfrak {g})\). Each \(c_g\) is an automorphism of the Lie group \(G\), so \(\operatorname {Ad}(g) = d_ec_g\) preserves brackets (Proposition 33.2.8). To see that \(\operatorname {Ad}\) is smooth, note that \(\operatorname {Ad}(g)X = \frac{\partial }{\partial t}\big|_{t=0}c_g(\exp tX)\), the partial derivative at \(t = 0\) of the smooth map \((g, t) \mapsto g\exp (tX)g^{-1}\); in a chart around \(e\) its components are smooth functions of \(g\), so the matrix entries of \(\operatorname {Ad}(g)\) in a basis of \(\mathfrak {g}\) are smooth.

For the bracket, let \(\widetilde{X}\), \(\widetilde{Y}\) be the left-invariant fields. By Step 1 of the proof of Theorem 33.3.3, the integral curve of \(\widetilde{X}\) through \(g\) is \(t \mapsto g\exp (tX)\), so the flow of \(\widetilde{X}\) is \(\Phi _t = R_{\exp (tX)}\). By Proposition 32.9.7,

\[ [X, Y] = [\widetilde{X}, \widetilde{Y}]_e = \frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}d(R_{\exp (-tX)})\bigl(\widetilde{Y}_{\exp (tX)}\bigr) = \frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}d(R_{\exp (-tX)})\, d(L_{\exp (tX)})(Y) = \frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}\operatorname {Ad}(\exp tX)Y, \]

because \(R_{\exp (-tX)} \circ L_{\exp (tX)} = c_{\exp (tX)}\). The curve \(t \mapsto \operatorname {Ad}(\exp tX)\) in \(\mathrm{GL}(\mathfrak {g})\) has velocity \(d_e\operatorname {Ad}(X) = \operatorname {ad}(X)\) at \(t = 0\), and evaluation at \(Y\) is linear, so the right-hand side is \(\operatorname {ad}(X)(Y)\). Finally, \(\operatorname {Ad}\) is a homomorphism \(G \to \mathrm{GL}(\mathfrak {g})\), and the exponential map of \(\mathrm{GL}(\mathfrak {g}) \cong \mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb {R})\) is the matrix exponential (Theorem 33.3.3), so naturality of \(\exp \) gives \(\operatorname {Ad}(\exp X) = \exp (d_e\operatorname {Ad}(X)) = e^{\operatorname {ad}X}\).

The next lemma says that to first order the group law, read through \(\exp \), is addition in \(\mathfrak {g}\).

02F6 Lemma 33.3.7

For \(X, Y \in \mathfrak {g}\), \(\lim _{k \to \infty }\bigl(\exp (X/k)\exp (Y/k)\bigr)^k = \exp (X + Y)\).

Proof â–¼

Let \(\exp \) map an open neighbourhood \(U_0\) of \(0\) diffeomorphically onto an open neighbourhood \(V_0\) of \(e\), with inverse \(\log \colon V_0 \to U_0\). For \((A, B)\) in a neighbourhood of \((0, 0)\) we have \(\exp (A)\exp (B) \in V_0\), and \(\mu (A, B) = \log (\exp (A)\exp (B))\) is smooth. By Lemma 33.2.2 and \(d_0\exp = \mathrm{id}\), its differential at \((0, 0)\) is \((A, B) \mapsto A + B\). So \(\mu (sX, sY) = s(X + Y) + r(s)\) with \(r(s)/s \to 0\) as \(s \to 0\). Since \(\exp (Z)^k = \exp (kZ)\) by the group laws,

\[ \bigl(\exp (X/k)\exp (Y/k)\bigr)^k = \exp \bigl(\mu (X/k, Y/k)\bigr)^k = \exp \bigl(X + Y + k\, r(1/k)\bigr), \]

and \(k\, r(1/k) \to 0\); by continuity of \(\exp \) the limit is \(\exp (X + Y)\).

02F7 Remark 33.3.8

Going one order further, \(\exp (sX)\exp (sY) = \exp \bigl(s(X + Y) + \tfrac {s^2}{2}[X, Y] + O(s^3)\bigr)\), and in fact \(\log (\exp X\exp Y)\) is given near \(0\) by a universal series in iterated brackets of \(X\) and \(Y\), the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula [Kna02, Chapter 1]. So near the identity the group law is determined by the bracket; this is why the Lie algebra captures the local structure of \(G\). We do not need these facts.

029F Proposition 33.3.9

Let \(G\) be a connected Lie group.

  1. \(G\) is generated by \(\exp (\mathfrak {g})\), indeed by \(\exp (U)\) for any neighbourhood \(U\) of \(0\).

  2. A homomorphism of Lie groups \(\varphi \colon G \to H\) is determined by \(d_e\varphi \).

  3. If \(G\) is abelian, then \(\exp \colon \mathfrak {g} \to G\) is a surjective homomorphism of groups, and \(\mathfrak {g}\) is abelian.

Proof â–¼

(1) By Theorem 33.3.3, \(\exp (U)\) contains an open neighbourhood \(V\) of \(e\), and replacing \(V\) by \(V \cap V^{-1}\) we may take \(V = V^{-1}\). By Proposition 19.2.3(3), \(V\) generates the connected group \(G\).

(2) \(\varphi (\exp X) = \exp (d_e\varphi (X))\), so \(d_e\varphi \) determines \(\varphi \) on \(\exp (\mathfrak {g})\), which generates \(G\) by (1).

(3) If \(G\) is abelian, every \(c_g\) is the identity, so \(\operatorname {Ad}(g) = \mathrm{id}\) for all \(g\), hence \(\operatorname {ad}= d_e\operatorname {Ad}= 0\), and \([X, Y] = \operatorname {ad}(X)(Y) = 0\) by Proposition 33.3.6. For \(X, Y \in \mathfrak {g}\), the curve \(t \mapsto \exp (tX)\exp (tY)\) is a homomorphism \(\mathbb {R}\to G\), because \(G\) is abelian, and its velocity at \(0\) is \(X + Y\) by Lemma 33.2.2; so it equals \(\exp (t(X + Y))\) by Theorem 33.3.3. So \(\exp \) is a homomorphism, and its image is a subgroup containing a neighbourhood of \(e\), hence all of \(G\) by (1).

029G Lemma 33.3.10

A discrete subgroup \(\Gamma \) of \(\mathbb {R}^n\) is of the form \(\mathbb {Z}v_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus \mathbb {Z}v_b\) for linearly independent vectors \(v_1, \ldots , v_b\).

Proof â–¼

Induction on the dimension of the span \(V\) of \(\Gamma \); the case \(V = 0\) is trivial. As \(\Gamma \) is discrete, it meets every bounded set in a finite set (a bounded infinite set would have an accumulation point, and differences of nearby points of \(\Gamma \) are small nonzero elements of \(\Gamma \), while discreteness gives a ball around \(0\) containing no other element of \(\Gamma \)). Choose a basis \(w_1, \ldots , w_b\) of \(V\) contained in \(\Gamma \), let \(W\) be the span of \(w_1, \ldots , w_{b-1}\), and \(\Gamma ' = \Gamma \cap W\), which by induction is \(\mathbb {Z}v_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus \mathbb {Z}v_{b-1}\) with \(v_i\) spanning \(W\). The elements of \(\Gamma \) of the form \(\sum _{i{\lt}b}s_iv_i + sw_b\) with \(s_i \in [0, 1]\) and \(s \in (0, 1]\) form a finite nonempty set (it contains \(w_b\)); choose one, \(v_b\), with the smallest \(s\), say \(s_0\). For \(\gamma \in \Gamma \), write \(\gamma = \sum _{i{\lt}b}c_iv_i + cw_b\) and let \(k = \lfloor c/s_0\rfloor \). Then \(\gamma - kv_b\) has \(w_b\)-coefficient in \([0, s_0)\), and subtracting a suitable element of \(\Gamma '\) moves its other coefficients into \([0, 1)\); by minimality of \(s_0\) the \(w_b\)-coefficient is \(0\), so \(\gamma - kv_b \in \Gamma '\). Hence \(\Gamma = \Gamma ' \oplus \mathbb {Z}v_b\).

029H Theorem 33.3.11

Every connected abelian Lie group is isomorphic to \(\mathbb {R}^a \times T^b\) for some \(a\), \(b\); in particular every compact connected abelian Lie group is a torus.

Proof â–¼

Let \(n = \dim G\) and identify \(\mathfrak {g}\) with \(\mathbb {R}^n\) by a basis. By Proposition 33.3.9(3), \(\exp \colon \mathbb {R}^n \to G\) is a surjective homomorphism. It is a diffeomorphism near \(0\), hence a local diffeomorphism everywhere, since \(\exp (X + Z) = \exp (X)\exp (Z)\), that is, \(\exp \circ (Z \mapsto X + Z) = L_{\exp X} \circ \exp \). Its kernel \(\Gamma \) is a subgroup with \(\Gamma \cap U = \{ 0\} \) for a neighbourhood \(U\) of \(0\) on which \(\exp \) is injective; so \(\Gamma \) is discrete, because \((\gamma + U) \cap \Gamma = \{ \gamma \} \) for \(\gamma \in \Gamma \). By Lemma 33.3.10, \(\Gamma = \mathbb {Z}v_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus \mathbb {Z}v_b\); completing to a basis \(v_1, \ldots , v_n\) of \(\mathbb {R}^n\) gives a linear isomorphism taking \(\Gamma \) to \(\mathbb {Z}^b \times \{ 0\} \), hence \(\mathbb {R}^n/\Gamma \cong (\mathbb {R}^b/\mathbb {Z}^b) \times \mathbb {R}^{n-b} = T^b \times \mathbb {R}^{n-b}\). Here \(\Gamma \) acts freely and properly on \(\mathbb {R}^n\) by translations (a compact set meets only finitely many of its translates by \(\Gamma \), since \(\Gamma \) meets bounded sets in finite sets), so \(\mathbb {R}^n/\Gamma \) is a Lie group by Theorem 32.7.1. The induced map \(\mathbb {R}^n/\Gamma \to G\) is a bijective homomorphism and a local diffeomorphism, hence an isomorphism of Lie groups (Theorem 32.4.14). If \(G\) is compact, so is \(T^b \times \mathbb {R}^{n-b}\), and \(n - b = 0\).

029I Counterexample 33.3.12

Connectedness is needed: \(\mathrm{O}(2)\) has the abelian Lie algebra \(\mathfrak {o}(2) \cong \mathbb {R}\), but it is not abelian, as a reflection does not commute with a rotation by an angle other than \(0\) or \(\pi \).
