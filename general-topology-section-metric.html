18.3 Metric spaces00R8

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

00R9 Definition 18.3.1

A metric on a set \(X\) is a map \(d \colon X \times X \to \mathbb {R}\) such that for all \(x, y, z \in X\): \(d(x, y) \geq 0\) with equality if and only if \(x = y\); \(d(x, y) = d(y, x)\); and \(d(x, z) \leq d(x, y) + d(y, z)\) (the triangle inequality). The open ball of radius \(r {\gt} 0\) around \(x\) is \(B(x, r) = \{ y : d(x, y) {\lt} r\} \). A set \(A\) is bounded if \(A \subseteq B(x, r)\) for some \(x\) and \(r\).

00RA Lemma 18.3.2

The open balls of a metric space form a basis of a topology, the metric topology: \(U\) is open if and only if for every \(x \in U\) there is \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\) with \(B(x, \varepsilon ) \subseteq U\). It is Hausdorff. A space whose topology is the metric topology of some metric is metrisable.

Proof â–¼

If \(x \in B(a, r) \cap B(b, s)\), let \(\varepsilon = \min (r - d(a, x), s - d(b, x)) {\gt} 0\). By the triangle inequality \(B(x, \varepsilon )\) is contained in both balls, so Lemma 18.2.10 applies; the description of open sets follows. If \(x \neq y\) and \(r = d(x, y)\), the balls \(B(x, r/2)\) and \(B(y, r/2)\) are disjoint by the triangle inequality.

00RB Definition 18.3.3

Let \(\mathbb {K} = \mathbb {R}\) or \(\mathbb {C}\), with absolute value \(|\cdot |\). A norm on a \(\mathbb {K}\)-vector space \(V\) is a map \(\| \cdot \| \colon V \to \mathbb {R}\) such that \(\| v\| = 0\) only for \(v = 0\), \(\| \lambda v\| = |\lambda |\, \| v\| \) and \(\| v + w\| \leq \| v\| + \| w\| \) (so \(\| v\| \geq 0\)). Then \(d(v, w) = \| v - w\| \) is a metric. On \(\mathbb {K}^n\) we use the Euclidean norm \(\| x\| _2 = (\sum _i |x_i|^2)^{1/2}\), the norm of the standard inner product (a norm by Proposition 11.10.2), and the maximum norm \(\| x\| _\infty = \max _i |x_i|\).

00RC Lemma 18.3.4

If \(d, d'\) are metrics on \(X\) with \(c\, d \leq d' \leq C\, d\) for constants \(c, C {\gt} 0\), they define the same topology. In particular, since \(\| x\| _\infty \leq \| x\| _2 \leq \sqrt{n}\, \| x\| _\infty \), the two norms on \(\mathbb {K}^n\) define the same topology. On \(\mathbb {R}\) it is the topology with basis the open intervals, the standard topology.

Proof â–¼

\(B_{d'}(x, c\varepsilon ) \subseteq B_d(x, \varepsilon )\) and \(B_d(x, \varepsilon /C) \subseteq B_{d'}(x, \varepsilon )\), so the criterion of Lemma 18.3.2 gives the same open sets. The norm inequalities hold because \(\max _i |x_i|^2 \leq \sum _i |x_i|^2 \leq n \max _i |x_i|^2\). On \(\mathbb {R}\) the balls are the bounded open intervals \((a - r, a + r)\), and every bounded open interval is such a ball.

00RD Proposition 18.3.5

Let \(f \colon X \to Y\) be a map of metric spaces.

  1. \(f\) is continuous if and only if for every \(x\) and \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\) there is \(\delta {\gt} 0\) with \(d(f(x), f(x')) {\lt} \varepsilon \) whenever \(d(x, x') {\lt} \delta \).

  2. For \(A \subseteq X\), a point \(x\) lies in \(\overline{A}\) if and only if it is the limit of a sequence in \(A\).

  3. \(f\) is continuous if and only if \(f(x_n) \to f(x)\) whenever \(x_n \to x\).

Proof â–¼

(1) is Proposition 18.2.13(4) with balls. (2) If \(x \in \overline{A}\), choose \(a_n \in A \cap B(x, 1/(n+1))\) by Lemma 18.2.4; then \(a_n \to x\). Conversely, if \(a_n \to x\), every open set containing \(x\) contains some \(a_n\). (3) If \(f\) is continuous and \(x_n \to x\), every open \(V \ni f(x)\) has open preimage containing \(x\), hence containing \(x_n\) for large \(n\). If \(f\) is not continuous at \(x\), there are \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\) and \(x_n\) with \(d(x, x_n) {\lt} 1/(n+1)\) and \(d(f(x), f(x_n)) \geq \varepsilon \); then \(x_n \to x\) but \(f(x_n) \not\to f(x)\).

02SR Remark 18.3.6

Limits of maps. Let \(f \colon X \to Y\) be a map of topological spaces and \(x_0 \in X\) a point that is not isolated, that is, every neighbourhood of \(x_0\) contains points other than \(x_0\). One writes \(\lim _{x \to x_0}f(x) = y\) if for every neighbourhood \(V\) of \(y\) there is a neighbourhood \(U\) of \(x_0\) with \(f(U \setminus \{ x_0\} ) \subseteq V\); the value \(f(x_0)\) plays no role. If \(Y\) is Hausdorff the limit is unique: limits \(y \neq y^{\prime }\) with disjoint neighbourhoods \(V\), \(V^{\prime }\) would give neighbourhoods \(U\), \(U^{\prime }\) with \(f(U \cap U^{\prime } \setminus \{ x_0\} ) \subseteq V \cap V^{\prime } = \emptyset \), although \(U \cap U^{\prime }\) contains points other than \(x_0\). By Proposition 18.2.13(4), applied at the single point \(x_0\), \(f\) is continuous at \(x_0\) exactly when

\[ \lim _{x \to x_0}f(x) = f(x_0), \]

the condition at \(x_0\) itself being automatic; and \(f\) is continuous if and only if it is continuous at every point. At an isolated point every map is continuous, and the limit is not defined (every \(y\) would satisfy the condition). For metric spaces, \(\lim _{x \to x_0}f(x) = y\) means that for every \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\) there is \(\delta {\gt} 0\) with \(d(f(x), y) {\lt} \varepsilon \) whenever \(0 {\lt} d(x, x_0) {\lt} \delta \), and, as in the proof of (3) above, it is equivalent to \(f(x_n) \to y\) for every sequence \(x_n \to x_0\) with \(x_n \neq x_0\). For \(X \subseteq \mathbb {R}\) this is the limit of real analysis (Chapter 27).

02T9 Example 18.3.7

The closed ball and the closure of the ball. In a metric space the closed ball \(\bar B(x, r) = \{ y : d(x, y) \leq r\} \) is closed (its complement is open by the triangle inequality), so it contains the closure \(\overline{B(x, r)}\) of the open ball. They can differ: for the discrete metric (\(d(x, y) = 1\) for \(x \neq y\)) on a set with at least two points, \(B(x, 1) = \{ x\} \) is closed, while \(\bar B(x, 1)\) is the whole space. In a normed space, for \(r {\gt} 0\) they agree: if \(\| y - x\| = r\), the points \(x + t(y - x)\), \(0 {\lt} t {\lt} 1\), lie in \(B(x, r)\) and tend to \(y\). Similarly the boundary of \(B(x, r)\) is contained in the sphere \(\{ d(x, y) = r\} \), with equality in normed spaces but not in general.

00RE Counterexample 18.3.8

In general spaces sequences do not detect closures. Give \(\mathbb {R}\) the cocountable topology. If \(x_n \to x\), the set \(C = \{ x_n : x_n \neq x\} \) is countable, so \(\mathbb {R}\setminus C\) is an open set containing \(x\), and hence \(x_n = x\) for large \(n\). Now \(A = \mathbb {R}\setminus \{ 0\} \) is uncountable (Proposition 4.8.6), so the only closed set containing it is \(\mathbb {R}\) and \(0 \in \overline{A}\); but no sequence in \(A\) converges to \(0\). Nets or filters replace sequences in general (Section 18.12).

00RF Proposition 18.3.9

Addition and multiplication \(\mathbb {K} \times \mathbb {K} \to \mathbb {K}\) are continuous for the metric \(\| (x, y) - (a, b)\| _\infty \), and so is \(x \mapsto 1/x\) on \(\mathbb {K} \setminus \{ 0\} \).

Proof â–¼

We have \(|(x + y) - (a + b)| \leq |x - a| + |y - b|\), and if \(|x - a|, |y - b| {\lt} \delta \leq 1\) then \(|xy - ab| \leq |x|\, |y - b| + |b|\, |x - a| \leq (|a| + 1 + |b|)\delta \). If \(|x - a| {\lt} |a|/2\) then \(|x| {\gt} |a|/2\) and \(|1/x - 1/a| = |x - a|/(|x|\, |a|) \leq 2|x - a|/|a|^2\). In each case the criterion of Proposition 18.3.5(1) holds.

00RG Proposition 18.3.10

Let \(X\) be a space, \((Y, d)\) a metric space and \(f_n \colon X \to Y\) continuous maps converging uniformly to \(f\): for every \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\) there is \(N\) with \(d(f_n(x), f(x)) {\lt} \varepsilon \) for all \(n \geq N\) and all \(x\). Then \(f\) is continuous.

Proof â–¼

Let \(x \in X\) and \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\). Choose \(n\) with \(d(f_n, f) {\lt} \varepsilon /3\) everywhere, and an open \(U \ni x\) with \(d(f_n(x), f_n(x')) {\lt} \varepsilon /3\) for \(x' \in U\). Then \(d(f(x), f(x')) {\lt} \varepsilon \) for \(x' \in U\), and Proposition 18.2.13(4) applies.

00RH Definition 18.3.11

A space is first countable if every point \(x\) has countably many open neighbourhoods such that every open set containing \(x\) contains one of them; second countable if its topology has a countable basis; separable if it has a countable dense subset; and Lindelöf if every open cover has a countable subcover.

00RI Proposition 18.3.12

Metric spaces are first countable. Second countable spaces are separable and Lindelöf. A separable metric space is second countable. In particular \(\mathbb {R}^n\) and \(\mathbb {C}^n\) are second countable.

Proof â–¼

The balls \(B(x, 1/(n+1))\) show first countability. Let \(\mathcal{B}\) be a countable basis. Choosing a point in each nonempty member gives a countable set meeting every nonempty open set, hence dense by Lemma 18.2.4. Given an open cover \(\mathcal{U}\), for each \(B \in \mathcal{B}\) contained in some member of \(\mathcal{U}\) choose one such member \(U_B\); these countably many sets cover, because a point \(x \in U \in \mathcal{U}\) lies in some \(B \in \mathcal{B}\) with \(B \subseteq U\), and then \(x \in U_B\). If \(D\) is a countable dense subset of a metric space, the balls \(B(q, 1/n)\) with \(q \in D\), \(n \geq 1\) form a basis: if \(x \in U\) with \(U\) open, choose \(n\) with \(B(x, 2/n) \subseteq U\) and \(q \in D \cap B(x, 1/n)\); then \(x \in B(q, 1/n) \subseteq B(x, 2/n) \subseteq U\). Finally \(\mathbb {Q}^n\) and \((\mathbb {Q}+ i\mathbb {Q})^n\) are countable (Corollary 3.6.13) and dense, by density of \(\mathbb {Q}\) in \(\mathbb {R}\) (Proposition 4.7.3).

029B Counterexample 18.3.13 The Sorgenfrey line

The half-open intervals \([a, b)\) form a basis of a topology on \(\mathbb {R}\), the Sorgenfrey line, finer than the Euclidean topology. It is first countable (the sets \([x, x + 1/n)\) form a neighbourhood basis at \(x\)) and separable (\(\mathbb {Q}\) is dense, as every \([a, b)\) contains a rational number). It is not second countable: if \(\mathcal{B}\) is a basis, then for each \(x\) some \(B_x \in \mathcal{B}\) satisfies \(x \in B_x \subseteq [x, x + 1)\), so \(x = \min B_x\), and \(x \mapsto B_x\) is injective from the uncountable set \(\mathbb {R}\) to \(\mathcal{B}\). By Proposition 18.3.12, it is therefore not metrisable: separable metric spaces are second countable.
