19.2 Topological groups025W

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

025X Definition 19.2.1

A topological group is a group \(G\) with a topology such that the multiplication \(G \times G \to G\), \((g, h) \mapsto gh\), and the inversion \(G \to G\), \(g \mapsto g^{-1}\), are continuous. A homomorphism of topological groups is a continuous group homomorphism.

025Y Example 19.2.2

  1. \((\mathbb {R}^n, +)\), \((\mathbb {C}, +)\), the multiplicative groups \(\mathbb {R}^\times \), \(\mathbb {R}_{{\gt}0}\) and \(\mathbb {C}^\times \), and the circle \(S^1 = \{ z \in \mathbb {C}: |z| = 1\} \), with the Euclidean topologies: the operations are polynomial or rational.

  2. \(\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb {R})\) and \(\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb {C})\), open subsets of the spaces of matrices \(\mathbb {R}^{n^2}\) and \(\mathbb {C}^{n^2}\) (as the complement of the zero set of \(\det \)); multiplication is polynomial in the entries and inversion is rational by Cramer’s rule. Their subgroups \(\mathrm{SL}_n\), \(\mathrm{O}(n) = \{ A : A^TA = I\} \), \(\mathrm{SO}(n)\), \(\mathrm{U}(n) = \{ A : \bar A^TA = I\} \) and \(\mathrm{SU}(n)\), with the subspace topologies. \(\mathrm{O}(n)\) and \(\mathrm{U}(n)\) are compact, being closed and bounded (their columns are unit vectors).

  3. Any group with the discrete topology; products of topological groups.

  4. \((\mathbb {Q}, +)\) with the subspace topology, a topological group that is neither discrete nor locally compact.

025Z Proposition 19.2.3

Let \(G\) be a topological group.

  1. For \(g \in G\), left translation \(h \mapsto gh\), right translation and inversion are homeomorphisms of \(G\).

  2. An open subgroup \(H\) is also closed.

  3. Let \(U\) be a neighbourhood of \(e\) with \(U = U^{-1}\). The subgroup generated by \(U\) is open. If \(G\) is connected, it is \(G\).

  4. The connected component \(G^0\) of \(e\) is a closed normal subgroup.

  5. \(G\) is Hausdorff if and only if \(\{ e\} \) is closed.

Proof â–¼

(1) Left translation by \(g\) is continuous, as the composite of \(h \mapsto (g, h)\) and multiplication, with continuous inverse left translation by \(g^{-1}\); similarly on the right, and inversion is its own inverse. (2) The complement of \(H\) is the union of the cosets \(gH\), \(g \notin H\), which are open by (1). (3) The subgroup \(H\) generated by \(U\) is the union of the sets \(u_1 \cdots u_kU\) with \(u_i \in U\), which are open by (1). By (2) it is also closed, and nonempty, so \(H = G\) if \(G\) is connected. (4) \(G^0G^0\) is the image of the connected set \(G^0 \times G^0\) under multiplication, hence connected, and it contains \(e\); so \(G^0G^0 \subseteq G^0\). Similarly \((G^0)^{-1} \subseteq G^0\) and \(gG^0g^{-1} \subseteq G^0\) for all \(g\). Components are closed. (5) If \(G\) is Hausdorff, points are closed. Conversely, if \(\{ e\} \) is closed, so is the diagonal \(\{ (g, h) : gh^{-1} = e\} \), the preimage of \(\{ e\} \) under the continuous map \((g, h) \mapsto gh^{-1}\). If \(g \neq h\), then \((g, h)\) lies in the open complement of the diagonal, so some product neighbourhood \(A \times B\) of \((g, h)\) misses the diagonal, that is, \(A \cap B = \emptyset \).

0260 Counterexample 19.2.4

Any group with the indiscrete topology, whose only open sets are \(\emptyset \) and \(G\), is a topological group, and it is not Hausdorff if \(|G| {\gt} 1\). So (5) is a genuine condition. More generally, for any normal subgroup \(N\) the quotient \(G/N\) with the quotient topology is a topological group, and it is Hausdorff if and only if \(N\) is closed (Exercise 19.8.1).
