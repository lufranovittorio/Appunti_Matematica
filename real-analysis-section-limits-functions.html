26.3 Limits of functions0249

Complete. This chapter is written; its proofs await independent checking.

To treat finite and infinite limits at once we use the extended real line \(\bar{\mathbb {R}} = \mathbb {R}\cup \{ -\infty , +\infty \} \), ordered so that \(-\infty {\lt} x {\lt} +\infty \) for \(x \in \mathbb {R}\).

024A Definition 26.3.1

For \(a \in \bar{\mathbb {R}}\) and \(\delta {\gt} 0\), the basic neighbourhood \(U_\delta (a)\) is \((a - \delta , a + \delta )\) if \(a \in \mathbb {R}\), \((1/\delta , +\infty )\) if \(a = +\infty \), and \((-\infty , -1/\delta )\) if \(a = -\infty \). Let \(D \subseteq \mathbb {R}\). A point \(a \in \bar{\mathbb {R}}\) is an accumulation point of \(D\) if every \(U_\delta (a)\) contains a point of \(D \setminus \{ a\} \). A point of \(D\) that is not an accumulation point is isolated.

A sequence \((x_n)\) tends to \(a \in \bar{\mathbb {R}}\) if and only if for every \(\delta {\gt} 0\), eventually \(x_n \in U_\delta (a)\): for \(a \in \mathbb {R}\) this is convergence, and for \(a = \pm \infty \) divergence to \(\pm \infty \). Every \(a \in \bar{\mathbb {R}}\) that is an accumulation point of \(D\) is the limit of a sequence in \(D \setminus \{ a\} \): choose \(x_n \in U_{1/(n+1)}(a) \cap D \setminus \{ a\} \).

024B Definition 26.3.2

Let \(f \colon D \to \mathbb {R}\), let \(a \in \bar{\mathbb {R}}\) be an accumulation point of \(D\), and \(L \in \bar{\mathbb {R}}\). We write \(\lim _{x \to a}f(x) = L\), or \(f(x) \to L\) as \(x \to a\), if for every \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\) there is \(\delta {\gt} 0\) such that \(f(x) \in U_\varepsilon (L)\) for all \(x \in D \cap U_\delta (a)\) with \(x \neq a\). For \(a, L \in \mathbb {R}\) this reads: for every \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\) there is \(\delta {\gt} 0\) with \(|f(x) - L| {\lt} \varepsilon \) whenever \(x \in D\) and \(0 {\lt} |x - a| {\lt} \delta \). The right limit \(\lim _{x \to a^+}f(x)\) is the limit of the restriction of \(f\) to \(D \cap (a, +\infty )\), when \(a\) is an accumulation point of that set, and the left limit \(\lim _{x \to a^-}f(x)\) is defined similarly. The same definitions apply to \(\mathbb {C}\)- or \(\mathbb {R}^m\)-valued functions and finite limits, with \(|\cdot |\) the modulus or the Euclidean norm.

The value of \(f\) at \(a\), if \(a \in D\), plays no role in the definition.

024C Proposition 26.3.3

Let \(f \colon D \to \mathbb {R}\) and let \(a \in \bar{\mathbb {R}}\) be an accumulation point of \(D\).

  1. The limit of \(f\) at \(a\), if it exists, is unique.

  2. If \(\lim _{x \to a}f(x) = L \in \mathbb {R}\), then \(f\) is bounded on \(D \cap U_\delta (a) \setminus \{ a\} \) for some \(\delta {\gt} 0\); if \(L {\gt} 0\), then \(f(x) {\gt} L/2\) for \(x \in D \cap U_\delta (a) \setminus \{ a\} \) for some \(\delta {\gt} 0\).

  3. If \(a \in \mathbb {R}\) is an accumulation point of both \(D \cap (a, +\infty )\) and \(D \cap (-\infty , a)\), then \(\lim _{x \to a}f(x) = L\) if and only if both one-sided limits exist and equal \(L\).

Proof â–¼

(1) If \(L \neq L'\) are both limits, choose \(\varepsilon \) with \(U_\varepsilon (L) \cap U_\varepsilon (L') = \emptyset \), and \(\delta \) working for both; since \(a\) is an accumulation point, some \(x \in D \cap U_\delta (a) \setminus \{ a\} \) exists, and \(f(x)\) lies in both neighbourhoods. (2) Take \(\varepsilon = 1\), respectively \(\varepsilon = L/2\). (3) If both one-sided limits equal \(L\), take the smaller of the two \(\delta \); the converse is clear.

024D Theorem 26.3.4 Sequential characterisation of limits

Let \(f \colon D \to \mathbb {R}\), let \(a \in \bar{\mathbb {R}}\) be an accumulation point of \(D\), and \(L \in \bar{\mathbb {R}}\). The following are equivalent:

  1. \(\lim _{x \to a}f(x) = L\);

  2. for every sequence \((x_n)\) in \(D \setminus \{ a\} \) with \(x_n \to a\), we have \(f(x_n) \to L\).

Proof â–¼

(1) \(\Rightarrow \) (2). Let \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\) and choose \(\delta \) as in Definition 26.3.2. Eventually \(x_n \in U_\delta (a)\), and \(x_n \in D \setminus \{ a\} \), so eventually \(f(x_n) \in U_\varepsilon (L)\).

(2) \(\Rightarrow \) (1). Suppose (1) fails. Then there is \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\) such that for every \(\delta {\gt} 0\) some \(x \in D \cap U_\delta (a) \setminus \{ a\} \) has \(f(x) \notin U_\varepsilon (L)\). Choosing such a point \(x_n\) for \(\delta = 1/(n + 1)\), for each \(n\) (this uses the axiom of countable choice), we obtain a sequence in \(D \setminus \{ a\} \) with \(x_n \to a\) and \(f(x_n) \notin U_\varepsilon (L)\) for all \(n\), so \(f(x_n) \not\to L\), contradicting (2).

Theorem 26.3.4, sometimes called the bridge theorem, transfers every result about limits of sequences to limits of functions, and it is the standard way to show that a limit does not exist: exhibit two sequences tending to \(a\) along which \(f\) has different limits.

024E Corollary 26.3.5 Rules for limits of functions

Let \(f, g, h \colon D \to \mathbb {R}\) and let \(a \in \bar{\mathbb {R}}\) be an accumulation point of \(D\).

  1. If \(f(x) \to L\) and \(g(x) \to M\) with \(L, M \in \mathbb {R}\), then \(f + g \to L + M\), \(fg \to LM\), and, if \(M \neq 0\), \(f/g \to L/M\) (where \(f/g\) is defined near \(a\)).

  2. If \(f \leq g\) near \(a\) and \(f \to L\), \(g \to M\), then \(L \leq M\).

  3. (Squeeze) If \(f \leq h \leq g\) near \(a\) and \(f(x) \to L\), \(g(x) \to L\), then \(h(x) \to L\).

  4. The rules for infinite limits of Proposition 26.2.7 hold for functions.

Proof â–¼

Apply Theorem 26.3.4 and Propositions 26.2.4, 26.2.5 and 26.2.7 to \(f(x_n)\), \(g(x_n)\), \(h(x_n)\) for sequences \(x_n \to a\) in \(D \setminus \{ a\} \). For the quotient, \(g(x) \neq 0\) near \(a\) by Proposition 26.3.3(2) applied to \(|g|\).

024F Proposition 26.3.6 Limits of composite functions

Let \(f \colon D \to \mathbb {R}\) and \(g \colon E \to \mathbb {R}\) with \(f(D) \subseteq E\). Suppose \(\lim _{x \to a}f(x) = b\) and \(\lim _{y \to b}g(y) = L\), and that one of the following holds:

  1. \(f(x) \neq b\) for all \(x \in D \setminus \{ a\} \) near \(a\);

  2. \(b \in E\) and \(g(b) = L\).

Then \(\lim _{x \to a}g(f(x)) = L\).

Proof â–¼

Let \(x_n \to a\) in \(D \setminus \{ a\} \). Then \(f(x_n) \to b\) by Theorem 26.3.4. In case (1), eventually \(f(x_n) \in E \setminus \{ b\} \), so \(g(f(x_n)) \to L\) by the same theorem. In case (2), given \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\) choose \(\eta \) with \(g(y) \in U_\varepsilon (L)\) for \(y \in E \cap U_\eta (b)\), including \(y = b\); eventually \(f(x_n) \in U_\eta (b)\), so \(g(f(x_n)) \in U_\varepsilon (L)\). Apply the theorem again.

024G Counterexample 26.3.7

Neither hypothesis can be dropped. Let \(f(x) = 0\) for all \(x\), and \(g(y) = 0\) for \(y \neq 0\), \(g(0) = 1\). Then \(\lim _{x \to 0}f(x) = 0\) and \(\lim _{y \to 0}g(y) = 0\), but \(g(f(x)) = 1\) for all \(x\).

024H Proposition 26.3.8 Cauchy criterion for functions

Let \(f \colon D \to \mathbb {R}\) and let \(a \in \bar{\mathbb {R}}\) be an accumulation point of \(D\). Then \(\lim _{x \to a}f(x)\) exists in \(\mathbb {R}\) if and only if for every \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\) there is \(\delta {\gt} 0\) with \(|f(x) - f(y)| {\lt} \varepsilon \) for all \(x, y \in D \cap U_\delta (a) \setminus \{ a\} \).

Proof â–¼

If \(f(x) \to L\), take \(\delta \) for \(\varepsilon /2\) and use the triangle inequality. Conversely, for every sequence \(x_n \to a\) in \(D \setminus \{ a\} \), the sequence \((f(x_n))\) is Cauchy, hence converges (Theorem 26.2.13). If \(x_n \to a\) and \(y_n \to a\) are two such sequences, the interleaved sequence \(x_0, y_0, x_1, y_1, \ldots \) also tends to \(a\), so \((f(x_n))\) and \((f(y_n))\) have the same limit, being subsequences of a convergent sequence. So all these limits equal a common \(L\), and \(f(x) \to L\) by Theorem 26.3.4.

024I Proposition 26.3.9 Monotone functions

Let \(f \colon (a, b) \to \mathbb {R}\) be increasing, with \(-\infty \leq a {\lt} b \leq +\infty \). For every \(c \in (a, b)\) the one-sided limits exist and

\[ \lim _{x \to c^-}f(x) = \sup _{a {\lt} x {\lt} c}f(x) \leq f(c) \leq \inf _{c {\lt} x {\lt} b}f(x) = \lim _{x \to c^+}f(x), \]

and \(\lim _{x \to b^-}f(x) = \sup f \in \mathbb {R}\cup \{ +\infty \} \), \(\lim _{x \to a^+}f(x) = \inf f \in \mathbb {R}\cup \{ -\infty \} \). The analogous statements hold for decreasing functions.

Proof â–¼

The set \(\{ f(x) : a {\lt} x {\lt} c\} \) is bounded above by \(f(c)\); let \(s\) be its supremum, so \(s \leq f(c)\). Given \(\varepsilon {\gt} 0\) there is \(x_0 {\lt} c\) with \(f(x_0) {\gt} s - \varepsilon \), and for \(x_0 {\lt} x {\lt} c\), \(s - \varepsilon {\lt} f(x_0) \leq f(x) \leq s\). So \(\lim _{x \to c^-}f(x) = s\). The other statements are proved in the same way (for \(b = +\infty \) or an unbounded supremum, use neighbourhoods of \(+\infty \)), and the decreasing case follows by passing to \(-f\).

024J Counterexample 26.3.10

  1. The sign function \(\operatorname {sgn}(x) = x/|x|\) on \(\mathbb {R}\setminus \{ 0\} \) has one-sided limits \(\pm 1\) at \(0\), and no limit there.

  2. \(1/x\) has no limit at \(0\): its one-sided limits are \(+\infty \) and \(-\infty \).

  3. The Dirichlet function \(\mathbf{1}_\mathbb {Q}\), equal to \(1\) on \(\mathbb {Q}\) and \(0\) on \(\mathbb {R}\setminus \mathbb {Q}\), has no limit at any \(a \in \mathbb {R}\). Both \(\mathbb {Q}\) and \(\mathbb {R}\setminus \mathbb {Q}\) are dense in \(\mathbb {R}\): \(\mathbb {Q}\) by Proposition 4.7.3, and \(\mathbb {R}\setminus \mathbb {Q}\) because \(q\sqrt2\) is irrational for rational \(q \neq 0\) (Proposition 4.6.4) and \(x/\sqrt2 {\lt} q {\lt} y/\sqrt2\) is possible for \(x {\lt} y\). So there are rational \(x_n \to a\) and irrational \(y_n \to a\), all \(\neq a\), and \(\mathbf{1}_\mathbb {Q}(x_n) \to 1\), \(\mathbf{1}_\mathbb {Q}(y_n) \to 0\).
