0190 Exercise 33.6.1

Show that \(\exp \colon \mathfrak {sl}_2(\mathbb {R}) \to \mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb {R})\) is not surjective, although \(\exp \) is surjective for compact connected Lie groups and for \(\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb {C})\).

Solution â–¼

If \(X \in \mathfrak {sl}_2(\mathbb {R})\) has eigenvalues \(\pm \lambda \), then \(e^X\) has eigenvalues \(e^{\pm \lambda }\) (triangularise \(X\) over \(\mathbb {C}\) and use Lemma 33.3.1(3)). If these are real and distinct, then \(\lambda \) is real (for \(\lambda = i\theta \), \(e^{\pm i\theta }\) is real only if it equals \(\pm 1\), and then the two eigenvalues coincide), so both are positive. The matrix \(\operatorname {diag}(-2, -1/2) \in \mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb {R})\) has distinct negative eigenvalues, so it is not in the image. For \(\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb {C})\): by the Jordan form it suffices to treat a block \(\lambda (I + N)\) with \(\lambda \neq 0\) and \(N\) nilpotent; choose \(c\) with \(e^c = \lambda \) and put \(L = \sum _{k \geq 1}(-1)^{k+1}N^k/k\), a finite sum. The identity \(\exp (\log (1 + x)) = 1 + x\) of formal power series gives \(e^L = I + N\), and \(L\) commutes with \(cI\), so \(e^{cI + L} = \lambda (I + N)\). For compact connected groups, surjectivity follows from the theory of maximal tori [BtD85, Chapter IV].

0191 Exercise 33.6.2

Show that \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\) is diffeomorphic to \(S^3\), that its Lie algebra is \(\mathfrak {su}(2) \cong \mathbb {R}^3\) with the cross product, and that the adjoint representation gives a double covering \(\mathrm{SU}(2) \to \mathrm{SO}(3)\).

Solution â–¼

The first two statements are Example 33.2.11, together with Example 33.2.10. For the third, the form \(\langle X, Y\rangle = -2\operatorname {tr}(XY)\) on \(\mathfrak {su}(2)\) makes the basis \(e_1, e_2, e_3\) of Example 33.2.11 orthonormal (for instance \(\operatorname {tr}(e_1^2) = -1/2\) and \(\operatorname {tr}(e_1e_2) = \operatorname {tr}(I_3)/4 = 0\)), and it is invariant under \(\operatorname {Ad}(A)X = AXA^{-1}\). So in this basis \(\operatorname {Ad}\) is a homomorphism \(\mathrm{SU}(2) \to \mathrm{O}(3)\); since \(\mathrm{SU}(2) \cong S^3\) is connected and \(\det \circ \operatorname {Ad}\) is continuous with values \(\pm 1\) and value \(1\) at \(I\), it maps into \(\mathrm{SO}(3)\). Its kernel consists of the \(A\) commuting with \(I_1, I_2, I_3\); these together with \(I\) span \(M_2(\mathbb {C})\) over \(\mathbb {C}\), so \(A = \lambda I\), and \(\det A = \lambda ^2 = 1\) gives \(A = \pm I\). Its differential \(\operatorname {ad}\colon \mathfrak {su}(2) \to \mathfrak {so}(3)\) is injective, because \(\operatorname {ad}X = 0\) means that \(X\) commutes with \(e_1, e_2, e_3\), which forces \(X = 0\) by the bracket relations; so it is an isomorphism, both spaces having dimension \(3\). As \(\operatorname {Ad}\) is a homomorphism, its differential is then an isomorphism everywhere, so \(\operatorname {Ad}\) is a local diffeomorphism. Its image is open, and compact hence closed, so it is all of the connected group \(\mathrm{SO}(3)\) (Proposition 33.4.6). The induced bijection \(\mathrm{SU}(2)/\{ \pm I\} \to \mathrm{SO}(3)\) is a local diffeomorphism, hence a diffeomorphism, where \(\mathrm{SU}(2)/\{ \pm I\} \) is the quotient of \(S^3\) by the free action of \(\{ \pm 1\} \), with covering map of degree \(2\) (Theorem 32.7.1). In particular \(\mathrm{SO}(3) \cong \mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^3\).

0192 Exercise 33.6.3

Show that the image of the homomorphism \(\mathbb {R}\to T^2\), \(t \mapsto (t, \alpha t) \bmod \mathbb {Z}^2\), with \(\alpha \) irrational, is a dense subgroup that is not closed and not an embedded submanifold; so “closed” cannot be dropped in Theorem 33.4.1.

Solution â–¼

Identifying \(\mathbb {R}/\mathbb {Z}\) with \(S^1\), this is the map of Counterexample 32.6.14(2): its image is a dense subgroup, and not a submanifold. It is not closed, since it is dense and not all of \(T^2\) (it meets the circle \(\{ 0\} \times S^1\) in a countable set).

0193 Exercise 33.6.4

Verify that \(\mathrm{Gr}(k, n) = O(n)/(O(k) \times O(n-k))\) has dimension \(k(n-k)\) and is connected, and identify \(\mathrm{Gr}(1, n)\) with \(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^{n-1}\).

Solution â–¼

By Theorem 33.4.4 and Example 33.2.5(2), the dimension is \(\frac{n(n - 1)}{2} - \frac{k(k - 1)}{2} - \frac{(n - k)(n - k - 1)}{2} = \frac{n^2 - k^2 - (n - k)^2}{2} = k(n - k)\). It is connected, being the image of the connected group \(\mathrm{SO}(n)\), which already acts transitively for \(0 {\lt} k {\lt} n\) (change the sign of one basis vector of \(W^\perp \)). For \(k = 1\) it is the space of lines, \(\mathbb {R}\mathbb {P}^{n-1}\) (Proposition 32.7.4).

02FB Exercise 33.6.5

Show that \(\exp \colon \mathfrak {so}(3) \to \mathrm{SO}(3)\) is surjective: every rotation of \(\mathbb {R}^3\) is \(e^{\theta L_a}\) for a unit vector \(a\) and an angle \(\theta \), with \(L_a\) as in Example 33.2.10.

Solution â–¼

Let \(A \in \mathrm{SO}(3)\). Its characteristic polynomial is real of degree \(3\), and its complex eigenvalues have modulus \(1\) and product \(1\); the nonreal ones come in conjugate pairs, so \(1\) is an eigenvalue. Let \(a\) be a unit vector with \(Aa = a\). Then \(A\) preserves the plane \(a^\perp \) and acts on it by an element of \(\mathrm{SO}(2)\), a rotation by some angle \(\theta \). In an orthonormal basis \(a, b, a \times b\), the matrix \(L_a\) acts on \(a^\perp \) as \(J\) of Example 33.3.2(3) and kills \(a\), so \(e^{\theta L_a}\) fixes \(a\) and rotates \(a^\perp \) by \(\theta \); hence \(e^{\theta L_a} = A\).

02FC Exercise 33.6.6

Let \(N\) be the group of real upper triangular \(3 \times 3\) matrices with \(1\) on the diagonal (the Heisenberg group). Show that \(N\) is a closed subgroup of \(\mathrm{GL}_3(\mathbb {R})\) with Lie algebra \(\mathfrak {n}\) the strictly upper triangular matrices, compute the bracket on \(\mathfrak {n}\), and show that \(\exp \colon \mathfrak {n} \to N\) is a diffeomorphism.

Solution â–¼

\(N\) is defined by polynomial equations on the entries, so it is closed, and it is a subgroup. For \(X \in \mathfrak {n}\) we have \(X^3 = 0\), so \(e^X = I + X + X^2/2 \in N\); conversely, if \(e^{tX} \in N\) for all \(t\), differentiating at \(0\) shows \(X \in \mathfrak {n}\). So \(\mathfrak {n}\) is the Lie algebra (Theorem 33.4.1). With \(X\) having entries \(x_{12}, x_{13}, x_{23}\), the bracket \([X, Y]\) has the single entry \(x_{12}y_{23} - y_{12}x_{23}\) in position \((1, 3)\). The inverse of \(\exp \) is \(\log (I + M) = M - M^2/2\) for \(M\) strictly upper triangular, since \(M^3 = 0\); both maps are polynomial, hence smooth, and they are inverse to each other by the formal identities \(\log (\exp x) = x\) and \(\exp (\log (1 + x)) = 1 + x\).

02FD Exercise 33.6.7

Let \(G\) be a connected Lie group. Show that its centre is the kernel of \(\operatorname {Ad}\), and that its Lie algebra is the centre \(\{ X : [X, Y] = 0 \text{ for all } Y\} \) of \(\mathfrak {g}\).

Solution â–¼

By naturality of \(\exp \), \(c_g(\exp X) = \exp (\operatorname {Ad}(g)X)\). If \(\operatorname {Ad}(g) = \mathrm{id}\), then \(g\) commutes with \(\exp (\mathfrak {g})\), which generates \(G\) (Proposition 33.3.9), so \(g\) is central; conversely, if \(g\) is central then \(c_g = \mathrm{id}\) and \(\operatorname {Ad}(g) = \mathrm{id}\). By Theorem 33.4.1, the Lie algebra of the centre is \(\{ X : \exp (tX) \in Z(G) \text{ for all } t\} = \{ X : \operatorname {Ad}(\exp tX) = \mathrm{id}\text{ for all } t\} \). Since \(\operatorname {Ad}(\exp tX) = e^{t\, \operatorname {ad}X}\) (Proposition 33.3.6), this holds for all \(t\) if and only if \(\operatorname {ad}X = 0\), by differentiating at \(t = 0\).
