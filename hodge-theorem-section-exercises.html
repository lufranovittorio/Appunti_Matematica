01DR Exercise 41.4.1

Show that on a compact connected oriented Riemannian \(n\)-manifold, \(\mathcal{H}^0 = \mathbb {R}\) and \(\mathcal{H}^n = \mathbb {R}\, \mathrm{vol}_g\), and deduce \(b_0 = b_n = 1\).

Solution â–¼

A harmonic function has \(df = 0\), hence is constant; apply \(*\) for the top degree (Corollary 41.2.6).

01DS Exercise 41.4.2

Show that the Green operator is self-adjoint, that it is compact as an operator on \(L^2\), and that \(\alpha \mapsto H\alpha \) is the orthogonal projection onto \(\mathcal{H}^k\) with \(H = I - \Delta G\).

Solution â–¼

\(H = I - \Delta G\) is the identity \(\alpha = H\alpha + \Delta G\alpha \) of Theorem 41.2.2. For self-adjointness write \(\alpha = H\alpha + \Delta a\) and \(\beta = H\beta + \Delta b\) with \(a = G\alpha \), \(b = G\beta \) orthogonal to \(\mathcal{H}^k\). Then \((G\alpha , \beta ) = (a, H\beta + \Delta b) = (a, \Delta b)\), since \(a \perp \mathcal{H}^k\), and likewise \((\alpha , G\beta ) = (\Delta a, b)\); these are equal because \(\Delta \) is formally self-adjoint. For compactness, use an orthonormal basis of eigenforms \(u_k\) of \(\Delta \) (Theorem 40.5.5): \(G\) kills the \(u_k\) with eigenvalue \(0\) and maps \(u_k\) to \(\lambda _k^{-1}u_k\) otherwise. As \(\lambda _k \to \infty \), \(G\) is the norm limit of the finite rank operators obtained by truncating this diagonal operator, so it is compact (Proposition 29.5.2), and it extends from smooth forms to \(L^2\).

01DT Exercise 41.4.3

Give an example of a compact manifold and two metrics for which the harmonic representatives of a fixed cohomology class differ. Why can the two metrics not be conformal to each other when the manifold is a surface and the class has degree \(1\)?

Solution â–¼

On the torus \(T^2 = \mathbb {R}^2/\mathbb {Z}^2\) take the flat metric \(dx^2 + dy^2\), for which the harmonic representative of \([dx]\) is \(dx\), and the metric \(g^{\prime } = e^{2\phi (x)}dx^2 + dy^2\) with \(\phi \) smooth, \(1\)-periodic and non-constant. An oriented orthonormal coframe for \(g^{\prime }\) is \((e^\phi dx, dy)\), so its Hodge star maps \(e^\phi dx \mapsto dy\) and \(dy \mapsto -e^\phi dx\). The form \(\alpha = c\, e^{\phi (x)}dx\) with \(c = (\int _0^1e^\phi \, dx)^{-1}\) is closed (its coefficient depends only on \(x\)) and \(*^{\prime }\alpha = c\, dy\) is closed, so \(\alpha \) is \(g^{\prime }\)-harmonic; its integrals over the two circles \(y = 0\) and \(x = 0\) are \(1\) and \(0\), as for \(dx\), so it lies in the class \([dx]\) (on the torus a class in \(H^1\) is determined by these two integrals, Exercise 36.5.1). It differs from \(dx\) because \(\phi \) is not constant; equivalently, \(dx\) is not \(g^{\prime }\)-harmonic, since \(*^{\prime }dx = e^{-\phi }dy\) has \(d(e^{-\phi }dy) = -\phi ^{\prime }e^{-\phi }dx \wedge dy \neq 0\). For conformal metrics on a surface this cannot happen, by Proposition 41.2.8; and \(g^{\prime }\) is indeed not conformal to the flat metric, as \(dx\) and \(dy\) have different \(g^{\prime }\)-lengths where \(\phi \neq 0\), while they remain orthogonal.

01DU Exercise 41.4.4

Let \(M\) be a compact orientable surface of genus \(g\). Using Theorem 41.2.5 and Corollary 41.2.6, show that \(\dim \mathcal{H}^1 = 2g\) and that the intersection pairing on \(\mathcal{H}^1\) is a nondegenerate alternating form.

Solution â–¼

\(\dim \mathcal{H}^1 = b_1(M) = 2g\) by Theorem 41.2.5 and the computation of the Betti numbers of \(\Sigma _g\) (Example 20.7.5). The pairing \((\alpha , \beta ) \mapsto \int _M\alpha \wedge \beta \) is bilinear and alternating, since \(\alpha \wedge \beta = -\beta \wedge \alpha \) for \(1\)-forms, and it is nondegenerate on \(\mathcal{H}^1\): for \(\alpha \neq 0\) harmonic, \(*\alpha \) is harmonic (Corollary 41.2.6) and \(\int _M\alpha \wedge *\alpha = \| \alpha \| ^2 {\gt} 0\). (A nondegenerate alternating form forces the dimension to be even, in accordance with \(2g\).)

02OZ Exercise 41.4.5

Let \(V^0 = \mathbb {R}\), \(V^1 = \mathbb {R}^2\), \(V^2 = \mathbb {R}\) with the standard inner products, \(d \colon V^0 \to V^1\), \(t \mapsto (t, t)\), and \(d = 0 \colon V^1 \to V^2\). Compute the cohomology, the Laplacians and their kernels in each degree, and verify Lemma 41.2.1. Which element of the cohomology class of \((1, 0)\) has the smallest norm?

Solution â–¼

\(H^0 = \ker (t \mapsto (t, t)) = 0\), \(H^1 = \mathbb {R}^2/\mathbb {R}(1, 1) \cong \mathbb {R}\) and \(H^2 = V^2 = \mathbb {R}\). The adjoint of \(d \colon V^0 \to V^1\) is \(\delta (a, b) = a + b\), and the adjoint of the zero map is zero. So \(\Delta = \delta d = 2\) on \(V^0\), with kernel \(0\); on \(V^1\), \(\Delta (a, b) = d\delta (a, b) = (a + b, a + b)\), with kernel the line \(a + b = 0\); and \(\Delta = 0\) on \(V^2\), with kernel \(V^2\). The dimensions \(0\), \(1\), \(1\) of the kernels are those of the cohomology. The class of \((1, 0)\) is \((1, 0) + \mathbb {R}(1, 1)\), and \((1, 0) = (\frac12, -\frac12) + d(\frac12)\) with \((\frac12, -\frac12)\) harmonic: it is the element of the class of smallest norm, \(\frac{1}{\sqrt2} {\lt} 1\), the orthogonal projection of \((1, 0)\) to the line \(a + b = 0\).

02P0 Exercise 41.4.6

Verify the Bochner–Weitzenböck formula for \(\alpha = dz|_{S^2}\) on the round unit sphere \(S^2 \subseteq \mathbb {R}^3\). Use that \(\Delta z = 2z\) (Exercise 40.6.6), so \(\Delta \alpha = d\Delta z = 2\alpha \), and compute \(\nabla ^*\nabla \alpha \) from the Hessian \(\nabla dz = -z\, g\) of the restriction of a linear function to the sphere.

Solution â–¼

For a function \(f\), \(\nabla df\) is the Hessian \((X, Y) \mapsto (\nabla _Xdf)(Y) = X(Y(f)) - (\nabla _XY)(f)\). For the restriction of \(z\) and tangent fields \(X\), \(Y\) on \(S^2\), \(Y(z) = \langle Y, e_3\rangle \) because \(z\) is linear, so \(X(Y(z)) = \langle \overline\nabla _XY, e_3\rangle \), where \(\overline\nabla \) is the derivative in \(\mathbb {R}^3\). By the Gauss formula (Theorem 39.10.2), \(\overline\nabla _XY = \nabla _XY + \mathrm{II}(X, Y)\), and on the unit sphere the normal part is \(\mathrm{II}(X, Y) = \langle \overline\nabla _XY, x\rangle x = -\langle Y, \overline\nabla _Xx\rangle x = -\langle X, Y\rangle x\) at the point \(x\). Hence \((\nabla _Xdz)(Y) = \langle \overline\nabla _XY - \nabla _XY, e_3\rangle = -\langle X, Y\rangle \langle x, e_3\rangle = -\langle X, Y\rangle z\). Hence \(\nabla _{e_i}\alpha = -z\, e^i\) in an orthonormal frame, and \(\nabla ^*\nabla \alpha = -\sum _i\nabla _{e_i}(-z\, e^i)\), which at a point with \(\nabla e_i = 0\) is \(\sum _ie_i(z)\, e^i = dz = \alpha \). Since \(\mathrm{Ric} = g\) on the unit sphere, the formula reads \(\Delta \alpha = \alpha + \alpha = 2\alpha \), as it should.
